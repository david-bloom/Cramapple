## Course relationship and scope

AP Calculus AB is equivalent to a first-semester college calculus course. AP Calculus BC is equivalent to first- and second-semester college calculus. BC contains all AB content plus additional integration content, parametric/polar/vector-valued functions, and sequences and series.

AB contains Units 1-8. BC contains Units 1-10. Units 9 and 10 are BC-only. Within Unit 6, Topics 6.12 and 6.13 are also BC-only.

Do not infer AB eligibility from a shared unit number alone. Every AB item must be checked at topic level.

## Exam structure

Both exams are 3 hours 10 minutes, with 42 multiple-choice questions and 6 free-response questions.

Section I, Part A: 29 MCQs, no graphing calculator, 62 minutes, 35% of score.

Section I, Part B: 13 MCQs, graphing calculator required, 38 minutes, 15% of score.

Section II, Part A: 2 FRQs, graphing calculator required, 30 minutes, approximately 16.7% of score.

Section II, Part B: 4 FRQs, no graphing calculator, 60 minutes, approximately 33.3% of score.

The AB and BC exams share three FRQs drawn from the AB domain. Each exam includes multiple function types and analytical, graphical, numerical/tabular, and verbal representations. Each includes at least two real-world contexts.

Calculator-enabled work may require graphing, numerical zero solving, numerical differentiation, and numerical definite integration. On an FRQ, students must show the mathematical setup that produced a calculator result.

## Mathematical practices

Practice 1 - Implementing Mathematical Processes: determine expressions and values using mathematical procedures and rules.

Practice 2 - Connecting Representations: translate mathematical information within and across graphical, numerical, analytical, and verbal representations.

Practice 3 - Justification: select and apply definitions, theorems, and tests; support conclusions; confirm conditions and accuracy.

Practice 4 - Communication and Notation: use precise language, units, symbols, graphing conventions, and rounding.

MCQ practice weighting: Practice 1, 50-70%; Practice 2, 15-30%; Practice 3, 10-20%. Practice 4 is not assessed in the MCQ section.

FRQ practice weighting: Practice 1, 35-60%; Practice 2, 10-20%; Practice 3, 35-60%; Practice 4, 10-25%.

## Units 1-3 deep-tier detail (2026-08-08 addition)

Verified directly against the CED PDF (pages 27-76) plus the scoring/reader-report sources listed in Source control above. Exclusion density here is genuinely sparser than Biology/Chemistry — Calc AB/BC's scope-limiting signal comes mostly from illustrative examples and scoring-guideline precision rules, not boxed "do not assess" statements, except where noted. Do not infer a missing exclusion tag means "anything goes" — check the illustrative examples and common-error notes below, which function as the real boundary.

### Unit 1 — Limits and Continuity (AB 10-15%, BC 5-10%)

*Exclusion:* The epsilon-delta definition of a limit is **not assessed** on the AP Calculus AB or BC Exam (attached to EK LIM-1.A.1, topic 1.2). Teachers may cover it in class, but do not author an item requiring a student to produce or reason from an epsilon-delta proof.

*Scope boundary via illustrative examples:* "Limit does not exist" cases (topic 1.3, EK LIM-1.C.4) are scoped to three canonical patterns — unbounded behavior (`lim(x→0) 1/x² = ∞`), oscillation (`lim(x→0) sin(1/x)` DNE), and mismatched one-sided limits (`lim(x→0) |x|/x` DNE). Stay within these patterns; do not invent exotic DNE cases the CED doesn't illustrate.

*High-yield content requirement (topic 1.16, IVT):* An item is not "IVT-complete" unless the response explicitly (a) states the function is continuous — and *why* (e.g., "differentiable, therefore continuous," not just asserted), (b) states the target value is strictly between the two endpoint values with the actual numbers, and (c) concludes existence. Per the 2025 Chief Reader Report, this was the single lowest-scoring point pattern in the entire AB/BC exam (mean ≈0.27-0.28/1 on the continuity-justification point; ≈0.28-0.43 on the conclusion point) — the failure mode is *not* mathematical, it's stating the conclusion without the two hypothesis-checks. A rubric that awards credit for "the right final answer" without requiring both checks does not match real AP grading and will silently pass a response real graders would dock.

*Documented misconception, direct from the Chief Reader Report:* "A few responses incorrectly attempted to apply the Mean Value Theorem" where IVT was required. MVT-vs-IVT confusion is real, reader-documented, and appropriate as an MCQ distractor.

*Documented misconception (end behavior / limits at infinity, topic 1.15):* Students confuse "end behavior" (limit as the variable approaches infinity) with evaluating near zero — the Chief Reader Report's most common error on the relevant 2025 FRQ part was writing `lim(t→0)` instead of `lim(t→∞)`. Also appropriate as an MCQ distractor.

### Unit 2 — Differentiation: Definition and Fundamental Properties (AB 10-15%, BC 5-10%)

No boxed exclusion statement for this unit's topics. The real boundary is notational/structural, and it is scored, not stylistic:

*Structural requirement (product/quotient rule, topics 2.8-2.9):* the CED's own "Preparing for the AP Exam" note states plainly: "Failure to present this structure will cost students the point they might have earned, even with a correct numerical answer." For `f(x) = u(x)·v(x)`, a response must show `f′(3) = u(3)v′(3) + v(3)u′(3)` as an explicit structural step, separate from the final numeric value — these are scored as two separate point-earning line items in real AP scoring guidelines (setup point, value point), not one all-or-nothing check.

*Documented misconception (chain rule on exponentials, boundary with Unit 3 but rooted in Unit 2 derivative rules):* per the Chief Reader Report, students frequently differentiate `e^u` as `u·e^u` instead of `e^u·u′` — i.e. they apply a garbled product-rule-shaped pattern to what should be a direct chain-rule application of a known derivative rule. Confirmed, quotable, appropriate as an MCQ distractor for any `d/dx[e^(g(x))]` item.

*Documented misconception (notation, topic 2.2/2.8):* renaming a derivative expression to new notation without defining it (e.g. silently calling `x_H′(t)` as `H′(t)`), and omitting parentheses around a binomial factor before a trig/exponential term (`2t − 4·e^(...)` instead of `(2t−4)·e^(...)`) — both documented as causing real point loss even when the underlying calculus was otherwise correct.

*Calculator-answer convention:* when a calculator-permitted item requires a numeric derivative value, the CED's own guidance requires "presenting mathematical expressions evaluated on the calculator" with results "rounded or truncated to three places after the decimal point" — and per the 2025/2026 Scoring Guidelines' universal front-matter note, **at most one point per entire FRQ (all parts combined) may be lost to inappropriate rounding** — never more, regardless of how many sub-answers are mis-rounded. Rubrics for calculator-permitted items should reflect this cap, not deduct per-instance.

### Unit 3 — Differentiation: Composite, Implicit, and Inverse Functions (AB 5-10%, BC 5-10%)

No boxed exclusion statement for this unit's topics either. This is the highest-error-density unit in the whole Units-1-3 range per the Chief Reader Report (AB6, the flagship implicit-differentiation FRQ, had a mean score of 4.12/9) — the boundary here is almost entirely about which near-miss responses do *not* earn credit despite reaching a materially correct answer:

*Documented misconception (chain rule mechanics, topic 3.1):* "Misapplying the chain rule by forgetting to also differentiate the inner function, or misidentifying the inner function" (CED's own Building Math Practices note) — e.g. failing to recognize the chain rule applies to `sin²x`, `tan(2x−1)`, or `e^(x²)`, or (in an implicit-differentiation context) not recognizing that `y` itself requires the chain rule because it depends on `x`.

*Documented misconception (implicit differentiation, topic 3.2) — five specific, reader-quoted errors, all appropriate as FRQ near-miss rubric language or MCQ distractors:*
1. Using `dy` where `dy/dx` is meant, or `dy/dx` where the operator `d/dx` is meant (conflating the differential with the derivative).
2. Dropping the "= 0" on the right-hand side after implicitly differentiating a constant (silently losing a term).
3. Omitting parentheses around the coefficient of `dy/dx` after collecting terms — e.g. writing `3y² − 2y − 1 · dy/dx = −½x` instead of `(3y² − 2y − 1)·dy/dx = −½x`, which changes the meaning.
4. Using separation of variables instead of implicit differentiation — a wrong-technique-choice error, not an arithmetic slip.
5. **Vertical-vs-horizontal tangent inversion**: setting the *numerator* of `dy/dx` to zero (the horizontal-tangent condition) when the problem requires the *denominator* to be zero (the vertical-tangent condition). Chief Reader Report calls this out explicitly and it is a strong, real, quotable MCQ distractor.

*Documented misconception (related rates via implicit differentiation w.r.t. t, still topic 3.2):* differentiating with respect to `x` and never reconnecting the result to `t` (forgetting the problem requires `d/dt`, introducing `dx/dt`/`dy/dt` terms); writing the derivative of `2xy` as `2(dx/dt)(dy/dt)` instead of correctly applying the product rule (`2(x·dy/dt + y·dx/dt)`); writing `d/dt(ln y)` as `1/y` instead of `(1/y)(dy/dt)` (dropping the required chain-rule factor). The Scoring Guidelines explicitly reject the shortcut "stating `dy/dt = dy/dx · dx/dt` alone, with no completed numeric computation" as earning zero points — citing the identity is not sufficient, it must be carried through.

*Selection-of-root requirement (topic 3.2, vertical tangent):* per the Scoring Guidelines' own scoring notes, finding both roots of a resulting quadratic is not sufficient for credit — the response must explicitly state which root satisfies the problem's domain restriction (e.g. "the curve requires y > 0, so y = 1" — merely listing "y = −1/3 and y = 1" without that selection step does not earn the point).

### Cross-unit scoring conventions confirmed from the 2025/2026 Scoring Guidelines (apply when authoring FRQ rubrics for any Units 1-3 item)

- AP Calc FRQ scoring decomposes into three separable point types wherever a multi-step calculation is involved: (1) a *setup/method-identification* point (e.g. "states Product Rule," "sets up the ratio," "writes the implicit-differentiation equation"), (2) an *execution* point (correct value from correctly executing the method), and (3) sometimes a separate *supported-conclusion* point. A response can earn the setup point with an arithmetic slip in execution, and vice versa — do not write single all-or-nothing criteria for what real AP scoring treats as 2-3 independent points, except where the guidelines are explicit that a step is all-or-nothing (implicit differentiation of the *whole* equation, topic 3.2, is scored as all-or-nothing for that one point — no partial credit for a partially correct differentiation).
- "Answers need not be simplified" — unsimplified but correct expressions earn full credit; do not write a criterion requiring a specific simplified form unless the prompt explicitly asks the student to simplify.
- Consistent/follow-through credit is real: if an earlier part's numeric answer is wrong, a later part that correctly *applies* that wrong number can still earn its own points (except for points explicitly gated on the correct earlier value).
- "Incorrect or unclear communication accompanying an otherwise correct step is treated as scratch work and is not scored" — a sign error in a linking sentence around an otherwise-correct computation does not cost the computation's point.

### Unit 4 — Contextual Applications of Differentiation (AB 10-15%, BC 5-10%)

*Exclusion, boxed and verbatim (topic 4.7, L'Hospital's Rule):* "There are many other indeterminate forms, such as ∞ − ∞, for example, but these will not be assessed on either the AP Calculus AB or BC Exam." Only two indeterminate forms are in scope: 0/0 and ∞/∞. Do not author an item requiring ∞−∞, 0·∞, 1^∞, 0^0, or ∞^0 reasoning.

*Notation precision requirement, stated directly in the CED's own unit guidance:* "it is incorrect to write lim f(x)/g(x) = 0/0" — 0/0 and ∞/∞ are labels for a situation, not values in an equation. An item's model solution/rationale should never write an equals sign into an indeterminate form.

*Related rates — real scoring architecture confirmed from 2025 Q6 Part D (curve `2xy + ln y = 8`, related rates via implicit differentiation w.r.t. t):* the setup point tolerates **at most one error** in the implicit-differentiation-w.r.t.-t step (a real, generous, documented tolerance — do not write a rubric requiring perfection on this step). The execution point requires both the product-rule term (each factor of `2xy` gets its own `d/dt` term) and the chain-rule term (`(1/y)(dy/dt)` on `ln y`) fully present. The Scoring Guidelines explicitly reject a shortcut: "stating dy/dt = dy/dx · dx/dt alone does not earn any points" — the chain-rule identity must be carried through to a completed computation with values, not just cited. Two full alternate solution paths are pre-blessed: differentiate w.r.t. t directly, OR differentiate w.r.t. x to get dy/dx, evaluate at the point, then multiply by dx/dt (valid only if that final multiplication is actually executed).

*Cross-check against real released items:* neither the 2025 nor 2026 released AB FRQ set contains a classic geometric related-rates word problem (ladder/shadow/cone-filling) — both years use "differentiate an implicit curve equation w.r.t. t" as the related-rates vehicle instead. Don't assume classic geometric setups are the dominant pattern in recent authoring; the implicit-curve-plus-given-rate form is now more representative of what's actually tested.

*Linearization — real scoring architecture confirmed from 2025 Q6 Part B:* the slope-value point and the tangent-line-approximation-mechanics point are scored **independently, with follow-through**. A response with a wrong slope k ≠ correct value does not earn the slope point, but the linearization-mechanics point can still be earned using that wrong k consistently (e.g. `−1 + k(−0.4)`) — this is a real, documented AP pattern: a process point survives an earlier wrong numeric value as long as it's applied consistently. An unresolved dangling expression (missing a closing parenthesis, no boxed final value) does **not** bank the point — it must resolve to a concrete number.

*L'Hospital's Rule — genuine coverage gap in the released-item signal:* neither the 2025 nor 2026 AB FRQ set tests L'Hospital's Rule directly (the one item that might look like it, 2026 Q6A, resolves by direct substitution since the function isn't indeterminate there). Do not treat this as evidence L'Hospital is untested — it's AB-eligible content per the CED, just not sampled in these two years' free-response sets. MCQ-level grounding would be needed for stronger signal.

### Unit 5 — Analytical Applications of Differentiation (AB 15-20%, BC 10-15%)

No boxed exclusion statements for any of this unit's 12 topics. **This is the single highest-value unit in the whole fact pack for scoring-architecture precision** — the CED's own unit-level guidance states the theme directly: "Students are expected to refer to f, f′, and f″ by name, rather than by 'it' or 'the function,' which may leave the reader unsure of their intended meaning," and warns against imprecise justification like "the graph of f is increasing because it's going up."

*The Candidates Test vs. local tests — the defining scoring split of this unit, confirmed across multiple real 2025 FRQ parts (Q1D, Q4D):* the CED names three distinct tools for extrema and does not subordinate one to another in its required content: the **Candidates Test** (topic 5.5, absolute/global extrema on a closed interval — evaluate at every critical point AND both endpoints) is structurally separate from the **First Derivative Test** (5.4) and **Second Derivative Test** (5.7), both used for relative/local extrema. Real scoring behavior is stark and consistent: **a response using a local argument (First or Second Derivative Test) to justify an *absolute* extremum does not earn the justification point, even when the final numeric answer is correct** — but that same response *remains eligible* for the separate final-answer point. This "local test blocks the justification point but never blocks the answer point" split repeats identically across every candidates-test item checked. Do not write a bundled criterion that awards or withholds credit as a single unit for "find and justify the absolute extremum" — real AP scoring treats identifying-the-value and justifying-it-globally as separable points.

*What counts as "considering [derivative] = 0" — a documented near-miss pattern:* the setup point for finding critical points is explicitly **not earned merely by writing down the resulting x-value**. The Scoring Guidelines state this directly: a response is not credited for "just presenting" a critical value without showing the equation-setting step, a sign-change discussion, or explicit critical-point language. When authoring a "find and justify" FRQ criterion, require the work step, not just the correct value.

*Alternate accepted global-justification forms (all equivalent, all confirmed credit-earning):* an explicit endpoint-vs-critical-point value table; a sign-change statement ("A′(t) > 0 for 4 < t < c, and A′(t) < 0 for c < t < b, therefore c is the location of the absolute maximum"); or a two-step relative-then-absolute argument ("the sign change makes t=c a relative maximum, and because it's the only critical point on the interval, it is also the absolute maximum" — this second form directly uses EK FUN-4.A.8, the single-critical-point-promotes-to-absolute-extremum shortcut fact).

*Real documented student error (from the Q1 sample-response commentary):* a response found the correct critical point and the correct final numeric answer but used only a local argument (first-derivative sign change with no endpoint comparison) — the commentary states plainly the justification point was not earned "because a local argument is given," despite the answer being numerically correct throughout.

### Unit 6 — Integration and Accumulation of Change (AB 15-20%, BC 15-20%)

No boxed exclusion statements for any of the 9 AB-eligible topics (6.1-6.10, 6.14). Three techniques in this unit are explicitly BC-only, each tagged in three places (title, learning objective, EK) exactly like Unit 4's L'Hospital exclusion pattern: **6.11 Integration by Parts**, **6.12 Integration by Linear, Nonrepeating Partial Fractions** (the CED's own scope language explicitly restricts this to "linear, nonrepeating factors" — repeated linear factors and irreducible quadratics are out of scope even for BC), and **6.13 Improper Integrals**. Do not author any of these three for `apcalcab-*`.

*Riemann-sum-to-definite-integral, the exact required structure (topic 6.3, quoted verbatim):* "∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx = lim(max Δxᵢ→0) Σᵢ f(xᵢ*)Δxᵢ." This is the complete CED-required form — no additional formal-proof requirement exists beyond this statement and its converse (a definite integral can be written as this limit, and vice versa).

*Over/under-estimate reasoning for Riemann sums (topic 6.2) — genuine CED gap, worth flagging for authoring caution:* the CED states only the general fact that it "may be possible to determine whether an approximation is an underestimate or overestimate" — it does **not** codify the specific left/right/midpoint/trapezoidal + monotonicity/concavity rules (e.g. "a right sum overestimates an increasing function") anywhere in its required-content text. That granularity is pedagogical convention, not CED-mandated content. Authoring against this topic should still use the standard rules (they're universally taught and tested), but don't cite the CED as the source for the specific monotonicity/concavity mapping — it isn't there.

*Trapezoidal-sum scoring — real, unusually granular threshold confirmed from 2025 Q3 Part C:* a trapezoidal sum with 3 subintervals has 6 factors (3 terms × 2 factors each); the Scoring Guidelines state explicitly that **at least 5 of 6 factors must be correct** for the setup point, and any one of the 6 being wrong blocks the answer point entirely. A fully correct **left or right** Riemann sum (as opposed to the requested trapezoidal sum) earns the setup point but never the answer point, even though it's a complete, internally consistent computation — substituting the wrong summation method is treated as a real defect, not a stylistic variant.

*Accumulation-function FTC scoring — confirmed from 2025 Q4:* `g′(x) = f(x)` (FTC) must appear as an explicit general statement to earn the "states FTC" point; simply writing the final numeric answer with no visible FTC statement can still earn the separate answer point (implied-application credit) but not the FTC-statement point. A specific, real documented error: writing `g′(8) = f(8) − f(6)` (confusing the derivative of an accumulation function with a difference quotient) earns the answer point if numerically consistent but explicitly **not** the FTC-statement point — a genuine, quotable near-miss for MCQ distractor or FRQ rubric design.

*Points-of-inflection-from-a-graph-of-f′ scoring (2025 Q4 Part B) — an unusually strict all-or-nothing threshold worth encoding:* the value-identification point is earned **only** for the exact, complete set of x-values — stating any additional or incorrect value blocks credit entirely (no partial credit for 2 of 3 correct plus one wrong extra value). A separate reason-giving point requires language tied to the given graph of **f′** specifically ("f′ changes from increasing to decreasing," "f′ has a relative extremum there") — language that instead restates the definition in terms of g ("g changes concavity there") earns only the value point, not the reasoning point, because it doesn't connect back to the graph actually given.

### Unit 7 — Differential Equations (AB 5-10%, BC 5-10%)

No boxed exclusion statements for any AB-eligible topic. Two topics are BC-only, each tagged in three places: **7.5 Euler's Method** and **7.9 Logistic Models**. The CED gives no explicit numeric formula or step-size notation for Euler's Method anywhere in its required content — the only framing is conceptual ("Euler's method provides a procedure for approximating a solution... by transferring skills using tangent line approximations, rather than simply memorizing an algorithm"). Do not assume a specific step-size/notation convention is CED-mandated; teach and test it as repeated tangent-line approximation.

*Exponential model — confirmed shared AB/BC (no BC-only tag anywhere on topic 7.8), with the exact required form:* `dy/dt = ky`, solution `y = y₀e^(kt)`. The CED explicitly permits the model to be "used or interpreted without performing the derivation" — an item may give the solution form directly and ask for interpretation/application, without requiring the student to derive it from the differential equation.

*Separation-of-variables scoring discipline (unit-level guidance, quoted verbatim):* "Failure to separate variables or omitting the constant of integration severely limits the number of points a student can earn." Also flagged directly: "A common error in antidifferentiation is to assume that all differential equations involving fractions have logarithmic solutions, presumably because some do" — a real, CED-documented misconception, appropriate as an MCQ distractor for any separable-DE item whose antiderivative is *not* logarithmic.

*Domain restrictions on particular solutions (topic 7.7, EK FUN-7.E.3):* "Solutions to differential equations may be subject to domain restrictions" — a real scoring nuance (a particular solution found via separation of variables may only be valid on part of its algebraic domain; this is a genuine AP point-earning consideration, not a stylistic add-on).

*Coverage gap, real and worth flagging directly:* neither the 2025 nor 2026 released AB FRQ set (as of this fact-pack update) contains a slope-field, Euler's-method, or separation-of-variables item with an official scoring guide available — 2026 Q3 is a full differential-equations FRQ but has no released scoring guideline yet. Unit 7's scoring-architecture signal here is inferred by analogy to structurally identical part-types elsewhere in the 2025 exam (the same setup/execution/answer point-ladder pattern used throughout), not independently confirmed for this unit specifically. ### Unit 8 — Applications of Integration (AB 10-15%, BC 5-10%)

*Exclusion, boxed and verbatim (topic 8.13, Arc Length):* BC-only, tagged three times (title, Learning Objective CHA-6.A, EK CHA-6.A.1), using its own dedicated Enduring Understanding (CHA-6) separate from the rest of the unit's area/volume group (CHA-5). **This corrects the prior version of this fact pack, which incorrectly listed arc length as shared AB/BC** (see Source control note above). Do not author or approve an `apcalcab-*` item on arc length. No live corpus defect from this was found when checked.

No other topic in this unit (8.1-8.12) carries a boxed exclusion.

*Average value vs. average rate of change — a real, CED-documented, and released-exam-confirmed confusion:* average value is `(1/(b−a))∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx` (topic 8.1); average rate of change is `(f(b)−f(a))/(b−a)` (a Unit 2/4 concept). The CED's own unit-level guidance calls this out directly: "Some students confuse the average value and the average rate of change of a function on an interval." **Real documented student error, from the 2025 Q1 sample-response commentary**: a response computed `(C(4)−C(0))/(4−0)` (average rate of change) when the item asked for average value — scored 0/2 on that part, with the commentary stating explicitly the response "computes the average rate of change... instead of the average value." This is the strongest, most concrete distractor material found in this entire research pass — use it directly for MCQ distractor design on any average-value item.

*Volume setup scoring — confirmed the exact credit-split the CED's own guidance describes ("focusing on orientation... will help students determine whether the 'thickness' is with respect to x or y"):* real 2025 scoring data (Q2 Parts B and C) confirms the setup/radius-or-side-length choice is scored **purely on structural form**, independent of final-answer correctness: a cross-section integrand earns its point for being "a product of two nonconstant factors, with one factor equal to x (or the height function) and the other equal to the correct base-length expression" — regardless of whether the expression is later evaluated correctly. For washer-method items, the radius-squared-difference **form** (`R² − r²` or `|R² − r²|`) earns its own point separately from the numerically correct radii, which is separate again from the limits/constant-π/differential point. A response that reverses the difference of squares (computes `r²−R²` instead) is **self-correcting and still earns full credit** if the sign is resolved either by a leading negative sign or by flipped integration limits, plus the differential present — but the same reversal left unresolved loses exactly the limits/constant point, not the whole part. **Omitting the constant π specifically costs exactly one point (the limits/constant point), not the setup or integrand points** — do not write a rubric that treats a missing π as disqualifying the whole volume computation.

*Real documented student error on volume setup (2025 Q2 sample-response commentary):* a response wrote the washer integrand without the required constant translation (revolving about a non-axis line, e.g. `y=−2`, requires each radius to include that shift) — commentary states plainly this "does not earn [the form point] because the integrand is not of the form R²−r²" (i.e. omitting the required constant shift breaks the recognized pattern entirely, not just the final value) — 0/3 on that part despite using the correct general disk/washer skeleton otherwise.

*Antiderivative-constant gatekeeping (2025 Q5 Part D, motion-via-integrals):* a documented, unusually strict rule — "if the antiderivative's leading constant k ≠ [the correct value], the response is not eligible to earn [the final-answer point]," even if all subsequent arithmetic is otherwise clean. A wrong constant multiplier on an otherwise-correctly-formed antiderivative disqualifies the answer point outright, distinct from Unit 4/5's more forgiving "wrong value, correct process, still eligible" pattern — do not assume all near-miss numeric errors get the same follow-through leniency; this one specifically does not.

### Cross-unit scoring conventions confirmed from the 2025 AB Scoring Guidelines (apply across Units 4-8, extending the Units 1-3 list above)

- The **local-vs-global justification split from Unit 5** (a correct-but-local argument earns the answer point but not the justification point) recurs by the same logic anywhere an FRQ asks "justify that this is a maximum/minimum" — including accumulation-function extrema (Unit 6/8 crossover, e.g. 2025 Q4 Part D).
- **Cross-part consistency/follow-through credit is explicit and named in the guidelines**, not just a general convention: values "imported from part C" or "restated from part B" are explicitly permitted to satisfy a later part's evaluation requirement, including when the imported value is itself wrong — confirmed across three separate 2025 items (Q1D, Q4D, Q5C). When authoring multi-part FRQs, a later part's criterion should allow a numerically-consistent-with-an-earlier-wrong-value answer to still earn credit, matching real AP practice.
- **"Incorrect or unclear communication between a correct setup and a correct final answer is treated as scratch work and is not scored"** — confirmed verbatim across multiple 2025 questions' scoring notes. A sign error or transcription slip strictly *between* an already-credited setup step and an already-credited answer does not cost either point.
- **The differential (`dx`, `dt`) is sometimes fully optional and sometimes load-bearing — it depends on the specific point being scored, not a blanket rule.** Confirmed both ways in real 2025 scoring: Unit 8's cross-section volume setup explicitly does not require the differential for its structural-form point ("the presence or absence of dx will not be considered"), while Unit 4/8's related-rates and reversed-difference-of-squares cases specifically require the differential present to resolve an otherwise-ambiguous expression into full credit. Do not write a universal "always require dx" or "never require dx" rule into a rubric — check the specific point being scored.

### Unit 9 — Parametric, Polar, and Vector-Valued Functions (BC only, 10-15%)

*Topics 9.1-9.2 (parametric derivatives) — the CED does supply explicit formulas here, in contrast with most of the rest of this unit:* EK CHA-3.G.2 gives $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}$, "provided $\dfrac{dx}{dt}$ does not equal zero." EK CHA-3.G.3 gives $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \dfrac{\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)}{\dfrac{dx}{dt}}$. Zero explicit exclusion statements for either topic. The CED's own unit-level guidance flags the natural failure point directly: "Since $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ is in terms of $t$, students must be particularly careful when determining $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}$" — i.e., $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} \neq \dfrac{d}{dx}\left(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right)$ applied naively; it requires a second division by $\dfrac{dx}{dt}$.

*Topic 9.3 (arc length of parametric curves) — a genuine CED formula gap:* EK CHA-6.B.1 states only "The length of a parametrically defined curve can be calculated using a definite integral." The CED never writes out $\int \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt$ anywhere in its required-content text for this topic — unlike 9.1/9.2, which do give explicit derivative formulas. Do not treat a specific integrand notation as CED-mandated beyond "a definite integral computes curve length." Zero explicit exclusion statement.

*Topics 9.4-9.6 (vector-valued functions) — conceptual "extension" EKs, no componentwise formulas given:* CHA-3.H.1 ("Methods for calculating derivatives of real-valued functions can be extended to vector-valued functions"), FUN-8.A.1 (same extension language for integration), and FUN-8.B.1 (same extension language for velocity/speed/acceleration) are all stated at this level of generality — the CED never writes $\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle x'(t), y'(t)\rangle$ or an equivalent componentwise form anywhere in Unit 9's required content. FUN-8.B.2 is the one topic in this cluster with real, quotable, specific content, quoted verbatim: "For a particle in planar motion over an interval of time, the definite integral of the velocity vector represents the particle's displacement (net change in position) over the interval of time, from which we might determine its position. The definite integral of speed represents the particle's total distance traveled over the interval of time." Zero explicit exclusion statements across 9.4-9.6.

*Topic 9.7 (polar coordinates and differentiation) — notation convention confirmed, and a real documented gap:* EK FUN-3.G.2 quoted verbatim: "For a curve given by a polar equation $r = f(\theta)$, derivatives of $r$, $x$, and $y$ with respect to $\theta$, and first and second derivatives of $y$ with respect to $x$ can provide information about the curve." **The CED uses lowercase $r$ as a function of $\theta$ — $r(\theta)$ or $r=f(\theta)$ — never $\rho$.** Use this convention in all authored polar items. Notably, the CED never writes the rectangular-polar conversion formulas $x = r(\theta)\cos\theta$, $y = r(\theta)\sin\theta$ anywhere in its required-content text for this topic, and this is not merely a documentation gap — the 2025 Chief Reader Report confirms real students don't reliably know it either: for the 2025 BC2 polar FRQ Part C, the report states plainly that "many either did not know that $x(\theta) = r(\theta)\cos\theta$ or were unsuccessful in applying it." This is a strong, real, quotable candidate for MCQ distractor design on any polar-to-rectangular item. Zero explicit exclusion statement for 9.7 itself.

*Topics 9.8-9.9 (polar area) — another conceptual-only EK pair, but with rich, granular real scoring data:* EK CHA-5.D.1/D.2 state only that rectangular area concepts "can be extended to polar coordinates" and that polar-region areas "can be calculated with definite integrals" — the CED never writes $\frac{1}{2}\int_\alpha^\beta [r(\theta)]^2\,d\theta$ anywhere in its required content. Real 2025 scoring data (BC2 Part B, the polar-area-between-two-curves FRQ) confirms a granular three-way point split for this integral setup: a point for an integrand that includes $(r(\theta))^2$; a separate point for the full correct integrand (e.g., $(r(\theta))^2 - \left(\frac12\right)^2$ as a difference of squares); and the constant $\frac12$, the correct limits of integration, and the final numeric answer are all assessed together in a third, separate point — the Scoring Guidelines state this split explicitly: "The limits $\theta_1 = \frac{\pi}{6}$... and $\theta_2 = \frac{5\pi}{6}$... and the factor $\frac12$ are assessed in P4, not in P2 or P3." Do not write a bundled all-or-nothing criterion for a polar-area setup; real AP scoring separates "includes the squared radius term," "full correct integrand," and "constant/limits/final value" into three independent points.

**Documented misconception, real graded student sample (2025 BC2 Sample 2C, scored 2/9):** a response wrote the integrand as $(r(\theta))^2 - \frac12$ instead of $(r(\theta))^2 - \left(\frac12\right)^2$ — forgetting to square the constant radius term of the semicircle being subtracted. The scoring commentary states this explicitly: the response "did not earn P3 because the presented integrand is incorrect as $\frac12$ is not squared." Real, quotable, appropriate as an MCQ distractor or a near-miss FRQ rubric line for any polar-area-between-two-curves item.

**Real Chief Reader Report data, BC2 overall (polar FRQ, mean 3.09/9 across all parts):** the final-answer point for the area computation (P4: correct numeric area, requiring the constant, correct limits, and correct evaluation together) had a mean of only **0.16/1**. Far more strikingly, the global-justification point for the farthest-point-from-the-$y$-axis candidates-test part (P6) had a mean of only **0.06/1** — the single lowest point mean documented anywhere in this fact pack's research to date, across both the AB and BC passes (for comparison, Unit 1's IVT justification point, previously the pack's lowest documented mean, was ≈0.27-0.28/1). Polar-optimization items combining an area setup with a global candidates-test justification are a genuinely hard, real, high-value target for authoring and review calibration.

**Documented misconception, CR report verbatim, BC2 Part C (candidates-test global argument for farthest point from the $y$-axis):** the most common reason for not earning the justification point was "providing a local argument or an insufficient or incorrect global argument" — e.g., stating only "$\frac{dx}{d\theta}$ changes from positive to negative at $\theta = 0.955$" (a local, sign-change argument) without the required endpoint comparison. This is the same local-vs-global justification pattern already documented for Unit 5's Candidates Test in the Units 1-3 pass, now confirmed to recur identically in Unit 9's polar-optimization context — a genuinely general AP Calc BC scoring pattern, not a Unit-5-specific one.

**Documented misconception, CR report verbatim, BC2 Part D (chain-rule rate-of-change-of-distance-from-origin item):** "Some responses did not earn P8 because of multiplying 15 by the wrong derivative such as $\dfrac{dx}{dt}$, $\dfrac{dr}{dt}$, or $\dfrac{d\theta}{dr}$" — i.e., chain-rule-multiplying the given $\frac{d\theta}{dt}=15$ by the wrong intermediate derivative instead of $\frac{dr}{d\theta}$. Real, quotable, appropriate MCQ distractor for any polar-motion chain-rule item.

**Coverage gap, real and worth flagging directly:** no FRQ in either the 2025 or 2026 released BC set assesses parametric-equations (9.1-9.3) or vector-valued-functions (9.4-9.6) content — both years' BC-only FRQ (Question 2) is a polar-curve item covering 9.7-9.9 instead. Only the polar sub-topics (9.7-9.9) have real FRQ-level scoring-architecture confirmation in this pass; the parametric/vector-valued scoring-architecture guidance above is inferred by analogy to structurally identical point-ladder patterns confirmed elsewhere in this fact pack (e.g., Unit 4's related-rates chain-rule tolerance), not independently confirmed against a real BC FRQ. Treat Topics 9.1-9.6 as lower-confidence than 9.7-9.9 for this reason, in the same spirit as the Unit 7 flag in the Units 4-8 pass.

### Unit 10 — Infinite Sequences and Series (BC only, 15-20%)

*Topics 10.1-10.7 (series/partial-sum definitions, geometric series, nth term test, integral test, harmonic/p-series, comparison tests, alternating series test) — mostly conceptual/naming EKs:* Zero explicit exclusion statements across any of these seven topics. Topic 10.2 is the one topic in this cluster with an explicit CED formula, quoted verbatim: "If $a$ is a real number and $r$ is a real number such that $|r| < 1$, then the geometric series $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} ar^n = \dfrac{a}{1-r}$." By contrast, Topics 10.3, 10.4, 10.6, and 10.7 state only that their named test "is a method to determine whether a series converges or diverges" — the CED never writes out the nth-term-test condition, the integral-test hypotheses, the comparison-test inequality structure, or the alternating-series-test hypotheses as explicit statements anywhere in its required content; these are assumed background knowledge, not CED-supplied formulas. Topic 10.5 is quoted verbatim: "In addition to geometric series, common series of numbers include the harmonic series, the alternating harmonic series, and $p$-series" — again naming, not defining, these series symbolically.

*Topic 10.8 (ratio test) — the one genuine boxed exclusion in Units 9-10, quoted verbatim in full:* "The $n$th term test for divergence, and the integral test, comparison test, limit comparison test, alternating series test, and ratio test for convergence are assessed on the AP Calculus BC Exam. Other methods are not assessed on the exam. However, teachers may include additional methods in the course, if time permits." This closes the entire in-scope convergence-test list to exactly six named tests. Do not author an item requiring the root test, condensation (Cauchy) test, Raabe's test, Dirichlet's test, or any other convergence test outside this list — this was not previously captured anywhere in this fact pack.

*Topic 10.9 (absolute/conditional convergence):* EK LIM-7.A.13/A.14 quoted verbatim: "If a series converges absolutely, then it converges" and "If a series converges absolutely, then any series obtained from it by regrouping or rearranging the terms has the same value." Note the CED explicitly scopes the rearrangement-invariance fact to absolutely convergent series — conditionally convergent series famously do not share this property, and an item should not imply otherwise. **Documented misconception, CR report verbatim, BC6 Part A:** "Many responses attempted to use an alternating series in a comparison test, which can only be used when the series being compared have positive terms" — real, quotable, appropriate distractor for any comparison-test item involving a series with mixed-sign terms. Also documented: "Many students claimed $\displaystyle\sum \frac{3}{n+1}$ is the harmonic series" when it is properly analyzed as divergent by limit comparison to (not literal identity with) the harmonic series — a real, subtle naming-precision misconception, good MCQ-distractor material.

*Topic 10.10 (alternating series error bound):* EK LIM-7.B.1 states the concept only ("...the alternating series error bound can be used to bound how far a partial sum is from the value of the infinite series") without writing the standard inequality (error ≤ magnitude of first omitted term) as an explicit formula in the CED text. Zero explicit exclusion statement.

*Topics 10.11-10.12 (Taylor polynomial approximation, Lagrange error bound):* EK LIM-8.A.1 gives the one fully explicit formula in this cluster, quoted verbatim: "The coefficient of the $n$th degree term in a Taylor polynomial for a function $f$ centered at $x=a$ is $\dfrac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}$." By contrast, the Lagrange error bound itself (Topic 10.12) is stated only conceptually in the CED ("...can be used to determine a maximum interval for the error of a Taylor polynomial approximation to a function") with no explicit max-derivative/$(n+1)!$ formula in the required-content text — but the 2025 Scoring Guidelines supply the real, expected applied form directly: for a response bounding $|f(1.1)-P_2(1.1)|$, the accepted form is $\dfrac{\max|f'''(x)|}{3!}|1.1-1|^3 \le \dfrac{60}{6}(0.1)^3 = 0.01$. Use this Scoring-Guidelines form as the CED-adjacent authoring standard for Lagrange error bound items, since the CED itself does not supply one.

**Real scoring architecture, 2025 BC5 Part C (Lagrange error bound):** the point for presenting the correct *form* of the error bound is scored separately from the point for correctly *connecting* that computed bound to the target inequality — a response that computes the bound correctly but merely states the error "equals" 0.01 (rather than "is at most" or "≤" 0.01) does **not** earn the connection point. **Real Chief Reader Report data confirms this is a genuine, high-error pattern:** BC5's connection/analysis point (P7) had a mean of only 0.28/1, the lowest point on that question (overall question mean 5.21/9) — consistent with students computing the numeric bound correctly but failing on the required inequality language.

*Topic 10.13 (radius and interval of convergence of power series):* EK LIM-8.D.1 gives the CED's exact power-series definition, quoted verbatim: "A power series is a series of the form $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n(x-r)^n$, where $n$ is a non-negative integer, $\{a_n\}$ is a sequence of real numbers, and $r$ is a real number." (Note this topic-local use of $r$ as the series' center is a different symbol from Unit 9's polar-radius $r(\theta)$ — do not conflate the two in cross-unit review.) EK LIM-8.D.4 is quoted verbatim and is explicitly CED-mandated, not just a scoring convention: "The radius of convergence of a power series can be used to identify an open interval on which the series converges, but it is necessary to test both endpoints of the interval to determine the interval of convergence." Zero explicit exclusion statement for 10.13 itself (its only interaction with an exclusion is that endpoint testing must use one of the six tests named in Topic 10.8's boxed exclusion).

**Real scoring architecture, 2025 BC6 Part A (radius/interval of convergence, overall question mean 4.32/9):** the point ladder for a single interval-of-convergence sub-part splits into an unusually fine five-way structure: ratio setup, limit evaluation, interior-interval identification, considering both endpoints, and endpoint analysis plus final stated interval. The final point (endpoint analysis, P5) had a mean of only **0.14/1** — by far the lowest point on the question and, alongside Unit 9's BC2 P6 (0.06/1) above, one of the two lowest point means documented anywhere in this fact pack's BC-specific research. Endpoint-of-interval-of-convergence analysis is a genuine, real, high-error-rate skill and a strong target for authoring/review emphasis.

**Documented misconceptions, CR report verbatim, BC6 Part A:** (1) "Some students presented intervals not centered about $x=4$" from arithmetic mistakes — e.g., incorrectly simplifying $-1 < \frac{x-4}{3} < 1$ to "$-1 < x < 7$" or "$\frac{11}{3} < x < \frac{14}{3}$" instead of the correct $1 < x < 7$ — real, quotable, appropriate MCQ distractor; (2) imprecise "radius of convergence" vs. "interval of convergence" language — some responses concluded "no, 8 is outside of the radius of convergence" (radius is a single number, not a set; the CED's own EK LIM-8.D.4 distinguishes the two explicitly) — a real, documented, notation-precision distractor; (3) a genuine, documented scoring tolerance: omitting absolute value bars when setting up the ratio test "did not have an impact on scoring for P1 or P2," provided the response still resolves to a symmetric interval — mirrors the "at most one error tolerated" leniency pattern already documented for Unit 4 related rates in the Units 4-8 pass.

*Topics 10.14-10.15 (Taylor/Maclaurin series, representing functions as power series):* EK LIM-8.F.1/F.2 quoted verbatim: "The Maclaurin series for $\dfrac{1}{1-x}$ is a geometric series" and "The Maclaurin series for $\sin x$, $\cos x$, and $e^x$ provides the foundation for constructing the Maclaurin series for other functions." Notably, the CED never writes out the actual series expansions for $\sin x$, $\cos x$, or $e^x$ anywhere in its required-content text — these three "foundation" series are treated as prerequisite/assumed knowledge, not CED-supplied formulas. This is confirmed by real exam practice: the 2026 released FRQ (Question 6, Part D) asks students cold to "Write the first three nonzero terms of the Maclaurin series for $e^x$" with no series given anywhere in the question stem — i.e., real AP exam practice confirms these three series are expected to be memorized or independently derived, not looked up mid-exam. When authoring or reviewing an item that relies on these three series, verify the model solution actually states them correctly (e.g., $e^x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^n}{n!}$) rather than assuming a student or an authoring model will supply them without error.

**Coverage gap, real and worth flagging directly:** no 2025 or 2026 released BC FRQ with an official scoring guide directly tests Topics 10.14-10.15 in isolation — both years' Question 6 leans on Topics 10.11-10.13 (Taylor polynomials, Lagrange error bound, radius/interval of convergence) plus geometric-series manipulation. The 2026 Question 6 (Maclaurin series manipulation across parts A, B, and D) has no released scoring guide available as of this update — the same "FRQ exists, no official points yet" caveat already used for Unit 7 in the Units 4-8 pass. Treat Topics 10.14-10.15 authoring guidance as lower-confidence than 10.1-10.13, grounded mainly in the CED text and an unscored released FRQ rather than an official scoring guide.

## Course and laboratory requirements

AP Chemistry is equivalent to a college-level general chemistry course.

Students should have completed an introductory high school chemistry course and Algebra II or an equivalent course.

At least 25% of instructional time must be hands-on laboratory work. The course requires at least 16 hands-on labs, including at least six inquiry-based investigations.

Laboratory content is not an optional appendix to the course. Authoring and review should include experimental design, procedure analysis, data representation, error analysis, and evidence-based argumentation across the bank.

## Exam structure

The exam is 3 hours 15 minutes.

Section I: 60 multiple-choice questions, 90 minutes, 50% of the score.

Section II: 7 free-response questions, 105 minutes, 50% of the score. The section contains three 10-point long questions and four 4-point short questions.

A scientific or graphing calculator is recommended on both sections. Students receive a periodic table and an equations-and-constants sheet.

## Science practices

Practice 1 - Models and Representations: describe and extract quantitative information from particulate and macroscopic models.

Practice 2 - Question and Method: identify testable questions, formulate hypotheses, select procedures, collect observations, identify error, and predict how procedural changes affect results.

Practice 3 - Representing Data and Phenomena: create graphs, diagrams, and cross-scale representations.

Practice 4 - Model Analysis: use, test, and connect models across particulate and macroscopic scales.

Practice 5 - Mathematical Routines: identify quantities and relationships, interpret graphs, balance equations, and calculate with dimensional and significant-figure discipline.

Practice 6 - Argumentation: make claims and support them with experimental evidence, models, chemical principles, quantitative justification, and error analysis.

MCQ practice weighting: Practice 1, 8-12%; Practice 2, 8-12%; Practice 4, 23-30%; Practice 5, 35-42%; Practice 6, 8-12%. Practice 3 is not assessed in the MCQ section.

FRQ practice weighting: Practice 1, 2-4%; Practice 2, 10-16%; Practice 3, 8-16%; Practice 4, 5-9%; Practice 5, 43-53%; Practice 6, 15-24%.

## High-risk exclusion boundaries

Do not assess interpretation of mass spectra containing multiple elements or peaks from species other than singly charged monatomic ions.

Do not assess assigning quantum numbers to individual electrons or writing electron configurations for aufbau exceptions.

Do not require memorized specific crystal structures.

Hybridization scope is sp, sp2, and sp3 nomenclature plus sigma/pi distinctions. Do not assess derivation or depiction of hybrid orbitals, d-orbital hybridization, or molecular-orbital diagrams and filling.

Do not assess phase-diagram interpretation.

Do not assess colligative properties or solution calculations using molality, percent by mass, or percent by volume.

Do not assess the vocabulary labels “reducing agent” and “oxidizing agent,” rote solubility-rule memorization beyond the framework, or Lewis acid-base concepts.

Do not assess Arrhenius-equation calculations or experimental collection of data intended to detect a reaction intermediate.

Do not assess technical distinctions between enthalpy and internal energy or the formal concept of state functions.

Do not assess conversion between Kc and Kp or equilibria in which a dissolved species is in equilibrium with the same species in the gas phase.

For polyprotic-acid titrations, do not require concentration calculations for every species. Qualitative reasoning about dominant species remains in scope.

Do not assess calculating buffer pH change after adding acid/base or deriving the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation.

Do not assess calculating solubility as a function of pH.

Do not assess labeling an electrochemical electrode as positive or negative. Anode/cathode roles, oxidation/reduction, electron flow, cell potential, and galvanic/electrolytic behavior remain in scope.

The current framework places pH and solubility in Topic 8.11, not Unit 7. Free energy of dissolution is Topic 9.6.

Electrochemistry is part of Unit 9 and runs through Topic 9.11. Do not use the older 9.7-9.10 numbering.

FRQ sets must preserve the current three-long/four-short structure and include quantitative reasoning, experimental/laboratory reasoning, representations, and evidence-based justification.

Use the provided equations sheet as the boundary for formula availability. Avoid making recall of a provided equation the only source of difficulty.

### General exam-wide conventions (apply beyond Units 1-3)
- "Thermodynamically favored" is the CED's preferred term over
  "spontaneous," specifically to avoid students conflating it with "sudden"
  or "without cause" (EK 9.3.A.2). Authored items/rubrics should use
  "thermodynamically favored," not "spontaneous."
- "Hydronium ion" and $H_3O^+(aq)$ are the CED's preferred terms/notation for
  the aqueous hydrogen ion, but $H^+(aq)$ is explicitly stated as also
  accepted on the AP Exam (EK 8.1.A.1).
- Scoring is part-specific and frequently carries explicit
  error-carried-forward credit: 2025 Scoring Guidelines repeatedly award a
  later point "consistent with" an earlier (possibly wrong) part's answer
  (verified directly in Q1 parts D/E(ii)/E(iii)/F of the 2025 SG).
- Chief Reader Report guidance (not a CED rule, but an observed reader
  convention): the number of decimal places reported for a log-derived value
  (pH, pOH, pKa, pKb) should match the number of significant figures in the
  underlying concentration/K value — e.g. a $2.75\times10^{-6}$ M (3 sig
  fig) $[OH^-]$ should yield a pOH reported to 3 decimal places (5.561), not
  fewer or more.
- Some rubric points explicitly do **not** require units or a sign to earn
  credit, while others explicitly do — this is stated point-by-point in the
  2025 SG, not by a blanket exam-wide rule; do not assume uniform
  units/sign requirements across all criteria when authoring rubrics.

### Unit 1 — Atomic Structure and Properties (7-9%)

- **1.1 Moles and Molar Mass.** EK: no direct particle count in the lab, so
  mass-to-particle-count needs a bridge. $N_A = 6.022\times10^{23}\,mol^{-1}$
  connects moles to particle count; average mass in amu of one particle
  equals the molar mass in grams. **EQN:** $n = m/M$. Zero explicit
  exclusion statements.
- **1.2 Mass Spectra of Elements.** EK: a single-element mass spectrum gives
  isotope identity and relative abundance; average atomic mass is the
  isotope-weighted average. **Exclusion Statement (verbatim):**
  "Interpreting mass spectra of samples containing multiple elements or
  peaks arising from species other than singly charged monatomic ions will
  not be assessed on the AP Exam." (This is the CED source of the existing
  flat-list item 1.)
- **1.3 Elemental Composition of Pure Substances.** EK: law of definite
  proportions — the mass ratio of constituent elements in any pure sample of
  a compound is always the same; empirical formula = lowest whole-number
  atom ratio. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **1.4 Composition of Mixtures.** EK: pure substances contain one particle
  type; mixtures contain two-or-more types in variable proportion; elemental
  analysis determines relative atom counts and purity. Zero explicit
  exclusion statements.
- **1.5 Atomic Structure and Electron Configuration.** EK: atom =
  negatively-charged electrons + positively-charged nucleus (protons +
  neutrons). Coulomb's law gives force between charged particles: **EQN:**
  $F_{coulombic}\propto q_1q_2/r^2$. Shell/subshell structure from the Aufbau
  principle; ionization energy trends estimated qualitatively via Coulomb's
  law (distance from nucleus + effective/shielded nuclear charge).
  **Exclusion Statement (verbatim):** "The assignment of quantum numbers to
  electrons in subshells of an atom will not be assessed on the AP Exam."
  (Source of the first half of existing flat-list item 2.)
- **1.6 Photoelectron Spectroscopy.** EK: PES peak position = energy needed
  to remove an electron from that subshell; peak height is (ideally)
  proportional to the number of electrons in that subshell. Zero explicit
  exclusion statements.
- **1.7 Periodic Trends.** EK: periodic table organization reflects
  recurring ground-state electron-configuration patterns and filled/partial
  shell presence. Periodicity (ionization energy, atomic/ionic radii,
  electron affinity, electronegativity) is explained qualitatively via
  Coulomb's law, shell model, shielding, and effective nuclear charge; used
  to predict/estimate property values absent data. **Exclusion Statement
  (verbatim):** "Writing the electron configuration of elements that are
  exceptions to the aufbau principle will not be assessed on the AP Exam."
  (Source of the second half of existing flat-list item 2 — this is a
  distinct topic/statement from the 1.5 quantum-number exclusion, though
  the prior flat list merged them into one sentence.)
- **1.8 Valence Electrons and Ionic Compounds.** EK: bond likelihood is
  governed by valence-electron/nucleus interactions; same-column elements
  form analogous compounds; typical ionic charges are predicted from
  valence-electron count and periodic-table position. Zero explicit
  exclusion statements.

**Documented misconception (2025 FRQ Q1, mass spectrum/isotopes, Topics
1.2/1.7):** mean score on the isotope-mass-difference point was 0.55/1.0.
The dominant error was attributing the mass difference between Mg-25 and
Mg-26 to differing numbers of *electrons and/or protons* rather than
neutrons — a real, frequently-observed confusion between isotope identity
(protons, fixed) and isotope mass (neutrons, variable), worth mirroring as a
distractor pattern. A companion point (annotating a mass-spectrum graph with
a second isotope line) scored 0.72/1.0, with the main error being
misreading the y-axis scale.

### Unit 2 — Compound Structure and Properties (7-9%)

- **2.1 Types of Chemical Bonds.** EK: electronegativity increases
  left-to-right across a period, decreases down a group (explained via
  shell model + Coulomb's law). Nonpolar covalent = shared valence electrons
  between similar-electronegativity atoms (C–H bonds are "effectively
  nonpolar" despite a small real difference); polar covalent = shared
  electrons between unequal-electronegativity atoms, with greater
  electronegativity difference producing greater bond dipole in single
  bonds; all polar bonds have some ionic character (ionic/covalent is a
  continuum, not a hard distinction). Metal-nonmetal bonds are generally
  ionic, nonmetal-nonmetal generally covalent, but compound *properties* are
  the best characterization tool. In metallic solids, valence electrons are
  delocalized, not tied to individual atoms. Zero explicit exclusion
  statements.
- **2.2 Intramolecular Force and Potential Energy.** EK: a potential-energy-
  vs-internuclear-distance graph shows equilibrium bond length (lowest PE
  point) and bond energy (energy to separate atoms). Higher bond order
  (single→double→triple) = shorter bond, larger bond energy. Coulomb's law
  governs cation-anion interaction strength: larger charges and smaller ions
  (shorter internuclear distance) both increase strength. Zero explicit
  exclusion statements.
- **2.3 Structure of Ionic Solids.** EK: cations/anions arrange in a
  systematic, periodic 3-D array maximizing attractive and minimizing
  repulsive Coulombic forces. **Exclusion Statement (verbatim):**
  "Knowledge of specific crystal structures is not essential to an
  understanding of the learning objective and will not be assessed on the
  AP Exam." (Source of existing flat-list item 3.)
- **2.4 Structure of Metals and Alloys.** EK: metallic bonding = positive
  metal-ion array + delocalized "sea of electrons." Interstitial alloys
  (e.g. steel: C in Fe interstices) form between atoms of significantly
  different radii; substitutional alloys (e.g. some brass) form between
  atoms of comparable radius. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **2.5 Lewis Diagrams.** EK: Lewis diagrams follow an established
  construction procedure. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **2.6 Resonance and Formal Charge.** EK: when more than one equivalent
  Lewis structure is possible, resonance is a required refinement for
  qualitatively accurate structure/property prediction. Octet rule + formal
  charge select among nonequivalent candidate structures. The Lewis model
  has real limitations, particularly for odd-electron-count species. Zero
  explicit exclusion statements.
- **2.7 VSEPR and Hybridization.** EK: VSEPR uses Coulombic repulsion
  between electron pairs to predict arrangement around a central atom;
  applies with Lewis diagrams to predict geometry (linear, trigonal planar,
  tetrahedral, trigonal pyramidal, bent, trigonal bipyramidal, seesaw,
  T-shaped, octahedral, square pyramidal, square planar), bond angles,
  relative bond energies/lengths, dipole presence, and valence-orbital
  hybridization. Ideal bond angles: sp = 180°, sp² = 120°, sp³ = 109.5°.
  Sigma bonds (stronger, from head-on orbital overlap) vs. pi bonds (from
  multiple-bond overlap; prevent bond rotation, cause geometric isomers;
  weaker, lower bond energy than sigma). **Exclusion Statements (verbatim,
  three — the prior flat list merged all three into one sentence):** (1)
  "An understanding of the derivation and depiction of hybrid orbitals will
  not be assessed on the AP Exam. The course includes the distinction
  between sigma and pi bonding, the use of VSEPR to explain the shapes of
  molecules, and the sp, sp2, and sp3 nomenclature." (2) "Hybridization
  involving d orbitals will not be assessed on the AP Exam. When an atom has
  more than four pairs of electrons surrounding the central atom, students
  are only responsible for the shape of the resulting molecule." (3)
  "Molecular orbital theory is recommended as a way to provide deeper
  insight into bonding. However, the AP Exam will neither explicitly assess
  molecular orbital diagrams, filling of molecular orbitals, nor the
  distinction between bonding, nonbonding, and antibonding orbitals."

**Documented misconception (2025 FRQ Q4/Q5, Topic 2.7):** hybridization
identification (Q4 Part A) scored only 0.56/1.0 — the dominant error was
counting four electron domains on a carbon with a C=O double bond (yielding
incorrect sp³) instead of the correct three bonding regions (sp²); other
wrong-format responses gave sigma/pi counts, electron configurations, or
molecular geometry instead of a hybridization label. VSEPR geometry
identification for a more complex, multi-central-atom molecule (Q5 Part A,
trimethylsilanol) was comparatively accessible (0.65/1.0), but common wrong
answers included "tetrahedral pyramidal" (an invalid compound label) and
giving a bond angle (109.5°) without a geometry name.

### Unit 3 — Properties of Substances and Mixtures (18-22%, the largest MCQ domain)

- **3.1 Intermolecular and Interparticle Forces.** EK: London dispersion
  forces arise from Coulombic interaction between temporary/fluctuating
  dipoles and are often the strongest net IMF between large molecules — the
  CED explicitly cautions "the term 'London dispersion forces' should not be
  used synonymously with the term 'van der Waals forces.'" Dipole-dipole
  forces occur between polar molecules (typically stronger than comparable
  nonpolar-molecule London forces because dipole-dipole acts *in addition
  to* London forces). Ion-dipole forces occur between ions and polar
  molecules and are typically stronger than dipole-dipole. Hydrogen bonding
  = strong IMF when H covalently bonded to N/O/F is attracted to the
  negative end of a dipole from N/O/F in a different molecule (or different
  part of the same molecule). Zero explicit exclusion statements on this
  topic itself.
- **3.2 Properties of Solids.** EK: vapor pressure/boiling point correlate
  directly with IMF strength (IMFs fully overcome on vaporization); melting
  point correlates more subtly (IMFs only rearrange). Four solid classes:
  ionic (low vapor pressure, high mp/bp, brittle, conducts only when ions
  are mobile — molten or dissolved), covalent network (3-D like diamond or
  layered like graphite; nonmetals/metalloids only; high mp, rigid — except
  graphite, soft because layers slide), molecular (weak IMFs between
  covalently-bonded units; low mp; non-conducting), metallic (good
  conductors, malleable/ductile; interstitial alloying reduces
  malleability/ductility but retains conductivity). Zero explicit exclusion
  statements.
- **3.3 Solids, Liquids, and Gases.** EK: solids may be crystalline
  (regular 3-D arrangement) or amorphous (irregular); solid/liquid molar
  volumes are typically similar (particles in close contact in both); gas
  particles are in constant motion with neither definite volume nor shape.
  **Exclusion Statement (verbatim):** "Understanding/interpreting phase
  diagrams will not be assessed on the AP Exam." (Source of existing
  flat-list item 5.)
- **3.4 Ideal Gas Law.** **EQN:** $PV = nRT$. Partial pressure is
  proportional to mole fraction: **EQN:** $P_A = P_{total}\times X_A$, where
  $X_A = mol_A/mol_{total}$; **EQN:** $P_{total}=P_A+P_B+P_C+\ldots$. Zero
  explicit exclusion statements.
- **3.5 Kinetic Molecular Theory.** EK: KMT relates macroscopic gas
  properties to particle motion; the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution
  describes the kinetic-energy distribution at a given temperature. **EQN:**
  $KE=\tfrac12mv^2$. Kelvin temperature is proportional to average particle
  KE. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **3.6 Deviation from Ideal Gas Law.** EK: real-gas deviations arise from
  interparticle attraction (especially near condensation conditions) and
  from particle volume (especially at very high pressure). Zero explicit
  exclusion statements.
- **3.7 Solutions and Mixtures.** EK: solutions (homogeneous mixtures) can
  be solid/liquid/gas, with uniform macroscopic properties throughout (vs.
  heterogeneous mixtures, which vary by location). **EQN:**
  $M = n_{solute}/L_{solution}$. Zero explicit exclusion statements on this
  specific topic (the molality/percent exclusion below is filed under 3.8).
- **3.8 Representations of Solutions.** EK: particulate models communicate
  solution structure/properties via relative concentration and interaction
  drawings. **Exclusion Statements (verbatim, two — the prior flat list
  merged both into one sentence):** (1) "Colligative properties will not be
  assessed on the AP Exam." (2) "Calculations of molality, percent by mass,
  and percent by volume for solutions will not be assessed on the AP Exam."
  (Source of existing flat-list item 6.)
- **3.9 Separation of Solutions and Mixtures.** EK: liquid-solution
  components cannot be separated by filtration; separation instead exploits
  differential IMF strength (e.g. chromatography — paper, thin-layer,
  column — separates by differential interaction strength between mobile-
  and stationary-phase components; the resulting chromatogram infers
  relative component polarity). Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **3.10 Solubility.** EK: substances with similar IMFs tend to be miscible/
  soluble in one another ("like dissolves like," stated via IMF-similarity
  language rather than the mnemonic itself). Zero explicit exclusion
  statements.
- **3.11 Spectroscopy and the Electromagnetic Spectrum.** EK: absorption/
  emission region correlates with transition type — microwave = molecular
  rotational transitions; infrared = molecular vibrational transitions;
  UV/visible = electronic energy-level transitions. Zero explicit exclusion
  statements. (Topic renumbering note: this topic's correct current CED
  number is 3.11 — verified directly against the source PDF, matching the
  existing topic map.)
- **3.12 Properties of Photons.** EK: photon absorption/emission changes
  species energy by an amount equal to the photon's energy. **EQN:**
  $c=\lambda\nu$ (wavelength/frequency/speed of light); **EQN:** $E=h\nu$
  (Planck's equation). Zero explicit exclusion statements. (Confirms the
  existing fact pack's correction that this topic is "Properties of
  Photons," not an older "Photoelectric Effect" label — verified directly
  against the CED page.)
- **3.13 Beer-Lambert Law.** **EQN:** $A=\varepsilon bc$ (absorbance = molar
  absorptivity × path length × concentration). In most experiments path
  length and wavelength are held constant, so absorbance is proportional
  only to concentration; spectrophotometers are typically set to the
  wavelength of maximum absorbance for best sensitivity. Zero explicit
  exclusion statements.

**Documented misconception (2025 FRQ Q1/Q2/Q4/Q5, Topic 3.1 IMF —
recurring across multiple questions this cycle):** the single most
frequently-tested topic-3 concept in 2025. In Q1 Part B(ii) (ionic radius
vs. Coulombic attraction strength), the dominant error (mean score only
0.11/1.0, the lowest point on the entire exam) was treating "r" in
Coulomb's law as the radius of one ion rather than the interparticle
separation between ion and water — explicitly flagged by the Chief Reader
as the single most important IMF-reasoning error observed in the whole
2025 administration. In Q4 Part B (hydrogen-bond identification in a
particulate diagram), common wrong answers included drawing the
attraction from the wrong atom (O of methanol to H of formaldehyde, rather
than O–H hydrogen to formaldehyde O), drawing same-molecule-type
attractions instead of the prompted cross-molecule pair, and confusing
intramolecular bonds with intermolecular forces. In Q5 Part B (comparing
London dispersion strength between C- and Si-centered molecules), the
dominant error was citing molar mass alone as justification rather than the
correct reasoning (more occupied electron shells → larger, more
polarizable electron cloud).

## Units 4-6 deep-tier detail (2026-08-08)

### Unit 4 — Chemical Reactions (7-9%)

- **4.1 Introduction for Reactions.** EK: physical change = property change
  without composition change (phase changes, mixture formation/separation);
  chemical change = transformation into new substance(s), typically with
  different composition — evidenced by heat/light, gas formation,
  precipitate formation, and/or color change. Zero explicit exclusion
  statements.
- **4.2 Net Ionic Equations.** EK: all physical/chemical processes can be
  represented symbolically by balanced equations; balancing enforces
  conservation of both atoms and charge. Molecular, complete ionic, and net
  ionic equations are differing symbolic forms chosen by context. Zero
  explicit exclusion statements.
- **4.3 Representations of Reactions.** EK: balanced equations in any form
  translate into symbolic particulate representations. Zero explicit
  exclusion statements.
- **4.4 Physical and Chemical Changes.** EK: bond-breaking/forming
  processes are typically chemical; IMF-only changes (e.g. phase changes)
  are typically physical — but some processes (e.g. salt dissolution) admit
  plausible arguments either way, since ionic-bond breaking and ion-dipole
  formation both occur. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **4.5 Stoichiometry.** EK: because atoms are conserved, product amounts
  can be calculated from known reactant amounts and vice versa, using
  balanced-equation coefficients as mole-ratio information; stoichiometry
  combines with the ideal gas law and molarity for gas/solution
  calculations. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **4.6 Introduction to Titration.** EK: the titrant (known concentration)
  reacts specifically/quantitatively with the analyte; the equivalence point
  is total analyte consumption; the endpoint is the observable property
  change (e.g. color) signaling it. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **4.7 Types of Chemical Reactions.** EK: acid-base reactions transfer one
  or more protons; redox reactions transfer one or more electrons (tracked
  via oxidation-number changes) — combustion is an important redox
  subclass, producing CO₂ + H₂O from complete hydrocarbon combustion;
  precipitation reactions mix aqueous ions to form an insoluble/sparingly
  soluble ionic compound (all Na⁺, K⁺, NH₄⁺, and NO₃⁻ salts are soluble).
  **Exclusion Statements (verbatim, two — the prior flat list merged both
  into one sentence with item 8.6's Lewis exclusion):** (1) "The meaning of
  the terms 'reducing agent' and 'oxidizing agent' will not be assessed on
  the AP Exam." (2) "Rote memorization of 'solubility rules' other than
  those implied in 4.7.A.5 will not be assessed on the AP Exam." (Source of
  the first two clauses of existing flat-list item 7.)
- **4.8 Introduction to Acid-Base Reactions.** EK: Brønsted-Lowry acid =
  proton donor, base = proton acceptor; water plays a dual donor/acceptor
  role in aqueous acid-base chemistry; conjugate acid-base pairs and
  relative strength can be identified from ionization. **Exclusion
  Statement (verbatim):** "Lewis acid-base concepts will not be assessed on
  the AP Exam. The emphasis in AP Chemistry is on reactions in aqueous
  solution." (Source of the third clause of existing flat-list item 7.)
- **4.9 Oxidation-Reduction (Redox) Reactions.** EK: balanced redox
  equations can be constructed from half-reactions. Zero explicit exclusion
  statements. (Note: CED's full topic title is "Oxidation-Reduction (Redox)
  Reactions" — the existing topic map's shorter "Oxidation-Reduction
  Reactions" is an acceptable alias, not an error.)

**Documented misconception (2025 FRQ Q6, Topics 4.5/4.9 — the single
lowest-scoring FRQ on the 2025 exam, mean 1.34/4):** writing a correctly-
balanced net ionic redox equation (Part B) was the most accessible point
(0.52/1.0); the two most challenging points on this question were
stoichiometric mass comparison across a redox reaction (Part C, 0.10/1.0 —
students routinely stopped at a mole-ratio comparison without converting to
mass via molar mass, e.g. concluding Zn's mass change is greater "because 3
mol Zn form per 2 mol Al" without multiplying through by each element's
molar mass) and constructing an oxidation half-reaction with electrons
correctly placed as a product (Part A errors included writing the
*reduction* direction for a species already identified as the anode, and
omitting electrons from the half-reaction entirely).

### Unit 5 — Kinetics (7-9%)

- **5.1 Reaction Rates.** EK: reaction rate = rate of reactant-to-product
  conversion per unit time; reactant/product concentration-change rates are
  fixed by balanced-equation stoichiometry; rate is influenced by
  concentration, temperature, surface area, catalysts, and other
  environmental factors. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **5.2 Introduction to Rate Law.** EK: rate is proportional to each
  reactant's concentration raised to a power (its order); the sum of
  reactant orders is the overall reaction order; the rate constant's units
  reflect the overall order and the constant is temperature-dependent;
  comparing initial rates determines individual reactant orders. Zero
  explicit exclusion statements.
- **5.3 Concentration Changes over Time.** EK: reaction order can be
  inferred from a concentration-vs-time graph shape. Integrated rate laws:
  **EQN (zeroth order):** $[A]_t-[A]_0=-kt$; **EQN (first order):**
  $\ln[A]_t-\ln[A]_0=-kt$ (linear ln[A] vs. t); **EQN (second order):**
  $1/[A]_t-1/[A]_0=kt$ (linear 1/[A] vs. t). Half-life (first order only,
  constant): **EQN:** $t_{1/2}=0.693/k$. Radioactive decay is an important
  first-order-kinetics illustration. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **5.4 Elementary Reactions.** EK: an elementary reaction's rate law can be
  inferred directly from collision stoichiometry; simultaneous three(+)-
  particle collisions are rare. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **5.5 Collision Model.** EK: successful elementary-reaction collisions
  need both sufficient energy (overcoming activation energy) and correct
  orientation; the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution gives a qualitative
  estimate of the energetically-sufficient collision fraction and its
  temperature dependence. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **5.6 Reaction Energy Profile.** EK: the reaction coordinate plots energy
  from reactants through a transition state to products; the
  reactant/transition-state energy difference is the forward activation
  energy; the Arrhenius equation relates rate-temperature dependence to
  activation energy. **Exclusion Statement (verbatim):** "Calculations
  involving the Arrhenius equation will not be assessed on the AP Exam."
  (Source of the first clause of existing flat-list item 8.)
- **5.7 Introduction to Reaction Mechanisms.** EK: a mechanism = sequence of
  elementary steps (reactants, intermediates, products, catalysts) that sum
  to the overall balanced equation; an intermediate is produced by some
  steps and consumed by others, present only transiently. **Exclusion
  Statement (verbatim):** "Collection of data pertaining to detection of a
  reaction intermediate will not be assessed on the AP Exam." (Source of the
  second clause of existing flat-list item 8; this identical exclusion
  sentence is restated verbatim again under 5.8 in the CED.)
- **5.8 Reaction Mechanism and Rate Law.** EK: for mechanisms where every
  step is irreversible, or where the first step is rate-limiting, the
  overall rate law is set by the slowest (rate-limiting) elementary step's
  molecularity. **Exclusion Statement:** identical restatement of the 5.7
  reaction-intermediate-detection exclusion (see above).
- **5.9 Pre-Equilibrium Approximation.** EK: when the first elementary step
  is *not* rate-limiting, approximations such as pre-equilibrium are needed
  to derive the rate law. Zero explicit exclusion statements. (Confirms the
  existing fact pack's correction: this is "Pre-Equilibrium Approximation,"
  not an older "Steady-State Approximation" label — verified directly
  against the CED page.)
- **5.10 Multistep Reaction Energy Profile.** EK: elementary-step energetics
  combine into a full multistep-reaction energy profile. Zero explicit
  exclusion statements.
- **5.11 Catalysis.** EK: a catalyst increases effective-collision frequency
  and/or lowers activation energy via an alternate pathway; catalyst net
  concentration is constant overall, though it may be consumed in the
  rate-determining step and regenerated later. Enzyme catalysis
  (reactant-binding), acid-base catalysis (covalent, proton gain/loss), and
  surface catalysis (covalent/binding to a surface) are named catalytic
  mechanisms, each introducing new intermediates/elementary reactions. Zero
  explicit exclusion statements.

**Documented misconception (2025 FRQ Q7 Part C, Topic 5.11, catalyst
identification in a mechanism):** mean score 0.51/1.0. The dominant error
was definitional rather than mechanistic — stating a generic catalyst
property ("increases rate," "lowers activation energy") without citing the
specific mechanistic evidence required (consumed as a reactant in step 1,
regenerated as a product in step 2, absent from the overall equation); a
secondary error confused the catalyst with a reaction intermediate. Chief
Reader note: catalyst/intermediate/rate-determining-step questions require
evaluating the *individual mechanism steps*, not the overall summed
equation — the overall equation is a validity check only, and its
absence-of-species is a common distractor students misread as "spectator
ion" reasoning.

### Unit 6 — Thermochemistry (7-9%)

- **6.1 Endothermic and Exothermic Processes.** EK: temperature change
  signals energy change; exothermic = system loses energy to surroundings
  (heat/work out); endothermic = system gains energy from surroundings
  (heat/work in); solution formation may be either, depending on relative
  IMF strength before/after dissolution. Zero explicit exclusion
  statements.
- **6.2 Energy Diagrams.** EK: a physical/chemical process can be
  represented with an energy diagram showing its endothermic/exothermic
  character. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **6.3 Heat Transfer and Thermal Equilibrium.** EK: warmer-body particles
  have greater average KE; collisions transfer energy ("heat transfer");
  thermal equilibrium = equal average KE (equal temperature) reached via
  continued collision. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **6.4 Heat Capacity and Calorimetry.** **EQN:** $q=mc\Delta T$. First law
  of thermodynamics: energy is conserved in chemical/physical processes.
  Equal thermal-energy transfer does not produce equal temperature change
  in equal masses of differing specific heat capacity. In dissolution
  calorimetry, a temperature increase signals exothermic dissolution
  (energy released) and a decrease signals endothermic (energy absorbed).
  Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **6.5 Energy of Phase Changes.** EK: melting/boiling requires energy input
  (system energy increases); freezing/condensing releases energy (system
  energy decreases); temperature is constant during a pure-substance phase
  change. The energy absorbed in one phase-change direction equals the
  negative of the energy released in the complementary direction (e.g.
  ΔH_condensation = −ΔH_vaporization). Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **6.6 Introduction to Enthalpy of Reaction.** EK: reaction enthalpy change
  = heat released (negative) or absorbed (positive) at constant pressure;
  the enthalpy difference between reactants and products (from
  bond-breaking/forming) manifests as a kinetic-energy/temperature change in
  particles. **Exclusion Statement (verbatim):** "The technical distinctions
  between enthalpy and internal energy will not be assessed on the AP Exam.
  Most reactions studied at the AP level are carried out at constant
  pressure, where the enthalpy change of the process is equal to the heat
  (and by extension, the energy) of reaction." (Source of the first clause
  of existing flat-list item 9.)
- **6.7 Bond Enthalpies.** EK: average bond energies of all reactant bonds
  broken vs. all product bonds formed can be summed to estimate reaction
  enthalpy — energy released > energy required means exothermic, and vice
  versa. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **6.8 Enthalpy of Formation.** **EQN:**
  $\Delta H^{\circ}_{reaction}=\Sigma\Delta H_f^{\circ}{}_{products}-\Sigma\Delta H_f^{\circ}{}_{reactants}$.
  Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **6.9 Hess's Law.** EK: (i) reversing a reaction reverses the sign of ΔH
  but not its magnitude; (ii) multiplying a reaction by factor $c$
  multiplies ΔH by $c$; (iii) summing reactions sums their individual ΔH
  values to the net ΔH. **Exclusion Statement (verbatim):** "The concept of
  state functions will not be assessed on the AP Exam." (Source of the
  second clause of existing flat-list item 9.)

**Documented misconception (2025 FRQ Q3 Parts C/D/E, Topics 6.4/6.6/6.9):**
the calorimetry heat calculation itself (Part C(i), $q=mc\Delta T$) was the
*least* accessible point on the entire Q3 (mean 0.06/1.0) — but the Chief
Reader is explicit that this was overwhelmingly a **significant-figures
failure, not a conceptual one**: "When students missed this point, it was
more commonly missed due to the significant figures error than due to a
misconception of the heat calculation." A related error used only the
solute mass (0.1 g) rather than the total solution mass (100.1 g) in the
$q=mc\Delta T$ substitution. For the enthalpy-of-reaction follow-up (Part
C(ii)), a common error omitted the negative sign despite a measured
temperature increase clearly indicating an exothermic reaction. For the
calorimetry-error-analysis part (Part D, "less limiting reactant added"),
the dominant gap was failing to connect a smaller limiting-reactant mass to
a smaller *moles reacted* and therefore smaller thermal-energy release —
students often reasoned about "a warm solid placed in water" instead. For
Hess's law (Part E), the dominant error added the given ΔH values without
first checking that the combined equations actually summed to the target
overall equation.

## Units 7-9 deep-tier detail (2026-08-08)

### Unit 7 — Equilibrium (7-9%; CED unit title is "Equilibrium," aliased in the course framework header as "Principles of Equilibrium")

- **7.1 Introduction to Equilibrium.** EK: many observable processes are
  reversible (evaporation/condensation, gas absorption/desorption, salt
  dissolution/precipitation; acid-base proton transfer and redox electron
  transfer are important reversible chemical examples). At equilibrium, no
  observable change occurs though forward/reverse processes continue at
  equal rates (dynamic equilibrium). Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **7.2 Direction of Reversible Reactions.** EK: net conversion direction is
  set by which of the forward/reverse rates is greater; equilibrium is
  reached when the rates are equal. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **7.3 Reaction Quotient and Equilibrium Constant.** EK: for
  $aA+bB\rightleftharpoons cC+dD$: **EQN:**
  $K_c=\dfrac{[C]^c[D]^d}{[A]^a[B]^b}$; **EQN:**
  $K_p=\dfrac{(P_C)^c(P_D)^d}{(P_A)^a(P_B)^b}$. $Q$ tends toward $K$ as
  equilibrium is approached ($K_c=Q_c$, $K_p=Q_p$ at equilibrium). Solids
  and pure liquids are excluded from the expression (concentration
  independent of amount). **Exclusion Statements (verbatim, two — the prior
  flat list merged both into one sentence):** (1) "Conversion between $K_c$
  and $K_p$ will not be assessed on the AP Exam. Students should be aware of
  the conceptual differences and pay attention to whether $K_c$ or $K_p$ is
  used in an exam question." (2) "Equilibrium calculations on systems where
  a dissolved species is in equilibrium with that species in the gas phase
  will not be assessed on the AP Exam." (Source of existing flat-list item
  10.)
- **7.4 Calculating the Equilibrium Constant.** EK: $K_c$/$K_p$ can be
  determined from experimentally measured equilibrium
  concentrations/partial pressures. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **7.5 Magnitude of the Equilibrium Constant.** EK: very large $K$ →
  reaction proceeds essentially to completion; very small $K$ → reaction
  barely proceeds. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **7.6 Properties of the Equilibrium Constant.** EK: reversing a reaction
  inverts $K$; multiplying stoichiometric coefficients by $c$ raises $K$ to
  the power $c$; summing reactions multiplies their $K$ values; all valid
  algebraic $K$ manipulations apply equally to $Q$ (identical mathematical
  form). Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **7.7 Calculating Equilibrium Concentrations.** EK: equilibrium
  concentrations/partial pressures are predictable from initial conditions
  + balanced reaction + $K$. $Q<K$ → net forward (toward products); $Q>K$ →
  net reverse (toward reactants); $Q=K$ → dynamic equilibrium, constant
  proportions. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **7.8 Representations of Equilibrium.** EK: particulate representations
  show relative reactant/product particle counts before and at equilibrium,
  and the $K$ value. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **7.9 Introduction to Le Châtelier's Principle.** EK: predicts system
  response to stresses (species addition/removal, temperature change,
  volume/pressure change in a gas-phase system, dilution) and the
  resulting effect on measurable properties (pH, temperature, color). Zero
  explicit exclusion statements.
- **7.10 Reaction Quotient and Le Châtelier's Principle.** EK: a disturbance
  makes $Q\ne K$, taking the system out of equilibrium; the system responds
  by bringing $Q$ back to $K$. Concentration-type stresses change $Q$ only;
  temperature changes change $K$ itself. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **7.11 Introduction to Solubility Equilibria.** EK: salt dissolution is a
  reversible process described by $K_{sp}$ (solubility-product constant);
  solubility can be calculated from $K_{sp}$ and vice versa (from molar
  solubility of a saturated solution); the 4.7.A.5 solubility rules can be
  related quantitatively to $K_{sp}$ (values >1 correspond to soluble
  salts). Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **7.12 Common-Ion Effect.** EK: a salt's solubility is reduced when
  dissolved into a solution already containing one of its constituent ions
  ("common-ion effect") — understood qualitatively via Le Châtelier's
  principle or calculated from $K_{sp}$. Zero explicit exclusion statements.

**Documented misconception (2025 FRQ Q1 Parts E/F and Q3 Part F, Topics
7.3/7.10/7.11):** writing a correct $K_{sp}$ expression itself was
accessible (mean 0.59/1.0), but common errors included keeping a term for
the solid (should be excluded), squaring/doubling the wrong species, using
parentheses instead of square brackets, and omitting the required square
on the doubly-charged-ion coefficient. The Q/Ksp *comparison-direction*
point (Part E(iii)) was moderately accessible (0.45/1.0) but a documented
specific error was inverting the magnitude comparison itself — misreading
$2.07\times10^{-11}$ as *greater than* $5.61\times10^{-12}$ — separate from
correctly stating the Q>Ksp→precipitates rule but then applying it
backwards. The lowest-scoring point on the entire 2025 exam's Q1 (0.17/1.0,
Part F, "common-ion effect in reverse" — removing rather than adding a
common ion by adding acid that consumes OH⁻) shows this qualitative-shift
reasoning is a genuine, not superficial, gap: the Chief Reader notes
explicitly that "just memorizing that 'basic salts are more soluble in
acidic solution than neutral solution' did not help the student earn this
point" — items should test the underlying Le Châtelier mechanism, not a
memorizable rule-of-thumb. For Kp calculation from a particulate diagram
(Q3 Part F(i), 0.43/1.0), the most common wrong result was $K_p=1\ atm$,
indicating a fundamental setup error rather than an arithmetic slip.

### Unit 8 — Acids and Bases (11-15%, second-largest MCQ domain)

- **8.1 Introduction to Acids and Bases.** **EQN:** $pH=-\log[H_3O^+]$;
  **EQN:** $pOH=-\log[OH^-]$. Water autoionization: **EQN:**
  $K_w=[H_3O^+][OH^-]=1.0\times10^{-14}$ at 25°C; **EQN:**
  $pK_w=14=pH+pOH$ at 25°C. $K_w$ (and thus neutral-water pH) is
  temperature-dependent — pH = 7.0 only at 25°C. "Hydrogen ion"/$H^+(aq)$
  and "hydronium ion"/$H_3O^+(aq)$ are used interchangeably; hydronium/
  $H_3O^+(aq)$ is preferred but $H^+(aq)$ is also accepted (see General
  Conventions above). Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **8.2 pH and pOH of Strong Acids and Bases.** EK: strong acids (HCl, HBr,
  HI, HClO₄, H₂SO₄, HNO₃) fully ionize, so $[H_3O^+]$ = initial acid
  concentration; strong bases (Group I/II hydroxides) fully dissociate, so
  $[OH^-]$ = initial concentration for Group I hydroxides, or 2× initial
  concentration for Group II. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **8.3 Weak Acid and Base Equilibria.** **EQN:**
  $K_a=\dfrac{[H_3O^+][A^-]}{[HA]}$; **EQN:** $pK_a=-\log K_a$. **EQN:**
  $K_b=\dfrac{[OH^-][HB^+]}{[B]}$; **EQN:** $pK_b=-\log K_b$. Percent
  ionization is calculable from $pK_a$/$pK_b$ and initial concentration, or
  from initial + any equilibrium species concentration. For any conjugate
  pair: **EQN:** $K_w=K_a\times K_b$; **EQN:** $pK_w=pK_a+pK_b$. Zero
  explicit exclusion statements.
- **8.4 Acid-Base Reactions and Buffers.** EK: strong acid + strong base →
  quantitative neutralization, $H^+(aq)+OH^-(aq)\rightarrow H_2O(l)$, pH
  from excess-reagent concentration. Weak acid + strong base (or weak base +
  strong acid): if the weak species is in excess, a buffer forms and pH
  comes from the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation (8.9.A.1); if the strong
  species is in excess, pH comes from excess-ion concentration; if
  equimolar, pH comes from the conjugate-species hydrolysis equilibrium.
  Weak acid + weak base reacts to an equilibrium state. Zero explicit
  exclusion statements.
- **8.5 Acid-Base Titrations.** EK: a titration curve (pH vs. titrant
  volume) summarizes titration results. At the equivalence point for
  monoprotic acids/bases, moles titrant = moles analyte (works for both
  strong and weak titrations). At the half-equivalence point for a weak
  titration, $[HA]=[A^-]$, so $pH=pK_a$ — this lets $pK_a$ be read directly
  off the curve. At the equivalence point itself, pH is set by the major
  species present: neutral for strong-strong; basic (weak-acid titrations)
  or acidic (weak-base titrations) because the conjugate species
  hydrolyzes. For polyprotic acids, titration curves reveal the number of
  acidic protons and each proton's associated $pK_a$. **Exclusion Statement
  (verbatim, partial — qualitative reasoning explicitly remains in scope):**
  "Computation of the concentration of each species present in the
  titration curve for polyprotic acids will not be assessed on the AP Exam.
  Such computations for titration of monoprotic acids are within the scope
  of the course (see 8.4.A.2 and 8.4.A.3), as is qualitative reasoning
  regarding what species are present in large versus small concentrations
  at any point in a titration of a polyprotic acid." (Source of existing
  flat-list item 11 — confirmed the existing summary is accurate.)
- **8.6 Molecular Structure of Acids and Bases.** EK: acidic-proton strength
  is inferable from molecular structure — strong acids (HCl, HBr, HI,
  HClO₄, H₂SO₄, HNO₃) have very weak conjugate bases stabilized by
  electronegativity, inductive effects, resonance, or a combination;
  carboxylic acids are a common weak-acid class; strong bases (Group I/II
  hydroxides) have very weak conjugate acids; common weak bases include
  nitrogenous bases (e.g. ammonia) and carboxylate ions; electronegative
  elements stabilize the conjugate base relative to the conjugate acid,
  increasing acid strength. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **8.7 pH and pKa.** EK: protonation state predicted by comparing solution
  pH to acid $pK_a$ — pH < $pK_a$ means acid form dominates, pH > $pK_a$
  means base form dominates. Acid-base indicators change a property (e.g.
  color) between protonated/deprotonated states; accurate titration
  endpoints need an indicator with $pK_a$ close to the equivalence-point pH.
  Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **8.8 Properties of Buffers.** EK: a buffer contains a large concentration
  of both conjugate-pair members; the conjugate acid reacts with added
  base, the conjugate base reacts with added acid — this reaction pair is
  what stabilizes pH. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **8.9 Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation.** **EQN:**
  $pH=pK_a+\log\dfrac{[A^-]}{[HA]}$. This follows from the weak-acid
  equilibrium expression; small acid/base additions don't significantly
  change the $[A^-]/[HA]$ ratio, hence don't significantly change buffer
  pH. **Exclusion Statements (verbatim, two — the prior flat list merged
  both into one sentence):** (1) "Computation of the change in pH resulting
  from the addition of an acid or a base to a buffer will not be assessed on
  the AP Exam." (2) "Derivation of the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation will
  not be assessed on the AP Exam." (Source of existing flat-list item 12.)
- **8.10 Buffer Capacity.** EK: increasing buffer-component concentration
  (holding their ratio constant) keeps pH the same but increases capacity
  to neutralize added acid/base. A buffer with more conjugate acid than
  base has greater capacity for added base than acid, and vice versa. Zero
  explicit exclusion statements.
- **8.11 pH and Solubility.** EK: a salt's solubility is pH-sensitive when a
  constituent ion is a weak acid, weak base, or hydroxide ion — understood
  qualitatively via Le Châtelier's principle. **Exclusion Statement
  (verbatim):** "Computations of solubility as a function of pH will not be
  assessed on the AP Exam." (Source of existing flat-list item 13 —
  confirms the existing fact pack's correction that pH-and-solubility is
  Topic 8.11, not Unit 7.)

**Documented misconception (2025 FRQ Q1 Part C, Q2 Parts A/B, Topics
8.2/8.5/8.9 — the highest-density misconception cluster in the 2025
administration):** strong-base pH calculation (Q1 Part C, 0.65/1.0) had a
specific, well-documented failure mode: students correctly calculated pOH
but then stopped and reported that value *as* pH — the Chief Reader flags
this explicitly as a recurring, non-trivial confusion worth deliberate
distractor design. Titration-curve molarity-from-equivalence-point (Q2
Part B(i), 0.35/1.0) most often failed via an $M_1V_1=M_2V_2$ shortcut
applied incorrectly (using the wrong volume, or the *total* graph volume
rather than the equivalence-point volume) rather than explicit mole-to-mole
conversion — it earned credit here only because the specific system
happened to have a 1:1 mole ratio, masking the underlying error. $pK_a$-
from-half-equivalence-point (Q2 Part B(ii), 0.56/1.0) most often failed by
misidentifying the equivalence point's pH (rather than the
half-equivalence point's) as $pK_a$. The Henderson-Hasselbalch
ratio-solving point (Q2 Part B(iii), 0.28/1.0) was the least accessible of
this cluster — dominant errors used $\ln$/$e^x$ or $10^{-(pH-pK_a)}$ instead
of correctly solving $10^{(pH-pK_a)}$ for the ratio, and some responses set
up the equation correctly but never isolated the ratio.

### Unit 9 — Thermodynamics and Electrochemistry (7-9%; runs through Topic 9.11, not the older 9.7-9.10 numbering)

- **9.1 Introduction to Entropy.** EK: entropy increases as matter
  disperses (e.g. solid→liquid→gas phase changes; gas volume increase at
  constant T) and as the total gas-phase product mole count exceeds
  reactant mole count. Entropy also increases as energy disperses — per
  KMT, the kinetic-energy distribution broadens with increasing
  temperature, increasing system entropy. Zero explicit exclusion
  statements.
- **9.2 Absolute Entropy and Entropy Change.** **EQN:**
  $\Delta S^{\circ}_{reaction}=\Sigma S^{\circ}_{products}-\Sigma S^{\circ}_{reactants}$.
  Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **9.3 Gibbs Free Energy and Thermodynamic Favorability.** EK: standard
  Gibbs free energy change ($\Delta G^{\circ}$) applies when all
  reactants/products are in a standard state (pure substance, 1.0 M
  solution, or 1.0 atm/1.0 bar gas). "Thermodynamically favored" ($\Delta
  G^{\circ}<0$) is the CED's preferred term over "spontaneous" (see General
  Conventions above). **EQN:**
  $\Delta G^{\circ}_{reaction}=\Sigma\Delta G_f^{\circ}{}_{products}-\Sigma\Delta G_f^{\circ}{}_{reactants}$;
  **EQN:** $\Delta G^{\circ}=\Delta H^{\circ}-T\Delta S^{\circ}$. Sign-table
  shortcut (no calculation needed for these two cases): $\Delta H^{\circ}<0,
  \Delta S^{\circ}>0$ → favored at all T; $\Delta H^{\circ}>0, \Delta
  S^{\circ}<0$ → favored at no T. (The other two sign combinations —
  both negative, or both positive — require the numeric $\Delta
  H^{\circ}-T\Delta S^{\circ}$ calculation, favored at low T or high T
  respectively.) Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **9.4 Thermodynamic and Kinetic Control.** EK: many thermodynamically
  favored processes occur at immeasurably slow rates ("kinetic control,"
  commonly from high activation energy); a process not proceeding at a
  measurable rate does not imply the system is at equilibrium. Zero
  explicit exclusion statements.
- **9.5 Free Energy and Equilibrium.** EK: "thermodynamically favored"
  ($\Delta G^{\circ}<0$) means products are favored at equilibrium ($K>1$)
  under standard conditions. **EQN:** $K=e^{-\Delta G^{\circ}/RT}$; **EQN:**
  $\Delta G^{\circ}=-RT\ln K$. When $\Delta G^{\circ}\approx0$, $K\approx1$;
  larger-magnitude $\Delta G^{\circ}$ (either sign) pushes $K$ further from
  1. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **9.6 Free Energy of Dissolution.** EK: dissolution $\Delta G^{\circ}$
  reflects three factors — breaking solid IMFs, solvent reorganization
  around the dissolved species, and dissolved-species/solvent interaction —
  whose enthalpic/entropic signs and magnitudes can be estimated
  individually, though total-ΔG prediction is genuinely hard due to
  cancellation among the three. Zero explicit exclusion statements. (Confirms
  the existing fact pack's correction that this topic is 9.6, distinct from
  Unit 7 solubility equilibria.)
- **9.7 Coupled Reactions.** EK: an external energy source can drive a
  thermodynamically unfavorable process (e.g. electrical energy for
  electrolysis/battery charging; light driving CO₂→glucose photosynthesis).
  Coupling an unfavorable reaction to a favorable one via a shared
  intermediate (e.g. ATP↔ADP) can achieve a net-favorable ($\Delta
  G^{\circ}<0$) overall reaction. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **9.8 Galvanic (Voltaic) and Electrolytic Cells.** EK: each cell component
  (electrodes, half-cell solutions, salt bridge, voltage/current meter) has
  a specific functional role, describable at both macroscopic and
  particulate levels. Galvanic/voltaic cells involve a thermodynamically
  favored reaction; electrolytic cells involve an unfavored one. Oxidation
  always occurs at the anode, reduction always at the cathode (for *all*
  electrochemical cells, not just galvanic). **Exclusion Statement
  (verbatim):** "Labeling an electrode as positive or negative will not be
  assessed on the AP Exam." (Source of existing flat-list item 14.)
- **9.9 Cell Potential and Free Energy.** EK: thermodynamically favored
  redox reactions in a cell give positive voltage; unfavored ones give
  negative voltage (requiring externally applied potential). Standard cell
  potential is calculated from half-reactions' standard reduction
  potentials. **EQN:** $\Delta G^{\circ}=-nFE^{\circ}$ — positive
  $E^{\circ}$ ↔ thermodynamically favored; negative $E^{\circ}$ ↔ unfavored.
  Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **9.10 Cell Potential under Nonstandard Conditions.** EK: cell potential
  under nonstandard conditions is a driving force toward equilibrium — the
  farther from equilibrium, the larger the potential magnitude; the closer,
  the smaller, reaching zero exactly at equilibrium ($Q=K$). Le Châtelier's
  principle does *not* apply to electrochemical systems (they are not at
  equilibrium). **EQN (qualitative use only — see exclusion):**
  $E=E^{\circ}-(RT/nF)\ln Q$ (Nernst equation). **Exclusion Statement
  (verbatim, distinct framing — not a hard prohibition on the Nernst
  equation itself, but on algorithmic-only use):** "Algorithmic calculations
  using the Nernst equation are insufficient to demonstrate an
  understanding of electrochemical cells under nonstandard conditions.
  However, students should qualitatively understand the effects of
  concentration on cell potential and use conceptual reasoning, including
  the qualitative use of the Nernst equation... to solve problems." This is
  a distinct, more nuanced boundary than a simple exclusion and is not
  captured by the existing flat list — item authors should require
  qualitative justification alongside any Nernst-equation numeric answer,
  not accept a bare plug-and-chug.
- **9.11 Electrolysis and Faraday's Law.** EK: Faraday's laws relate
  electrons transferred, mass deposited/removed at an electrode
  (electroplating), current, elapsed time, and ionic species charge.
  **EQN:** $I=q/t$. Zero explicit exclusion statements.

**Documented misconception (2025 FRQ Q3 Parts B/F and Q6, Topics
9.1/9.3/9.8/9.9 — entropy/Gibbs reasoning was the single most conceptually
under-performing content pair on the whole 2025 exam):** the entropy-
decrease explanation point (Q3 Part B(i)) scored only 0.25/1.0; the
dominant failure was giving a "more order/less disorder" justification
*without* any particle-level discussion of the specific reaction species
(gas dispersal vs. solid dispersal, or microstate counting) — vague
order/disorder language alone did not earn credit. The companion
$\Delta G^{\circ}=\Delta H^{\circ}-T\Delta S^{\circ}$ favorability-reasoning
point (Part B(ii), 0.35/1.0) most often failed by claiming *both* $\Delta
H^{\circ}$ and $\Delta S^{\circ}$ contribute favorably when only one term's
sign actually supports favorability — some responses transcribed the
equation from the reference sheet without engaging the specific prompt at
all. The $K_p$-vs-temperature-shift reasoning point (Part F(ii), 0.31/1.0)
most often confused equilibrium-shift reasoning with an ideal-gas-law
pressure argument (claiming higher T directly raises system pressure)
instead of applying Le Châtelier's principle to an exothermic reaction.
For the galvanic-cell FRQ (Q6, the single lowest-mean FRQ of 2025 at
1.34/4), the maximum-cell-potential point (Part D, 0.23/1.0) had a
striking, well-documented misconception: many students assumed Zn had to
remain the *cathode* throughout the question (because it was the cathode
in Parts A-C) and never considered that Zn could instead serve as the
*anode* against a more-positive-reduction-potential metal (Au) to generate
a larger cell potential — a "the role established earlier in the question
transfers to later parts" reasoning error, not a calculation error, since
the arithmetic for the (wrong) Zn-cathode case was frequently done
correctly.

### General exam-wide conventions (apply to every FRQ, not just Units 1-3)
- Positive work is defined as work done **on** a system.
- Free-body-diagram convention: each force is a distinct straight arrow
  **starting on, and pointing away from,** the dot representing the object/
  system's center of mass; forces in the same direction are drawn side by
  side, never overlapping/stacked.
- Accepted force labels are specific: gravity accepts $F_G, F_g, F_{grav}, W,
  mg$ — **"G" or "g" alone is explicitly NOT acceptable.** Normal force
  accepts $F_n, F_N, N$; tension accepts $F_T, F_s$ (spring), etc.
- $g = 10\ m/s^2$ is used for all numeric problems; students are **not**
  penalized for using $9.8$ or $9.81\ m/s^2$ instead.
- Credit is part-specific and often carries follow-through/error-carried-
  forward credit — a wrong earlier substitution does not zero out a later,
  internally-consistent part (verified directly in a real 5/7-scored Q1
  sample response: wrong height substitution, but full downstream
  qualitative-reasoning credit still earned).

### Unit 1 — Kinematics (10-15%)

**1.1 Scalars and Vectors in One Dimension.** Vectors carry an arrow
($\vec{v}$); vector notation is *not* required for a signed one-dimensional
component ($v_x$) — sign alone conveys direction. Zero explicit exclusion
statements.

**1.2 Displacement/Velocity/Acceleration.** $\Delta x = x - x_0$;
$\vec{v}_{avg} = \Delta\vec{x}/\Delta t$; $\vec{a}_{avg} = \Delta\vec{v}/\Delta t$.
Instantaneous velocity/acceleration are defined as the limit of the average
over a *very small* time interval — **never via a derivative**; no derivative
notation appears anywhere in this course. Zero explicit exclusion statements
(the algebra-based framing is implicit, not stated as an exclusion).

**1.3 Representing Motion.** Constant-acceleration kinematic equations (in
any single dimension, written in $x$):
$$v_x = v_{x0}+a_xt \qquad x=x_0+v_{x0}t+\tfrac12a_xt^2 \qquad v_x^2=v_{x0}^2+2a_x(x-x_0)$$
Graphical relationships: instantaneous velocity = slope of the position-time
tangent; instantaneous acceleration = slope of the velocity-time tangent;
displacement = area under a velocity-time graph; change in velocity = area
under an acceleration-time graph.
**Boundary statement (verbatim):** "AP Physics 1 does not expect students to
quantitatively analyze nonuniform acceleration. However, students will be
expected to be able to qualitatively analyze, sketch appropriate graphs of,
and discuss situations in which acceleration is nonuniform." — i.e. the three
kinematic equations above apply to constant-acceleration cases only; a
nonuniform-acceleration item may only be graphical/qualitative.
**Boundary statement #2 (verbatim):** "For all situations in which a
numerical quantity is required for g, the value g = 10 m/s² will be used.
However, students will not be penalized for correctly using the more precise
commonly accepted values of g = 9.81 m/s² or g = 9.8 m/s²."

**1.4 Reference Frames and Relative Motion.** Acceleration is frame-
independent across all inertial reference frames; velocity is not (it
combines object velocity + observer-frame velocity via vector addition/
subtraction). **Boundary statement (verbatim):** "Unless otherwise stated,
the frame of reference of any problem may be assumed to be inertial. Adding
or subtracting vectors to find relative velocities is restricted to motion
along one dimension for AP Physics 1." — 2-D relative-velocity problems
(e.g. "boat crossing a river" vector addition) are **out of scope**.

**1.5 Vectors and Motion in Two Dimensions.** Standard right-triangle
resolution: $\sin\theta=a/c$, $\cos\theta=b/c$, $\tan\theta=a/b$,
$a^2+b^2=c^2$. Projectile motion = zero acceleration in one dimension +
constant nonzero acceleration in the other; solved by separating into 1-D
kinematics per axis. Zero explicit exclusion statements on this topic itself
(the 1.4 restriction to 1-D relative motion is the only nearby scope cap, not
repeated here).

**Documented misconception (2026 released FRQ, projectile item):**
qualitative reasoning is scored as a *separate, distinct* requirement from
the mathematical derivation — "include qualitative reasoning beyond
mathematical derivations" is an explicit, separately-graded instruction, not
boilerplate. A second 2026 item requires linearizing $v^2$ vs. $d$ data from
a friction-ramp experiment to extract $\mu_k$ from the slope — a real
Experimental-Design-and-Analysis pattern worth mirroring.

### Unit 2 — Force and Translational Dynamics (18-23%)

**2.1 Systems and Center of Mass.** $\bar{x}_{cm} = \Sigma m_i\bar{x}_i /
\Sigma m_i$. **Boundary statement (verbatim):** "AP Physics 1 only expects
students to calculate the center of mass for systems of five or fewer
particles arranged in a two-dimensional configuration or for systems that
are highly symmetrical."

**2.2 Forces and Free-Body Diagrams.** Forces are vectors originating from
the object's center-of-mass dot; a coordinate axis should be chosen parallel
to the acceleration direction. **Boundary statement (verbatim):** "AP
Physics 1 only expects students to depict the forces exerted on objects, not
the force components on free-body diagrams." (Component-decomposed FBDs are
out of scope — draw whole force vectors only.)

**2.3 Newton's Third Law.** $\vec{F}_{A\ on\ B} = -\vec{F}_{B\ on\ A}$;
internal forces don't affect a system's center-of-mass motion. Ideal string:
massless, inextensible, uniform tension throughout; non-ideal (massive)
string: tension may vary along its length. **Boundary statements (verbatim,
two):** (1) "AP Physics 1 only expects students to describe tension
qualitatively in a string, cable, chain, or similar system with mass." (2)
"The interaction between objects or systems at a distance is limited to
gravitational forces in AP Physics 1. In AP Physics 2, gravitational,
electric, and magnetic forces may be considered." — action-at-a-distance
items must be gravity-only.

**2.4 Newton's First Law.** $\Sigma_i\vec{F}_i = 0$ (equilibrium); velocity
constant iff net force zero; forces can be balanced in one dimension and
unbalanced in another. Zero explicit exclusion statements.

**2.5 Newton's Second Law.** $\vec{a}_{sys} = \Sigma\vec{F}/m_{sys} =
\vec{F}_{net}/m_{sys}$. Zero explicit exclusion statements.

**2.6 Gravitational Force.** $|\vec{F}_g| = Gm_1m_2/r^2$ (always attractive,
acts along the center-of-mass line); gravitational field
$|\vec{g}| = |\vec{F}_g|/m = GM/r^2$; weight $=F_g=mg$; near Earth's surface
$g=10\ N/kg$. Apparent weight = normal force magnitude, which is $\ne
F_g$ under acceleration; "weightless" = zero net force or gravity-only net
force. Equivalence principle: an observer in a noninertial frame can't
distinguish apparent weight from a gravitational field. Zero explicit
exclusion statements in this topic.

**2.7 Kinetic and Static Friction.** Kinetic friction is an **equality**:
$|\vec{F}_{f,k}| = |\mu_k\vec{F}_n|$ — independent of contact-surface area.
Static friction is an **inequality**: $|\vec{F}_{f,s}| \le |\mu_s\vec{F}_n|$,
with the slipping threshold as a separate derived equation
$F_{f,s,max} = \mu_sF_n$. $\mu_s$ is "typically" (not universally) greater
than $\mu_k$. Zero explicit exclusion statements on this topic (the only
friction-scope cap is under 2.9, banked curves).

**2.8 Spring Forces.** Ideal spring (massless): Hooke's law
$\vec{F}_s = -k\Delta\vec{x}$, directed toward the spring-system equilibrium
position. Zero explicit exclusion statements.

**2.9 Circular Motion.** $a_c = v^2/r$ (toward center); tangential
acceleration = rate of speed change, tangent to the path; net acceleration =
vector sum of centripetal + tangential. Only **uniform** circular motion
(constant speed) gets period/frequency treatment: $T=1/f$,
$T=2\pi r/v$. Vertical-loop minimum speed (gravity alone supplies centripetal
force at the top): $v=\sqrt{gr}$. Banked curves and conical pendulums:
normal-force/tension components can supply centripetal force. Orbital
period-radius relation (Kepler's third law, circular case only):
$T^2 = (4\pi^2/GM)R^3$. **Boundary statements (verbatim, two):** (1) "AP
Physics 1 only expects students to quantitatively analyze banked curves in
which no friction is required to maintain uniform circular motion. Analysis
of situations in which friction is required on a banked curve is limited to
qualitative descriptions." (2) "AP Physics 1 does not expect students to know
Kepler's first or second laws of planetary motion."

**Documented misconception (2024 Chief Reader Report, Q1 vertical-loop FBD):
only ~50% of students correctly drew the normal force pointing *downward* at
the top of a vertical loop** — the single strongest documented Unit 2 error
in this research pass; students default to "normal force points up" from
flat-surface habit. Other documented errors: drawing an illegitimate extra
"net centripetal force" vector on an FBD (centripetal force is not a
distinct force, it's the net result); labeling non-force quantities
(friction, "KE," "momentum") as if they were forces on an FBD; miscalculating
vertical-loop height as $3R$ instead of $4R$ (loop diameter, not radius, from
bottom to top); believing mechanical energy "decreases" on a frictionless
loop. Gravitation (CED 2.6): documented misuse of $g=F_g/m$ backward,
concluding more planetary mass *lowers* $g$, and confusing mass with weight
across different planets.

### Unit 3 — Work, Energy, and Power (18-23%)

**3.1 Translational Kinetic Energy.** $K=\tfrac12mv^2$ (scalar; frame-
dependent — different observers can measure different $K$ for the same
object). Zero explicit exclusion statements.

**3.2 Work.** Work is scalar, signed. For a **constant** force only:
$W=F_\parallel d = Fd\cos\theta$ — **never presented as a dot product**, and
never given an integral/calculus treatment; the only nod to variable-force
work is graphical: "work is equal to the area under the curve of $F_\parallel$
vs. displacement." Work-energy theorem: $\Delta K=\Sigma_iW_i=\Sigma_iF_{\parallel,i}d$.
Conservative-force work is path-independent, depends only on initial/final
configuration, and is exactly zero over a closed loop; potential energy
exists *only* for conservative forces; nonconservative-force work is
path-dependent (friction, air resistance named as the examples).
**Boundary statement (verbatim):** "AP Physics 1 only expects students to
analyze the transfer of mechanical energy... although students should be
aware that mechanical energy may be dissipated in the form of thermal energy
or sound."

**3.3 Potential Energy.** Zero of PE is an observer's choice, not fixed.
Spring: $U_s=\tfrac12k(\Delta x)^2$. General (inverse-square) gravitational
PE between two spherical masses: $U_g=-Gm_1m_2/r$. Near-surface
**approximation** of that general form (explicitly labeled an approximation,
not an independent formula): $\Delta U_g = mg\Delta y$. Multi-object system
PE = sum over all pairs. Zero explicit exclusion statements.

**3.4 Conservation of Energy.** A single-object system can only have $K$
(never $U$); a system needs internally-conservative interactions (or
reversible shape change) to have both $K$ and $U$. Mechanical energy =
$K+U$; any energy change within a system is balanced by another internal
energy change or a transfer across the system boundary. **Boundary statement
(verbatim, framed as inclusion not exclusion):** "AP Physics 1 expects
students to know that mechanical energy can be dissipated as thermal energy
or sound by nonconservative forces."

**3.5 Power.** $P_{avg}=\Delta E/\Delta t = W/\Delta t$. Instantaneous power
for a **constant** force parallel component: $P_{inst}=F_\parallel v =
Fv\cos\theta$ (explicitly labeled a *derived* equation) — no treatment given
for instantaneous power under a time-varying force/velocity. Zero explicit
exclusion statements.

**Documented misconceptions/scoring patterns (2024 Q1 energy-bar-chart FRQ,
Chief Reader Report + Q1/Q2 Sample Responses):**
- Default 50/50 KE/$U_g$ energy split regardless of the scenario's actual
  height ratio — the most common bar-chart error, despite ~90% of students
  getting *total*-energy conservation conceptually right.
- $\Delta K = \tfrac12m(\Delta v)^2$ used instead of the correct
  $\Delta K=\tfrac12m\,\Delta(v^2)$ — a recurring algebra error, not a
  conceptual one.
- A real 2/7-scored response invoked $W=Fd\cos\theta$ and $P=\Delta E/\Delta t$
  in place of conservation of energy (0 pts on the derivation), mislabeled a
  normal-force arrow as "$F_f$" on a *frictionless* track (an "extraneous
  vector" FBD error, same family as the Unit 2 loop misconception), and lost
  qualitative credit for a bare, unjustified assertion.
- Point-earning is precise and part-specific: "a correct response with no
  supporting work earns this point only" for some points; "the unit and the
  negative sign are not required to earn this point" for others — do not
  assume a blanket units/sign requirement across all criteria.

### Unit 4 — Linear Momentum (10-15%)

**4.1 Linear Momentum.** $\vec{p}=m\vec{v}$; momentum is a vector, same
direction as velocity. Momentum can be used to analyze collisions (a model
where the forces exerted between the objects in the system are much larger
than the net external force during the interaction, so only initial/final
states need be analyzed — the "object model" stays valid) and explosions (a
model where internal forces move objects within the system apart).
**Boundary statement (verbatim):** "Unless otherwise stated, the general
term 'momentum' will refer specifically to linear momentum."

**4.2 Change in Momentum and Impulse.** $\vec{F}_{net}=\Delta\vec{p}/\Delta t$;
impulse $\vec{J}=\vec{F}_{avg}\Delta t$, same direction as net force; impulse
delivered = area under a net-force-vs-time graph; net force = slope of a
momentum-vs-time graph. $\Delta\vec{p}=\vec{p}-\vec{p}_0$; impulse-momentum
theorem $\vec{J}=\vec{F}_{avg}\Delta t=\Delta\vec{p}$; Newton's second law is
a *direct result* of the impulse-momentum theorem for constant-mass systems:
$\vec{F}_{net}=\Delta\vec{p}/\Delta t=m\Delta\vec{v}/\Delta t=m\vec{a}$.
**Boundary statement (verbatim):** "AP Physics 1 does not require students to
quantitatively analyze systems in which the mass of the system changes with
respect to time."

**4.3 Conservation of Linear Momentum.** Center-of-mass velocity for a
collection of objects: $\vec{v}_{cm}=\Sigma\vec{p}_i/\Sigma m_i=
\Sigma m_i\vec{v}_i/\Sigma m_i$, constant absent a net external force. Total
system momentum = sum of constituent momenta. Impulse exerted by one object
on a second is equal and opposite to the impulse the second exerts on the
first (a direct result of Newton's third law); a system may be chosen so its
total momentum is constant; $\vec{J}=\Delta\vec{p}$; conservation of momentum
determines system velocity immediately before/after collisions or
explosions. 4.3.B: momentum is conserved in all interactions; zero net
external force on the selected system $\Rightarrow$ constant total momentum;
nonzero net external force $\Rightarrow$ momentum is transferred between
system and environment. **Boundary statement (verbatim, important —
clarifies that 2-D momentum problems are *not* fully excluded, only capped
in rigor):** "AP Physics 1 includes a quantitative and qualitative treatment
of conservation of momentum in one dimension and a semiquantitative
treatment of conservation of momentum in two dimensions. Exam questions
involving solution of simultaneous equations are not included in AP Physics
1, but the AP Physics 1 Exam may include questions that assess whether
students can set up the equations properly and reason about how changing a
given mass, speed, or angle would affect other quantities. AP Physics 2
includes a full treatment of conservation of momentum in two dimensions for
problems that include one unknown final velocity."

**4.4 Elastic and Inelastic Collisions.** Elastic collision: system KE
before = system KE after (individual objects' KE may still change).
Inelastic collision: total system KE decreases; lost KE is transformed by
nonconservative forces into other energy forms. Perfectly inelastic: objects
stick together and move with the same final velocity. Zero explicit
exclusion statements on this topic.

**Documented misconceptions (2024 Q5 Short Answer, Collisions, mean
2.96/7 — tied for the lowest FRQ mean of 2024; and 2024 Q2 Experimental
Design, mean 7.34/12):**
- Only **~35%** of Q5 responses correctly calculated both objects' post-
  collision speeds *and* represented the resulting slopes correctly on a
  position-vs-time graph, despite **~75%** handling the simpler single-object
  $K\rightarrow v$ conversion correctly — the harder-earned skill is chaining
  momentum conservation into a graphical representation, not the algebra
  itself.
- Only **~25%** of responses correctly identified that a second, stick-
  together scenario was *inelastic* and that the center-of-mass line is
  **identical** across the elastic and inelastic cases (constant regardless
  of collision type, since no external force acts on the two-block system) —
  an invariance point the Chief Reader Report calls out as under-recognized.
- Common wrong pattern: drawing Object A's post-collision position line flat
  (implying it stops) or with negative slope (implying it reverses), rather
  than a reduced positive slope.
- A specific, quantified conceptual error from Q2: many responses treated
  instantaneous momentum as $p=Ft$ (a net force times elapsed time from some
  origin) rather than the correct incremental relationship
  $\Delta p=F_{avg}\Delta t$ — reported verbatim example: "$p=Ft=(0.8\ N)
  (2.0\ s)=1.6\ N\cdot s$" offered as "the momentum at $t=2.0\ s$."
- Real graded exemplar (2024 Q2 Sample 2A, scored near-full-credit): reasoned
  that $\Delta p=0$ over $t=0.5$–$2.5\ s$ for an oscillating cart-spring
  system because the areas under the $F$-vs-$t$ graph from $0.5$–$1.5\ s$
  and $1.5$–$2.5\ s$ are equal and opposite — a distinct area-cancellation
  pattern for oscillating (rather than one-directional) impulse problems,
  worth mirroring in item design.
- 2026 released FRQ (Question 1, two-disk collision) confirms the momentum-
  vector-diagram convention already documented for force diagrams applies to
  momentum vectors too: "Each arrow must start on, and point away from, each
  dot" — and separately asks students to compare $\Delta p_R$ vs. $\Delta p_S$
  during the same collision via Newton's third law, the same
  equal-and-opposite-impulse pattern the Chief Reader Report flags as
  under-recognized above.

### Unit 5 — Torque and Rotational Dynamics (10-15%)

**5.1 Rotational Kinematics.** $\Delta\theta=\theta-\theta_0$ (radians). A
rigid system holds its shape but different points move differently during
rotation — it *cannot* be modeled as a single object, except when rotation
about an axis is well-described by the motion of the system's center of mass
(CED's own example: Earth's axial rotation is negligible when considering
Earth's revolution about the Earth-Sun system's center of mass — one object
can validly be treated as a point for one motion and an extended rigid body
for another, depending on which axis is being analyzed). CW/CCW convention:
one direction is "typically" (not universally) positive, the other negative.
$\omega_{avg}=\Delta\theta/\Delta t$; $\alpha_{avg}=\Delta\omega/\Delta t$.
Constant-angular-acceleration equations — exact rotational analogs of the
Unit 1 kinematics equations:
$$\omega=\omega_0+\alpha t \qquad \theta=\theta_0+\omega_0t+\tfrac12\alpha t^2 \qquad \omega^2=\omega_0^2+2\alpha(\theta-\theta_0)$$
Graphical relationships mirror the linear-kinematics ones. **Boundary
statement (verbatim, applies to both 5.1 and 5.2):** "Descriptions of the
directions of rotation for a point or object are limited to clockwise and
counterclockwise with respect to a given axis of rotation."

**5.2 Connecting Linear and Rotational Motion.** $\Delta s=r\Delta\theta$;
$v=r\omega$; $a_T=r\alpha$ (tangential acceleration only). All points within
a rigid system share the same $\omega$ and $\alpha$. Same CW/CCW boundary
statement as 5.1.

**5.3 Torque.** Torque results *only* from the force component perpendicular
to the position vector from the axis of rotation to the point of force
application: $\tau=rF_\perp=rF\sin\theta$. Lever arm = perpendicular distance
from the axis to the force's line of action. Force diagrams are analogous to
free-body diagrams but additionally depict the location at which each force
is exerted relative to the axis of rotation. **Boundary statement
(verbatim):** "While AP Physics 1 expects students to mathematically
manipulate the magnitude of torque using vector conventions, the direction
of torque is beyond the scope of the course."

**5.4 Rotational Inertia.** Point-mass/particle-at-radius case:
$I=mr^2$; total for a collection: $I_{tot}=\Sigma I_i=\Sigma m_ir_i^2$. A
rigid system's rotational inertia in a given plane is minimized when the
axis passes through the system's center of mass. Parallel axis theorem:
$I'=I_{cm}+Md^2$. **Boundary statement (verbatim, two-part):** "AP Physics 1
only expects students to calculate the rotational inertia for systems of
five or fewer objects arranged in a two-dimensional configuration. Students
do not need to know the rotational inertia of extended rigid systems, as
these will be provided within the exam. Students should have a qualitative
understanding of the factors that affect rotational inertia; for example,
how rotational inertia is greater when mass is farther from the axis of
rotation, which is why a hoop has more rotational inertia than a solid disk
of the same mass and radius."

**5.5 Rotational Equilibrium and Newton's First Law in Rotational Form.** A
system may exhibit rotational equilibrium (constant angular velocity)
*without* being in translational equilibrium, and vice versa. $\Sigma\tau_i=0$
defines rotational equilibrium. Rotational analog of Newton's first law:
constant angular velocity iff net torque is zero. Rotational corollary to
Newton's second law: unbalanced torques mean angular velocity must be
changing. **Boundary statement (verbatim):** "AP Physics 1 does not expect
students to simultaneously analyze rotation in multiple planes."

**5.6 Newton's Second Law in Rotational Form.**
$\alpha_{sys}=\Sigma\tau/I_{sys}=\tau_{net}/I_{sys}$. To fully describe a
rotating rigid system, linear and rotational analyses may need to be
performed independently. Zero explicit exclusion statements on this topic.

**Documented misconceptions (2024 Question 3, Qualitative/Quantitative
Translation, "Equilibrium," mean 5.04/12 — one of the two lowest FRQ means of
2024, alongside Q4):**
- **~70%** of responses correctly drew both the gravitational and tension
  force directions on a beam free-body diagram, and **~70%** correctly showed
  the beam in *translational* equilibrium — but the forces resulted in
  correctly-drawn *rotational* equilibrium (hinge force starting on and
  extending away from the correct point) in **fewer than 20%** of responses.
  This is the single largest documented gap in this Unit 5 pass: translational-
  equilibrium fluency does not transfer to rotational-equilibrium fluency.
  Common failure: drawing the hinge force as a horizontal vector, which
  cannot balance torque about the hinge.
- **~20%** of responses had extraneous forces (e.g., an illegitimate normal
  force), drew only tension *components* rather than the whole force vector,
  or omitted vector labels — the same "extraneous/component vector on a
  force diagram" failure mode documented for Unit 2 FBDs in Section 5 above,
  now confirmed to recur on rotational force diagrams.
- On the qualitative tension-comparison part (b), **~70%** correctly selected
  $F_{T2}>F_{T1}$, but only **~30%** grounded it in the vertical tension
  component staying constant (or equivalently, the torque requirement) —
  most instead reached for a vaguer "shorter string $\Rightarrow$ greater
  tension" heuristic without the underlying mechanism.
- Only **~50%** of responses executed the required multi-step derivation
  ($\Sigma\tau=I\alpha$, hinge chosen as axis, $\Sigma\tau=0$) even though most
  could *identify* Newton's second law in rotational form on the reference
  sheet — a large share defaulted to Newton's second law in *translational*
  form instead, setting the tension's perpendicular component equal to the
  beam's weight while ignoring the hinge's vertical force component.
- On the angular-speed-vs-time sketch: **~90%** correctly indicated angular
  speed continuously increases after a string is cut, but only **~20%**
  correctly showed the *decreasing rate* of that increase (concave-down
  shape) — **~80%** instead drew a straight (constant-slope) line, defaulting
  to constant angular acceleration even when torque (and hence angular
  acceleration) is decreasing as the system rotates.
- 2026 released FRQ (Question 4, "Toys X and Y" string-and-axle problem)
  confirms this exact archetype recurs: qualitative prediction ($\omega_Y$
  vs. $\omega_X$) $\rightarrow$ multi-step derivation from the work-energy
  theorem or Newton's second law in rotational form $\rightarrow$ "justify
  how your derived equation is or is not consistent with your reasoning" —
  the same predict/derive/reconcile structure as Q3 2024, now confirmed
  stable across two exam administrations for Unit 5/6 content.

### Unit 6 — Energy and Momentum of Rotating Systems (5-8%)

**6.1 Rotational Kinetic Energy.** $K=\tfrac12I\omega^2$; this can be shown
equivalent to an object's translational kinetic energy (i.e., it *is* the
object's total KE when treated as a point). Total KE of a rigid system =
rotational KE about its center of mass + translational KE of the center of
mass. A rigid system can have rotational KE while its center of mass is at
rest, because individual points within it still have linear speed.
Rotational KE is a scalar. Zero explicit exclusion statements.

**6.2 Torque and Work.** A torque transfers energy into or out of a system
when exerted over an angular displacement: $W=\tau\Delta\theta$. Work done by
a torque can also be found from the area under a torque-vs-angular-position
graph. Zero explicit exclusion statements.

**6.3 Angular Momentum and Angular Impulse.** Magnitude about a specific
axis: $L=I\omega$. Magnitude about a given point: $L=rmv\sin\theta$ — axis/
point selection affects the determined value; the measured angular momentum
of an object traveling in a straight line depends on the distance to the
reference point, the object's mass and speed, and the angle between the
radial distance and the velocity. Angular impulse = $\tau\Delta t$, same
direction as the torque, and equals the area under a torque-vs-time graph.
$\Delta L=L-L_0$; rotational impulse-momentum theorem: angular impulse
$=\tau\Delta t=\Delta L$, a direct result of the rotational form of Newton's
second law for constant-$I$ systems: $\tau_{net}=\Delta L/\Delta t=
I\Delta\omega/\Delta t=I\alpha$. Net torque = slope of an $L$-vs-time graph;
angular impulse = area under a torque-vs-time graph. **Boundary statement
(verbatim):** "While AP Physics 1 expects that students can mathematically
manipulate the magnitude of angular momentum using one-dimensional vector
conventions, the direction of angular momentum and angular impulse is
beyond the scope of the course." — the same magnitude-only-vector treatment
already documented for torque in 5.3, now confirmed to extend to angular
momentum and angular impulse as a standing pattern across both units.

**6.4 Conservation of Angular Momentum.** Total angular momentum about a
rotational axis = sum of the constituent parts' angular momenta about that
axis. Any change to a system's angular momentum must come from an
interaction with its surroundings. The angular impulse one object/system
exerts on a second is equal and opposite to the impulse the second exerts on
the first (Newton's third law consequence). A system may be chosen so its
total angular momentum is constant. **Key nonrigid-system principle:** the
angular speed of a *nonrigid* system may change **without** its angular
momentum changing, if the system changes shape by moving mass closer to or
farther from the rotational axis (the ice-skater-pulling-arms-in mechanism).
6.4.B: angular momentum is conserved in all interactions; zero net external
torque on the selected system $\Rightarrow$ constant total angular momentum;
nonzero $\Rightarrow$ angular momentum is transferred between system and
environment. Zero explicit exclusion statements on 6.4 itself (the relevant
boundary statement is filed under 6.3 above).

**6.5 Rolling.** $K_{tot}=K_{trans}+K_{rot}$. Rolling *without* slipping:
$\Delta x_{cm}=r\Delta\theta$, $v_{cm}=r\omega$, $a_{cm}=r\alpha$; for ideal
cases, rolling without slipping means the frictional force dissipates **no**
energy from the system. When slipping, the system's center-of-mass motion
and its rotational motion **cannot** be directly related, and because the
point of application of the kinetic friction force moves with respect to the
surface, kinetic friction *does* dissipate energy from a slipping system.
**Boundary statements (verbatim, two):** (1) "Rolling friction is beyond the
scope of AP Physics 1." (2) "The precise mathematical relationships between
linear and angular quantities while a rigid body is rolling while slipping
are beyond the scope of AP Physics 1 and 2, and students will not be
expected to model those relationships quantitatively. However, students are
expected to qualitatively explain the changes to linear and angular
quantities while a rigid body is rolling while slipping."

**6.6 Motion of Orbiting Satellites.** In a system of a massive central
object and an orbiting satellite whose mass is negligible relative to the
central object's, the central object's own motion is negligible. In
**circular** orbits: the system's total mechanical energy, the system's
gravitational PE, **and** the satellite's angular momentum and kinetic
energy are all constant. In **elliptical** orbits: the system's total
mechanical energy and the satellite's angular momentum are constant, but the
system's gravitational PE and the satellite's KE can each change. Zero of
gravitational PE is defined at infinite separation: $U_g=-Gm_1m_2/r$.
Escape velocity is the speed at which the satellite-central-object system's
mechanical energy equals zero (satellite asymptotically reaches zero speed
at infinite distance); derived via conservation of energy:
$v_{esc}=\sqrt{2GM/r}$. Zero explicit exclusion statements on this topic
itself (the CED's Kepler's-first/second-law exclusion is filed under 2.9 in
Section 5 above, not repeated here).

KE, torque-and-work, angular momentum/impulse, rolling) in isolation — the

### Unit 7 — Oscillations (5-8%)

**7.1 Defining Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM).** SHM is a special case of
periodic motion. SHM results when the magnitude of the restoring force
exerted on an object is proportional to that object's displacement from
equilibrium — derived equation: $ma_x=-k\Delta x$. A restoring force acts
opposite the object's displacement from equilibrium; an equilibrium position
is where net force is zero. A pendulum's motion with **small** angular
displacement can be modeled as SHM because the restoring *torque* is
proportional to the angular displacement.

**7.2 Frequency and Period of SHM.** $T=1/f$. Spring-object oscillator:
$T_s=2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$. Simple pendulum at small angle:
$T_p=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}$.

**7.3 Representing and Analyzing SHM.** Displacement from equilibrium:
$x=A\cos(2\pi ft)$ or $x=A\sin(2\pi ft)$. Minima, maxima, and zeros of
displacement, velocity, and acceleration are all features of harmonic
motion, and recognizing their positions/times aids qualitative description.
Changing the **amplitude** of an SHM system does **not** change its period.
Properties of SHM can be determined and analyzed graphically.

**7.4 Energy of Simple Harmonic Oscillators.** $E_{total}=U+K$; conservation
of energy $\Rightarrow$ $E_{total}$ is constant. $K$ is maximum when $U$ is
minimum and vice versa; minimum $K=0$. Changing the amplitude changes the
system's maximum PE and therefore its total energy. For a spring-object
system specifically: $E_{total}=\tfrac12kA^2$.

**Notable finding — zero explicit exclusion statements anywhere in Unit 7.**
Every CED page for topics 7.1 through 7.4 was checked individually (pages
124-127); none contains a "Boundary Statement" callout box. This is a
genuine primary-source finding, not an oversight: Unit 7 is the only unit in
Physics 1 (of the eight checked across both sessions today) with no CED-
stated scope caps at all.

**Documented misconceptions (2024 Question 2, Experimental Design,
"Dynamics," mean 7.34/12; and Question 4, Paragraph, "Oscillations,
Gravitation," mean 3.72/7 — the lowest FRQ mean of the entire 2024 exam):**
- **~75%** of Q2 responses designed a valid oscillating-mass experiment with
  the limited kit provided, but a notable share instead reached for a static
  Hooke's-law measurement despite the prompt restricting equipment to "only"
  a stopwatch and digital scale — an overlearned default to force-based
  spring-constant measurement that persists even against an explicit
  instruction not to use it.
- **Recurring, independently-documented period-measurement error:** multiple
  responses defined "period" as the time to travel from the top to the
  bottom of an oscillation (i.e., a half-cycle) rather than a full cycle
  back to the same point moving the same direction. The Chief Reader Report
  flags this same error twice, independently, in two different response
  excerpts for the same question — a strong, repeated signal.
- On the force-time $\rightarrow$ momentum-estimation task, many test-takers
  struggled to estimate $\Delta p$ from the *area under* a force-vs-time
  graph even though most could compute $\Delta p$ directly from a velocity-
  time graph — the harder-earned skill is graph-integration reasoning, not
  the momentum concept itself (see also the Unit 4 $p=Ft$ misconception
  above, drawn from the same question).
- **Q4 had the single lowest mean of any 2024 Physics 1 FRQ (3.72/7).** Most
  responses correctly reasoned that gravitational force is weaker on a
  larger-radius planet and correctly cited $T=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}$, but a
  significant share described "gravity" imprecisely instead of distinguishing
  gravitational force / acceleration / field by name or symbol, and many
  addressed only **one** of the two competing effects in play (the change in
  $g$ vs. the change in effective pendulum length from spring stretch)
  rather than reconciling both — a documented pattern of defaulting to
  single-variable reasoning even when a claim requires balancing two
  opposing dependencies.
- A small number of Q4 responses incorrectly imported the spring-mass period
  equation $T=2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$ to justify the *pendulum's* period change — a
  plausible wrong-answer/distractor pattern worth reusing when writing MC
  options for this topic.

### Unit 8 — Fluids (10-15%)

**8.1 Internal Structure and Density.** Distinguishing properties of solids,
liquids, and gases stem from the varying interactions between their
constituent atoms and molecules. A fluid is a substance with no fixed shape.
Density: $\rho=m/V$. An **ideal fluid** is defined by two properties
together: it is incompressible **and** has no viscosity. Zero explicit
exclusion statements on this topic itself (the ideal-fluid/full-pipe
boundary statement is filed under 8.4 below, since it applies globally).

**8.2 Pressure.** $P=F_\perp/A$ (perpendicular force component per unit
area). Pressure is a **scalar** quantity. The volume and density of an
incompressible fluid are constant regardless of the pressure exerted on it.
The pressure exerted by a fluid results from the entirety of the
interactions between the fluid's constituent particles and the surface they
contact. Absolute pressure at a point: $P=P_0+\rho gh$, where $P_0$ is a
reference pressure (e.g. atmospheric, $P_{atm}$). Gauge pressure of a
vertical fluid column: $P_{gauge}=\rho gh$. Zero explicit exclusion
statements.

**8.3 Fluids and Newton's Laws.** Newton's laws describe the motion of
particles within a fluid; the macroscopic behavior of a fluid results from
the internal interactions between its constituent particles and any external
forces exerted on it. The **buoyant force** is a net upward force exerted on
an object by a fluid, resulting from the collective forces the fluid's
particles exert on the object. Its magnitude equals the weight of fluid
displaced by the object (Archimedes' principle, though the CED text itself
never uses that name): $F_b=\rho Vg$. Zero explicit exclusion statements.

**8.4 Fluids and Conservation Laws.** A pressure difference between two
locations causes a fluid to flow. For an incompressible fluid in a tube open
at both ends, the rate at which matter enters equals the rate it exits;
matter's flow rate into a location is proportional to the cross-sectional
area of the flow and the fluid's speed there — derived equation: $V/t=Av$.
Continuity equation (conservation of mass flow rate for incompressible
fluids): $A_1v_1=A_2v_2$. A gravitational-PE difference between two locations
in a fluid results in a difference in KE and pressure between those
locations, described by conservation laws. Bernoulli's equation (conservation
of mechanical energy in fluid flow):
$$P_1+\rho gy_1+\tfrac12\rho v_1^2=P_2+\rho gy_2+\tfrac12\rho v_2^2$$
Torricelli's theorem (speed of fluid exiting an opening, derivable from
conservation of energy): $v=\sqrt{2g\Delta y}$. **Boundary statement
(verbatim):** "All fluids will be assumed to be ideal, and all pipes are
assumed to be completely filled by the fluid, unless otherwise stated."

### Cross-unit scoring conventions (Units 4-8 additions to Section 5's list)

- **Functional-dependence points can be earned even when applied
  incorrectly**, as long as an attempt is made to connect it to prior
  reasoning — 2024 Q3 scoring note, verbatim: "The functional dependance
  does not need to be used correctly to earn this point." This is a distinct,
  explicit convention not covered by the Units 1-3 pass and should inform how
  strictly "connect your claim to your derivation"-style rubric items are
  graded/authored.
- **Vector-diagram convention extends beyond force diagrams to momentum
  vectors.** The Units 1-3 FBD convention ("each force is a distinct arrow
  starting on, and pointing away from, the object/system dot") is restated
  verbatim for momentum-vector diagrams in the 2026 released FRQ: "Each
  arrow must start on, and point away from, each dot." Treat this as a
  general Physics 1 vector-diagram convention, not FBD-specific.
- **Torque and angular momentum are both magnitude-only vector quantities in
  this course.** AP Physics 1 expects students to mathematically manipulate
  the *magnitude* of both torque (5.3 boundary statement) and angular
  momentum/angular impulse (6.3 boundary statement) using one-dimensional
  (signed, CW/CCW) conventions; true vector direction (e.g., right-hand-rule
  cross products) is explicitly out of scope for both quantities — a
  consistent pattern across Units 5 and 6, not an isolated exclusion.
- **Multi-feature graphs are scored in separate point buckets per feature.**
  The 2024 Q3(e) angular-speed-vs-time sketch awards one point for
  "monotonically increasing" and a separate point for "concave down" —
  confirming, with a rotational-motion example, the same piecewise
  graph-scoring pattern already documented for Units 1-3 (e.g., the vertical-
  loop energy bar chart).

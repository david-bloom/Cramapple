Estimators

Core idea

p̂ is the point estimator of the population proportion p. Unbiasedness is a property of p̂'s sampling distribution across every possible sample of size n, not a property of any single sample's result. An unbiased estimator's distribution has mean exactly p (μp̂ = p).

What to understand

Bias describes long-run behaviour over repeated sampling. A single sample landing far from the true p is completely consistent with p̂ being unbiased. Bias comes from a sampling method that systematically pulls one way.

How points are earned

Name p̂ as the estimator of the population parameter p. Explain unbiasedness as the estimator's average behaviour across repeated sampling, not any one sample's result. Keep sampling variability separate from bias.

How points are lost

Calling one sample result biased because it is not exactly equal to the parameter. Explaining bias with a single sample instead of the sampling distribution.

The answer move

Name the quantity, structure, condition or representation the prompt is really asking about. Identify the statistic as an estimator of a population parameter. Explain unbiasedness as no systematic over- or underestimation. Then do the arithmetic.

Full deep dive

Core idea

p̂ is the point estimator of the population proportion p, and unbiasedness is a property of the sampling distribution of p̂ across every possible sample of size n, not a property of any single sample's result. An unbiased estimator's sampling distribution has mean exactly equal to p (μp̂ = p, formalised in 3.2), even though almost every individual sample's p̂ will land somewhat above or below p by chance.

What students need to understand

Because bias describes the estimator's long-run behaviour over repeated sampling, a single sample landing far from the true p is completely consistent with p̂ being unbiased. What would make p̂ biased is a sampling method that systematically pulls results in one direction — for example, a convenience sample that over-represents one group — not ordinary sample-to-sample variability.

How this becomes points

Points require correctly naming p̂ as the estimator of the population parameter p, explaining unbiasedness in terms of the estimator's average behaviour across repeated sampling rather than any one sample's result, and not mistaking sampling variability (a single result differing from p) for bias (a systematic direction to that difference).

Answer move

First name the quantity, structure, condition, or representation the prompt is really asking about; then identify the statistic as an estimator of a population parameter and explain unbiasedness as no systematic over- or underestimation before doing arithmetic or writing the final sentence.

Mini example

A citywide poll of n = 50 randomly selected voters finds p̂ = 0.58 support for a ballot measure. The true (but unknown to the pollster) population support is later revealed to be p = 0.52. A student is asked whether this poll's estimator was biased.

Weak answer

Since the poll's result of 0.58 doesn't match the true value of 0.52, the estimator p̂ must be biased.

Point-attaining answer

p̂ is an unbiased estimator of p regardless of this one result, because unbiasedness describes the mean of p̂'s sampling distribution across every possible random sample of size 50, which equals p = 0.52 (μp̂ = p) — not whether any single sample happens to land exactly on p. This particular sample's p̂ = 0.58 differing from p = 0.52 by 0.06 reflects ordinary sampling variability, not bias; bias would require the sampling method itself to systematically favour one outcome.

Common point loss

Calling one sample result biased just because it is not exactly equal to the parameter.

Open practice and, for any biased-versus-unbiased question, ask whether the described issue is about one sample's distance from p (not bias) or the sampling method's systematic direction (bias).

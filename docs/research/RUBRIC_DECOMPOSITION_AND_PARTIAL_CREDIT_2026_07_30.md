# Rubric decomposition and partial-credit behaviour on real content

**Date:** 2026-07-30
**Question:** does the real Cramapple bank contain the rubric defect found in a synthetic
rubric, and does it degrade partial-credit grading?
**Verdict:** **No, and no.** One hypothesis killed, two clean nulls. The practical consequence is
that a large content project (authoring point decompositions for 455 criteria) is **not**
justified by evidence.
**Cost:** ~$0.35 across ~150 model calls.
**Ground truth:** none required — both step-2 measurements are self-referential (stability, and
ordering across content levels), which is what makes them trustworthy despite no adjudicated gold.

---

## Step 1 — bank-wide check

### 1a. The synthetic defect does NOT exist in the real bank

Working against a rubric I wrote myself, I found two criteria that required the same fact in
different words ("the rate holds steady" vs "the curve flattens"), and proposed that this
ambiguity explained observed grader instability.

**It does not generalise.** Real Cramapple criteria are keyed `a`/`b`/`c`/`d` or
`part-a-criterion-1` and are **partitioned one per question part**. Each criterion grades a
different sub-question, so a student cannot accidentally satisfy criterion (c) while answering
part (a). Cross-criterion semantic collision is structurally prevented by the authoring format.

Lexical corroboration across all 1,028 within-item criterion pairs in multi-point items: mean word
overlap **7.9%**, only 12 pairs above 30%.

**My synthetic rubric was unrepresentative in exactly the way that produced the defect.** Any
finding derived from it about criterion boundaries should be discarded.

### 1b. A different, real gap: 74% of multi-point criteria never state how points divide

| | n | share |
|---|---:|---:|
| multi-point criteria (>1 point) | **617** | |
| ...with explicit point decomposition (`1pt: X. 1pt: Y.`) | 126 | 20% |
| ...with a numbered element list `(1)…(2)…` | 47 | 8% |
| **...with any decomposition** | **162** | **26%** |
| **...with NO stated decomposition** | **455** | **74%** |

Decomposed, from `apcalcbc-frq-001`:
> `1pt: rewrites as 3*sin(3x)/(3x). 1pt: applies the standard limit sin(theta)/theta -> 1. 1pt: evaluates the limit to 3.`

Undecomposed, from `APBIO-FRQ-L-041` part c, also worth 3 points:
> `Diversity and evenness increase from Plot A to Plot C as succession proceeds; explanation connects community assembly and changing habitat or species interactions to the trend.`

For the 74%, a grader awarding partial credit must **invent** the decomposition. The obvious
prediction is that this produces unstable or undiscriminating partial awards. Step 2 tested that.

**Incidental confirmation:** the 26% that do decompose write explicit per-point splits, which is
independent evidence from the content authors that partial credit is intended behaviour — the
owner decision of 2026-07-28 matches how the bank is already written.

## Step 2 — does the decomposition gap actually matter?

Real published 3-point criteria, 5 decomposed and 5 not. Answers generated by
`google/gemini-2.5-flash`; grading by `gpt-4.1-mini` on the per-criterion path so nothing else on
the item can influence the award.

### 2a. Award stability — null

Same answer, 3 identical repeat calls:

| | n | unstable | mean spread (of 3 pts) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| decomposed | 9 | 1 | 0.11 |
| NOT decomposed | 8 | **0** | **0.00** |

**16 of 17 answer-instances scored identically across repeats**, and the single unstable case was
a *decomposed* criterion. Instability is not the mechanism, and it is not worth chasing.

*Design note:* this run also had a confound — every answer was generated to be uniformly "roughly
half", so uniform awards could not have distinguished good discrimination from none. That is what
2b was built to test.

### 2b. Discrimination across content levels — null

Three answers per criterion, generated to address ~1/3, ~2/3 and all of what the prompt asks:

| | n | monotone | mean range (0–3) | full answer scored 3/3 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| decomposed | 5 | 4 | 1.80 | 4/5 |
| NOT decomposed | 5 | 4 | **2.00** | 3/5 |

**No meaningful difference.** Both groups: 8/10 monotone, mean range ~1.9 of 3 points. Partial
credit tracks how much the student actually did, whether or not the rubric says how to split the
points.

### 2c. Two things worth noting that are NOT about decomposition

- **2 of 10 criteria scored non-monotonically** — a fuller answer awarded fewer points than a
  thinner one (`D4`: 2→1→2, `U4`: 0→2→1). ~20% ordering violations, spread across both groups.
- **A complete answer received full marks in only 7 of 10 cases.** On real content the grader
  leans *under*-credit, which is the opposite direction from the over-crediting seen on my
  synthetic rubric — further evidence the synthetic instrument was misleading.

---

## What this changes

1. **Do not author decompositions for the 455 undecomposed criteria.** It is a large content
   project and the measured benefit is zero on both stability and discrimination. Revisit only if
   a real error corpus later implicates them.
2. **Discard synthetic-rubric findings about criterion boundaries.** The instrument was
   unrepresentative. This includes the C3 over-crediting that motivated the whole line of
   investigation — including its use as evidence against Arm A, which should be re-measured on
   real criteria before the arm decision is settled.
3. **Partial credit works on real content.** 8/10 monotone with a ~1.9/3 range, high stability.
   This strengthens the case for deploying it.
4. **The real open quality issues are the two in 2c** — ordering violations and under-crediting of
   complete answers — neither of which is a boundary or decomposition problem, and both of which
   need a real error corpus to size.

## Method note worth keeping

Both step-2 designs measure the grader **against itself** (repeat agreement; ordering across
constructed content levels) rather than against labels. That is why they are trustworthy at n=10
with no adjudicated gold: there is no ground truth to get wrong. Where an experiment can be framed
self-referentially, it should be — it removes the labelling bottleneck that has limited every other
measurement in this workstream.

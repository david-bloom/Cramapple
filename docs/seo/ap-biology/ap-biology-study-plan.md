# AP Biology Study Plan: 3-Month, 1-Month, and 10-Day Schedules

The most common AP Biology study mistake is not spending too little time — it's spending time on the wrong things. This page gives you three plans calibrated to how much time you have before exam day. Each plan prioritizes by exam weight and learning efficiency, not alphabetical unit order.

## How to Choose Your Plan

| Time Before Exam | Use This Plan |
|---|---|
| 10+ weeks out | 3-Month Plan |
| 3–9 weeks out | 1-Month Plan |
| Under 2 weeks | 10-Day Plan |

Before starting any plan: identify which two units gave you the most trouble in class or on a recent practice test. That's your triage list — those units come first inside each plan, regardless of the default order shown.

## 3-Month Plan (12 Weeks)

**Who this is for:** Students starting in January or February, or anyone who wants to cover all 8 units with time remaining for serious FRQ practice.

**The core principle:** Content first, then application. The first 8 weeks build knowledge unit by unit. The final 4 weeks convert that knowledge into points through scored FRQ practice and timed exams.

### Weeks 1–2: The Three Heaviest Units

Start with Units 7, 6, and 3 — together they account for 37–52% of the exam.

| Unit | Exam Weight | Why First |
|---|---|---|
| Unit 7: Natural Selection | 13–20% | Highest single-unit weight. Hardy-Weinberg math requires repeated practice and should start early. |
| Unit 6: Gene Expression and Regulation | 12–16% | Transcription, translation, and regulation are conceptually dense and need time to consolidate. |
| Unit 3: Cellular Energetics | 12–16% | Electron transport chain and ATP synthesis are the most frequently missed FRQ concepts. |

**What to do each week:**
- Read the College Board CED learning objectives for the unit before anything else — they define exactly what the exam tests.
- Take notes on the mechanism, not just the vocabulary. The exam asks why, not what.
- Do 5 official MCQs from past AP exams (available at AP Central) for each unit.

**Why this order matters:** Students who spend the first month on Units 1 and 2 because "they're the start of the course" spend their highest-energy study weeks on 8–11% of the exam. Start where the points are.

### Weeks 3–4: The Mid-Weight Units

Cover Units 2, 4, and 8.

| Unit | Exam Weight | Key Concepts |
|---|---|---|
| Unit 2: Cell Structure and Function | 10–13% | Membrane transport (osmosis, active transport), endomembrane system, cell-cell communication |
| Unit 4: Cell Communication and Cell Cycle | 10–15% | Signal transduction pathways, mitosis vs. meiosis, feedback mechanisms |
| Unit 8: Ecology | 10–15% | Energy flow, biogeochemical cycles (nitrogen, carbon, phosphorus — newly emphasized in 2025-26 CED), population dynamics |

**Why Units 2 and 4 together:** They share conceptual overlap around membrane function and receptor-based signaling. Studying them back-to-back reinforces both.

**Note on Unit 8:** Biogeochemical cycles were added to Topic 8.2 in the 2025-26 Course and Exam Description. If you're using prep materials from before fall 2025, verify they cover this content.

### Weeks 5–6: Fill the Gaps (Units 1 and 5)

| Unit | Exam Weight | Focus Areas |
|---|---|---|
| Unit 1: Chemistry of Life | 8–11% | Macromolecule structure-function relationships; enzyme kinetics; properties of water |
| Unit 5: Heredity | 8–11% | Non-Mendelian patterns, sex-linkage, pedigree interpretation, meiotic errors |

**These units are lower weight but high partial-credit opportunities.** Pedigree questions and macromolecule structure-function questions appear consistently on the FRQ section and are often worth 1–2 standalone points that require no extended writing.

### Weeks 7–8: Targeted Repair

Return to whichever two units felt hardest in Weeks 1–6. This is not a passive review — it's active repair.

**How to do it:** Take a 10-question MCQ set for that unit. For every question you miss, write one sentence explaining why the correct answer is correct. That's active learning. Re-reading notes is not.

### Weeks 9–10: FRQ Practice

Do 3 complete short FRQs per week under timed conditions (10–12 minutes each). Score yourself against official scoring guidelines from AP Central. Track which criterion type you consistently miss:

- **Mechanism gaps:** You describe what happened, but not why.
- **Control gaps:** You design an experiment but omit the control group or fail to hold variables constant.
- **Data connection gaps:** You draw a conclusion without referencing the data in the prompt.

**Why FRQ practice at week 9, not week 1?** Because you need enough content knowledge to recognize what a strong answer requires. Starting FRQs too early leads to writing practice, not scoring practice. The goal in this phase is learning to see the rubric, not learning the biology.

### Weeks 11–12: Full Practice Exams + Targeted Review

Take one full timed practice exam (3 hours, both sections) each week.

After each exam:
1. Score the MCQ section using the College Board answer key.
2. Self-score the FRQ section against published rubrics.
3. For every FRQ point missed, write a 1-sentence explanation of the gap.
4. Spend 60–90 minutes on targeted review of your weakest area.

**Rule:** Never take more practice exams than you score. Untimed, unscored practice exams don't improve your score — they just take time.

## 1-Month Plan (4 Weeks)

**Who this is for:** Students with 3–9 weeks before the exam who need an efficient plan that doesn't waste time on low-return content.

**The core principle:** Significantly reduce time on low-weight units. Units 1, 5, and 8 get less coverage here. The goal is maximum expected point gain given the time you have.

### Week 1: Units 7, 6, and 3 Only

These three units represent roughly 40–50% of the exam. Competence here means you can pass.

**Daily structure:**
- 45 minutes of content review (reading, notes, or video)
- 10 MCQs from past exams on that unit
- 1 short FRQ — even if you don't know enough to write a strong one yet. The failure teaches you what you need to focus on.

**Why attempt FRQs you'll fail?** Doing a weak FRQ at the start of your prep and then reading the scoring guideline accelerates learning faster than reading content first. The gap between what you wrote and what the rubric wanted is the most efficient possible study signal.

### Week 2: Units 2, 4, and 8

Hit the concepts that bridge cellular biology and the organism level.

**Key focus by unit:**
- Unit 2: Membrane transport — know which mechanisms require energy and why; osmosis direction from solute concentration
- Unit 4: Signal transduction — know the three stages: reception, transduction, response; know at least one complete pathway end-to-end
- Unit 8: Biogeochemical cycles — nitrogen, carbon, and phosphorus cycles are newly emphasized in 2025-26 and frequently tested

**Skim Units 1 and 5.** Read the CED learning objectives for each, do 5 MCQs per unit to identify gaps, and move on. Don't invest study time in units with the lowest exam weight when higher-weight units still need reinforcement.

### Week 3: FRQs Every Day

Write one complete short FRQ (4 points) every day under timed conditions. By Friday you'll have done 5 scored FRQs. Score each one against official rubrics.

**The most important observation to make:** Which criterion type do you consistently miss? Once you identify the pattern — mechanism explanations, experimental design requirements, data-to-conclusion connections — that becomes your targeted daily drill.

If you miss every mechanism question, spend 15 minutes per day practicing the cause → mechanism → effect structure before writing anything. Get the structure right without the content pressure, then add the content.

### Week 4: One Full Exam + Targeted Repair

Take one timed full exam. Score it. Spend the final 3 days drilling your lowest-scoring unit using past FRQs and MCQs.

**Stop studying new content by Wednesday before the exam.** Introducing unfamiliar material in the final week creates confusion, not confidence. Consolidation of what you already know beats acquisition of new content at this stage.

## 10-Day Emergency Plan

**Who this is for:** The exam is under 2 weeks away. This is triage. The goal is to maximize the points you can realistically earn given available time — not to cover everything.

**The core principle:** Only work on concepts that appear on every exam. Skip anything with low exam weight or high memorization cost relative to point yield.

### Day 1: Unit 7 — Natural Selection

Priority concepts:
- Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium: p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1. Practice 5 calculations until the algebra is automatic.
- Natural selection mechanisms: stabilizing, directional, disruptive — know an example of each
- Genetic drift: bottleneck effect vs. founder effect
- Speciation: allopatric (geographic separation) vs. sympatric (same location, reproductive isolation)

**Why Unit 7 first:** It's the highest-weight unit (13–20%) and the question types are more predictable than any other unit. Hardy-Weinberg questions appear on nearly every exam and are learnable quickly — they're calculation-based, which means there's a right answer with no interpretation required.

### Day 2: Unit 3 — Cellular Energetics

Priority concepts:
- Photosynthesis: light-dependent reactions in the thylakoid membrane (photosystems I and II, NADPH production, ATP synthesis), Calvin cycle in the stroma (carbon fixation, G3P, RuBP regeneration)
- Cellular respiration: glycolysis → pyruvate oxidation → Krebs cycle → electron transport chain → ATP synthase
- The critical mechanism: NADH and FADH₂ carry electrons from earlier stages to the ETC. Electrons flowing through ETC proteins release energy used to pump H⁺ across the inner mitochondrial membrane. The resulting proton gradient drives ATP synthase (chemiosmosis).

**Why the proton gradient is what you need to know:** The electron transport chain mechanism appears in some form on the FRQ section almost every year. Students who write "electrons are passed through proteins, releasing energy that makes ATP" lose points. Students who write "electron flow pumps H⁺ from the matrix to the intermembrane space, creating a proton gradient; H⁺ flows back through ATP synthase, driving phosphorylation of ADP to ATP" earn them.

### Day 3–4: Unit 6 — Gene Expression and Regulation

Day 3 priority concepts:
- Transcription: DNA → pre-mRNA in the nucleus; RNA polymerase reads the template strand 3'→5' and synthesizes mRNA 5'→3'; start codon, stop codon, polyadenylation
- Translation: mRNA → polypeptide at the ribosome; codon-anticodon base pairing; start (AUG, methionine), elongation, stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA)
- Post-translational modification and protein folding

Day 4 priority concepts:
- Gene regulation: promoters, transcription factors, enhancers (eukaryotes); operons — lac operon and trp operon (prokaryotes)
- Mutations: frameshift vs. point mutations — know why frameshifts are typically more damaging (they alter every codon downstream of the mutation vs. one codon for a substitution)
- Epigenetics: DNA methylation and histone modification as gene expression regulators

**The most commonly missed FRQ topic in Unit 6:** Students can describe transcription and translation correctly but can't explain why a specific mutation would or would not affect the final protein. Practice connecting: mutation type → codon change → amino acid change → protein structure → protein function.

### Day 5: Units 2 and 4

Unit 2 (60 minutes):
- Membrane transport: passive (simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis) vs. active (requires energy, moves against gradient)
- Osmosis: water moves from high water potential to low water potential — toward higher solute concentration

Unit 4 (60 minutes):
- Signal transduction pathway structure: ligand → receptor → second messenger → protein kinase cascade → cellular response
- Know one complete pathway: epinephrine → GPCR → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → protein kinase A → phosphorylation of target proteins
- Cell cycle checkpoints: G1, G2, and spindle assembly checkpoints; what happens when they fail (cancer)

### Day 6: FRQ Structure — Read Rubrics Only

Pull 3 official short FRQs from AP Central (2020–2024). Do not write answers. Read the scoring guidelines line by line.

**The one activity most students never do — and should do first:** Most students have never read an AP Biology scoring guideline. Reading them reveals that each point is a discrete, specific criterion — not a holistic judgment. Seeing how points are literally awarded changes how you write. Understanding that "ATP is produced" earns 0 points while "ATP is produced by ATP synthase as H⁺ flows through it down the electrochemical gradient" earns 1 point is worth more than an hour of note-taking.

### Days 7–8: Two Scored FRQs

Write two complete short FRQs under timed conditions (10–12 minutes each). Score yourself against official rubrics immediately after writing.

For every point you missed, write one sentence: "I missed this point because \_\_\_. The correct answer needed \_\_\_."

That sentence is more valuable than the practice question itself. It is the precise knowledge of your gap, not general anxiety about the topic.

### Day 9: Units 5 and 8 (Light Pass)

Unit 5 (45 minutes):
- Reading pedigrees: identify carriers, determine inheritance pattern (autosomal dominant/recessive, X-linked)
- Non-Mendelian ratios: codominance, incomplete dominance, epistasis, linked genes

Unit 8 (45 minutes):
- Energy flow: 10% rule between trophic levels; why energy is lost (heat, metabolism)
- Nitrogen cycle: nitrogen fixation (bacteria), nitrification, denitrification — know the direction of conversion
- Water cycle: evapotranspiration, runoff, groundwater recharge

**If you're truly out of time:** Skip Unit 1 entirely. The macromolecule content has the lowest point yield relative to study time required. Prioritize the time here instead on a third scored FRQ attempt.

### Day 10 — Exam Eve: No New Material

- Re-read your scored FRQs from Days 7–8 and the rubric explanations
- Practice 2 Hardy-Weinberg calculations (just the math, no need for context)
- Review the formula sheet format — know where Hardy-Weinberg, chi-square, and water potential formulas are located before you need them under pressure
- Do not study new content. The research on memory consolidation is clear: sleep the night before an exam improves recall more than late-night studying. Go to sleep at your normal time.

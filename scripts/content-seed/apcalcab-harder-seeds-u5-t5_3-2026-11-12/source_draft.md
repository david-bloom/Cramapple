## Seed N-5.3-A (new) — Topic 5.3 Determining Intervals of Increase and Decrease

**Skill:** derive increasing and decreasing intervals from a factored derivative



A differentiable function f has f'(x)=(x+2)(x-1) on (-4,4). On which intervals is f increasing, and why?



- **A. f increases on (-4,-2) and (1,4), where both derivative factors have the same sign.**

- B. f increases only on (-2,1), where the two factors have opposite signs.

- C. f increases on all of (-4,4) because f' has two zeros.

- D. f increases only at x=-2 and x=1 because f'=0 there.



Check: The product is positive for x<-2 and x>1, negative between the roots, and zero only at the roots. f increases where f'>0.

Rationales: B: Opposite factor signs make f' negative between the roots, so f decreases there. C: Zeros split the domain into sign intervals; they do not make f positive everywhere. D: At the two isolated zeros the instantaneous slope is zero; intervals of increase come from positive derivative signs.



**N-5.3-A-v1.** Suppose g'(x)=-(x+3)(x-2) for every x in (-5,4). Which description of g is supported?



- **A. g increases on (-3,2) and decreases on (-5,-3) and (2,4).**

- B. g decreases on (-3,2) because the two factors have opposite signs there.

- C. g increases on both outer intervals because a quadratic derivative is positive for large |x|.

- D. g is constant on (-3,2) because g' vanishes at the interval's ends.



Check: Inside (-3,2) the product (x+3)(x-2) is negative, so its negative is positive. On either outer interval the product is positive and g' negative.

Rationales: B: The leading minus sign reverses the sign of the negative product between roots. C: The leading minus sign makes the derivative negative outside the roots in this case. D: The derivative vanishes only at two points, not throughout the open interval between them.



**N-5.3-A-v2.** A differentiable h has h'(x)=x(x-2)(x+1) on (-3,3). Which intervals of increase follow from a sign analysis?



- **A. h increases on (-1,0) and (2,3), where the three-factor product is positive.**

- B. h increases on (-3,-1) and (0,2), where the product is negative.

- C. h increases on all intervals because h' has three real zeros.

- D. h increases only at x=-1, 0, and 2.



Check: Across roots -1,0,2, the cubic derivative signs are negative, positive, negative, positive. Positive signs give (-1,0) and (2,3).

Rationales: B: A negative derivative means h decreases on those two intervals, not increases. C: Roots identify sign boundaries; the derivative is negative on two of the four intervals. D: A derivative equal to zero at isolated points does not define an increasing interval.



**N-5.3-A-v3.** A differentiable p satisfies p'(x)=(x-1)²(x+2) on (-4,4). Where does p decrease?



- **A. p decreases on (-4,-2); the squared factor does not reverse the derivative's sign at x=1.**

- B. p decreases on (-4,-2) and (1,4), since every derivative zero flips its sign.

- C. p decreases on (-2,1), because x+2 is positive there.

- D. p never decreases, because a squared factor makes p' nonnegative everywhere.



Check: (x-1)² is nonnegative and positive away from 1, so the sign comes from x+2. It is negative only for x<-2; the zero at 1 does not change sign.

Rationales: B: An even-multiplicity zero at 1 does not switch derivative sign. C: Both x+2 and the squared factor are positive on (-2,1), so p increases there. D: The other factor x+2 is negative to the left of -2, making p' negative there.

## Seed N-5.3-B (new) — Topic 5.3 Determining Intervals of Increase and Decrease

**Skill:** interpret a derivative sign representation without confusing it with function values



A graph of f' lies above the x-axis on (-3,-1), below it on (-1,2), and above it on (2,4), crossing at -1 and 2. A student says f is negative on (-1,2). What is the justified conclusion?



- **A. f decreases on (-1,2); the sign of f' gives direction, but it does not determine whether f itself is positive or negative there.**

- B. f is negative on (-1,2) because its derivative graph is below the axis.

- C. f increases on (-1,2) because the graph of f' may be rising there.

- D. f is constant on (-1,2) because f' equals zero at both ends.



Check: f'<0 throughout (-1,2), so f decreases. Derivative sign alone cannot determine the vertical position or sign of f.

Rationales: B: A negative derivative means negative slope, not necessarily negative function values. C: Whether f' itself rises does not change its negative sign on the stated interval. D: Zero derivative at isolated boundaries does not make f constant across the interval.



**N-5.3-B-v1.** The derivative of a differentiable g is negative on (0,2) and positive on (2,5), with g(2)=7. Which comparison is certain?



- **A. g(1)>7 and g(4)>7; g decreases toward x=2 then increases away from it, but g(1) versus g(4) is undetermined.**

- B. g(1)<7 and g(4)<7 because g' changes sign at 2.

- C. g(1)>g(4) because the negative derivative on (0,2) controls the whole interval.

- D. g(1)=g(4) because the intervals lie on opposite sides of x=2.



Check: As x increases from 1 to 2, g falls to 7, so g(1)>7. As x increases from 2 to 4, g rises from 7, so g(4)>7. Their relative heights need rate magnitudes.

Rationales: B: Both side values exceed the local minimum g(2)=7, not fall below it. C: Derivative signs establish each comparison to g(2), but not between g(1) and g(4). D: Opposite sides of a minimum need not have equal function values without symmetry or rate information.



**N-5.3-B-v2.** A differentiable function h has h'(x)>0 on (-4,0) and h'(x)<0 on (0,3). Which statement about h(-2), h(0), and h(2) must hold?



- **A. h(-2)<h(0) and h(2)<h(0); the chart does not order h(-2) against h(2).**

- B. h(-2)<h(2)<h(0) must hold because -2<2.

- C. h(-2)>h(0)>h(2) because a positive derivative makes earlier values larger.

- D. h(-2)=h(2) because the derivative signs switch at 0.



Check: A positive derivative up to 0 raises h from -2 to 0, and a negative derivative afterward lowers it from 0 to 2. The two side values can differ either way.

Rationales: B: x-order alone cannot compare values across two intervals with opposite derivative signs. C: A positive derivative makes later values greater on (-4,0), not earlier ones. D: A sign change at 0 establishes a peak, not symmetry of the two side values.



**N-5.3-B-v3.** A graph shows q' positive for 1<x<3, zero at x=3, and negative for 3<x<6. A student concludes q is increasing on (1,6) because q'(3)=0. What correction is valid?



- **A. q increases on (1,3) and decreases on (3,6); one zero does not override the derivative signs on either side.**

- B. q increases on all of (1,6) because a derivative can be zero at one point.

- C. q decreases on (1,3) because q' is above the axis there.

- D. Nothing can be inferred about monotonicity unless q values are also given.



Check: The sign of q' is positive before 3 and negative afterward. Those signs determine the two monotonic intervals independently of the isolated zero.

Rationales: B: A zero derivative at one point does not erase a negative derivative on the entire right interval. C: A derivative graph above zero means q has positive slope and increases to the left of 3. D: Derivative signs are sufficient to establish monotonic intervals when differentiability is given.

## Seed N-5.3-C (new) — Topic 5.3 Determining Intervals of Increase and Decrease

**Skill:** make a derivative sign chart around zeros and points outside the domain



A function f is differentiable wherever x≠2 and satisfies f'(x)=(x+1)/(x-2). Its domain includes both sides of x=2 but excludes x=2. Which monotonic intervals follow?



- **A. f increases on (-infinity,-1) and (2,infinity), and decreases on (-1,2); the domain gap separates the last two intervals.**

- B. f increases on (-1,2) because the numerator is positive there.

- C. f increases on (-infinity,-1) and (-1,infinity) because only the zero at -1 matters.

- D. f decreases on both sides of x=2 because the denominator is zero there.



Check: The ratio signs across -1 and 2 are positive, negative, positive. x=2 is outside the domain, so monotonic intervals cannot be joined through it.

Rationales: B: The denominator is negative on (-1,2), making the positive numerator produce a negative derivative. C: The denominator changes sign at the excluded point 2, so the sign chart must split there. D: A derivative undefined at one point does not force the same sign on both neighboring intervals.



**N-5.3-C-v1.** A differentiable g has g'(x)=(x-3)/(x+1) wherever x≠-1; g is undefined at -1. On which intervals is g decreasing?



- **A. g decreases on (-1,3) only, where the numerator and denominator have opposite signs.**

- B. g decreases on (-infinity,-1) and (3,infinity), because both factors share a sign there.

- C. g decreases on (-infinity,3) as a single interval, crossing the excluded x=-1.

- D. g never decreases because the derivative is a quotient rather than a product.



Check: For x<-1 the ratio is positive, for -1<x<3 it is negative, and for x>3 it is positive. The domain gap at -1 prevents merging across it.

Rationales: B: Equal signs make a quotient positive, so g increases on the two outer intervals. C: A monotonic interval cannot contain the point -1 where g is not defined. D: A quotient can be negative; its sign is determined by numerator and denominator signs.



**N-5.3-C-v2.** A function h has h'(x)=(x+2)/(x-1) wherever x≠1, and h is undefined at 1. Which statement is warranted by the derivative?



- **A. h decreases on (-2,1) and increases on (-infinity,-2) and (1,infinity); x=1 is a domain break, not a critical point of h.**

- B. h decreases on both intervals to the left of 1 because the denominator is negative there.

- C. x=1 is a critical point because h' does not exist there, even though h itself is undefined.

- D. h increases throughout (-infinity,infinity) because h' is positive for large x.



Check: Across -2 and 1, ratio signs are positive, negative, positive. A critical point must be in the function's domain; x=1 is excluded.

Rationales: B: For x<-2 both numerator and denominator are negative, making h' positive. C: An excluded input is not a point of h, so it cannot be a critical point on its graph. D: Positive derivative for some large x-values does not establish increase on intervals where h'<0.



**N-5.3-C-v3.** A differentiable p has p'(x)=(x-2)/(x+4) except at x=-4, where p is also undefined. A student says p increases on (-infinity,2) because the numerator is negative there. Which correction is correct?



- **A. p increases on (-infinity,-4) and (2,infinity), but decreases on (-4,2); the quotient changes sign at the excluded x=-4.**

- B. p increases on (-4,2) because a negative numerator always makes a quotient positive.

- C. p increases on all x<2 as one interval because x=-4 is just an isolated point.

- D. p decreases on both outer intervals because the numerator has a zero at x=2.



Check: The ratio is positive for x<-4, negative for -4<x<2, and positive for x>2. The excluded point splits the domain and the derivative sign.

Rationales: B: On (-4,2), the denominator is positive while the numerator is negative, so p'<0. C: No monotonic interval can cross an input at which the function is undefined. D: A derivative zero at x=2 does not make the derivative negative on either outer interval.

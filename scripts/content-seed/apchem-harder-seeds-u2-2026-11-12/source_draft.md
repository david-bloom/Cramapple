## Seed N-2.1-A (new) — Topic 2.1 Types of Chemical Bonds
**Skill:** classify the bonding in a solid from a set of property data, resisting a single misleading cue (low melting temperature).

Substance Q is a silvery solid that can be cut with a knife. It conducts electricity as a solid, can be hammered into thin sheets without breaking, and melts at 98°C. A student reasons that the low melting temperature shows that Q consists of separate molecules held together by weak forces. Which statement is best supported by all of the data?

- A. Q is a molecular solid, because a low melting temperature always indicates weak attractions between separate molecules.
- B. Q is an ionic solid, because its metal atoms have transferred electrons to nonmetal atoms in a lattice.
- **C. Q is a metallic solid; conduction as a solid and malleability require delocalized electrons shared among metal cations, and the low melting temperature only shows that these attractions are comparatively weak.**
- D. Q is a network covalent solid, because covalent bonds between neighboring atoms allow it to conduct as a solid.

Check: conduction in the solid state plus malleability point to a sea of delocalized electrons around metal cations, so the bonding is metallic. A low melting temperature is consistent with metallic bonding that is weak, such as in sodium, 98°C.
Rationales: A: molecular solids do not conduct electricity as solids and are not malleable, so these data contradict a molecular model even though the melting temperature is low. B: ionic solids are brittle and do not conduct as solids, because their ions are fixed in the lattice. D: network covalent solids are very hard with very high melting temperatures, and their electrons are localized in bonds, so they are not malleable.

**N-2.1-A-v1.** Substance R is a gray solid that conducts electricity both as a solid and as a liquid. It can be pressed into a new shape without shattering, and it melts at 30°C, just above room temperature. A student argues that because R melts so easily it must be a molecular compound. Which statement is best supported by all of the data?

- **A. R is a metallic solid; its delocalized valence electrons explain conduction and shape change, and the low melting temperature simply means the attraction between cations and electrons is relatively weak.**
- B. R is a molecular solid, because substances with melting temperatures near room temperature are always made of small molecules.
- C. R is an ionic solid, because it conducts electricity when it is a liquid.
- D. R is a network covalent solid, because covalent bonds hold the atoms together in a continuous array that can be reshaped.

Check: conduction as a solid and malleability require mobile delocalized electrons, which identifies metallic bonding; gallium is a real example, melting at about 30°C.
Rationales: B: molecular solids have no mobile charge carriers and cannot conduct as solids, so the conductivity rules out a molecular model despite the low melting temperature. C: ionic solids conduct only when molten or dissolved, not as solids, and they shatter when struck instead of changing shape. D: network covalent solids have very high melting temperatures, and covalent networks are rigid and would fracture rather than be pressed into shape.

**N-2.1-A-v2.** Substance S is a colorless crystal that does not conduct electricity as a solid or when molten, is extremely hard, does not dissolve in water, and melts above 1700°C. A student concludes that the high melting temperature shows S is ionic. Which statement is best supported by all of the data?

- A. S is an ionic solid, because only ionic attractions are strong enough to give a melting temperature above 1700°C.
- B. S is a metallic solid, because very hard solids with high melting temperatures contain a sea of delocalized electrons.
- C. S is a molecular solid, because it is a nonconductor and nonconductors are always made of discrete molecules.
- **D. S is a network covalent solid; each atom is covalently bonded to its neighbors in a continuous three-dimensional array, so there are no mobile ions or electrons and melting requires breaking many covalent bonds.**

Check: a nonconductor in both solid and molten states has no mobile ions, which rules out ionic bonding; extreme hardness and a very high melting temperature indicate a covalent network, as in quartz or diamond.
Rationales: A: an ionic compound would conduct once it is molten because its ions would be free to move, and S does not. B: a metallic solid would conduct electricity as a solid, which S does not. C: molecular solids have weak forces between molecules and melt at low temperatures, so they cannot be extremely hard with a melting temperature above 1700°C.

**N-2.1-A-v3.** Substance T is a white, brittle solid that does not conduct electricity as a solid but conducts well when molten at 650°C. A student concludes that T is a metal because it conducts when molten. Which statement is best supported by all of the data?

- A. T is a metallic solid, because any substance that conducts electricity when molten must contain delocalized electrons.
- **B. T is an ionic solid; its ions are fixed in a lattice in the solid, so it does not conduct, and they become mobile charge carriers when T melts.**
- C. T is a network covalent solid, because it is brittle and the melting temperature is high.
- D. T is a molecular solid, because it is a nonconductor in the solid state.

Check: nonconductor as a solid, brittle, and conducting only when molten is the pattern for an ionic solid; the charge carriers when molten are ions, not electrons.
Rationales: A: a metal would conduct as a solid, and T does not, so molten conductivity alone cannot distinguish metallic from ionic bonding. C: network covalent solids do not conduct when molten because they have no mobile ions or electrons, whereas T does. D: molecular solids do not conduct when molten either, because neutral molecules carry no charge.

## Seed N-2.1-B (new) — Topic 2.1 Types of Chemical Bonds
**Skill:** use electronegativity values to determine the direction of bond polarity, including cases where hydrogen is the more electronegative atom.

Pauling electronegativities: H 2.20, C 2.55, Si 1.90. A student states that in any bond to hydrogen, the hydrogen atom carries the partial positive charge. Which statement correctly gives the partial charge on the hydrogen atom in the C–H bonds of CH₄ and in the Si–H bonds of SiH₄?

- A. Hydrogen is δ+ in both CH₄ and SiH₄, because hydrogen is always the less electronegative partner.
- B. Hydrogen carries no partial charge in either molecule, because C, Si and H all form covalent bonds by sharing electrons.
- C. Hydrogen is δ− in CH₄ and δ+ in SiH₄, because the larger electronegativity difference makes it more negative.
- **D. Hydrogen is δ+ in CH₄ and δ− in SiH₄, because the shared electrons are drawn toward the more electronegative atom in each bond.**

Check: in C–H the difference is 2.55 − 2.20 = 0.35 with C more electronegative, so H is δ+; in Si–H the difference is 2.20 − 1.90 = 0.30 with H more electronegative, so H is δ−.
Rationales: A: hydrogen is not always the less electronegative atom; silicon at 1.90 is less electronegative than hydrogen at 2.20, so the polarity reverses. B: sharing electrons does not make the sharing equal, and unequal electronegativities give polar covalent bonds with partial charges. C: this reverses both directions, because carbon at 2.55 attracts the shared pair more than hydrogen and hydrogen attracts it more than silicon.

**N-2.1-B-v1.** Pauling electronegativities: H 2.20, B 2.04, N 3.04. Which statement correctly gives the partial charge on the hydrogen atom in BH₃ and in NH₃?

- **A. Hydrogen is δ− in BH₃ and δ+ in NH₃.**
- B. Hydrogen is δ+ in both BH₃ and NH₃.
- C. Hydrogen is δ+ in BH₃ and δ− in NH₃.
- D. Hydrogen is δ− in both BH₃ and NH₃, because hydrogen has only one electron to share.

Check: in B–H, H (2.20) is more electronegative than B (2.04), so H is δ−; in N–H, N (3.04) is more electronegative than H (2.20), so H is δ+.
Rationales: B: it assumes that hydrogen is always the positive end, but boron is less electronegative than hydrogen, so the shared pair shifts toward hydrogen in BH₃. C: this reverses the polarity of both bonds and ignores that nitrogen at 3.04 pulls the shared pair away from hydrogen at 2.20. D: the number of valence electrons an atom has does not set bond polarity, and nitrogen attracts the shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen.

**N-2.1-B-v2.** Pauling electronegativities: H 2.20, Na 0.93, Br 2.96. Which statement correctly describes the charge on the hydrogen atom in the compound NaH and in the molecule HBr?

- A. Hydrogen is δ+ or positively charged in both NaH and HBr.
- B. Hydrogen is negatively charged in HBr and positively charged in NaH.
- **C. Hydrogen is negatively charged in NaH, and it carries a partial positive charge in HBr.**
- D. Hydrogen is neutral in NaH and δ+ in HBr, because only HBr has a difference in electronegativity.

Check: in NaH the electronegativity difference is 2.20 − 0.93 = 1.27 with H more electronegative, so H is the negative end; in HBr, Br at 2.96 is more electronegative than H at 2.20, so H is δ+.
Rationales: A: this treats hydrogen as the positive end of every bond, but sodium at 0.93 is far less electronegative than hydrogen. B: this reverses both cases, because bromine at 2.96 attracts the shared pair more than hydrogen and hydrogen attracts the electrons more than sodium does. D: NaH also has a large electronegativity difference, 1.27, so hydrogen is not neutral there.

**N-2.1-B-v3.** Pauling electronegativities: H 2.20, Ge 2.01, O 3.44. Which statement correctly gives the partial charge on the hydrogen atoms in GeH₄ and in H₂O?

- A. Hydrogen is δ+ in both GeH₄ and H₂O, because it is the smallest atom in each molecule.
- **B. Hydrogen is δ− in GeH₄ and δ+ in H₂O.**
- C. Hydrogen is δ+ in GeH₄ and δ− in H₂O.
- D. Hydrogen is δ− in both GeH₄ and H₂O, because it is bonded to a central atom in each.

Check: in Ge–H, H (2.20) exceeds Ge (2.01) so H is δ−; in O–H, O (3.44) exceeds H (2.20) so H is δ+.
Rationales: A: atomic size does not decide polarity, which depends on electronegativity, and germanium at 2.01 is less electronegative than hydrogen. C: this reverses both bonds, since oxygen is the more electronegative atom in water and hydrogen is the more electronegative atom in GeH₄. D: being bonded to a central atom does not make hydrogen negative, and in water oxygen draws the electrons away from hydrogen.

## Seed N-2.2-A (new) — Topic 2.2 Intramolecular Force and Potential Energy
**Skill:** read bond length (position of the minimum) and bond energy (depth of the minimum) from potential-energy data, without assuming that the shorter bond is the stronger one.

Potential energy (PE) is defined as 0 kJ/mol for fully separated atoms. A student records PE for two hypothetical diatomic molecules, X₂ and Y₂, at several internuclear distances.

Distance (pm): 90, 110, 130, 150, 170
PE of X₂ (kJ/mol): +50, −300, −180, −90, −30
PE of Y₂ (kJ/mol): +900, −200, −430, −520, −400

Which statement correctly compares the two molecules?

- A. X₂ has the longer bond, and X₂ has the greater bond energy.
- B. X₂ has the longer bond, and Y₂ has the greater bond energy.
- C. Y₂ has the longer bond, and X₂ has the greater bond energy.
- **D. Y₂ has the longer bond, and Y₂ has the greater bond energy.**

Check: the minimum for X₂ is −300 kJ/mol at 110 pm and for Y₂ is −520 kJ/mol at 150 pm. Bond length is the distance at the minimum (150 pm > 110 pm, so Y₂ is longer) and bond energy is the depth of the minimum (520 > 300 kJ/mol, so Y₂ is stronger).
Rationales: A: it reads both quantities for the wrong molecule; the lowest PE and the larger distance both belong to Y₂. B: it reverses the bond lengths, because the PE minimum of X₂ occurs at the shorter distance, 110 pm. C: it applies the rule that the longer bond must be the weaker one, but bond energy is the depth of the well, and Y₂ has the deeper well at the longer distance.

**N-2.2-A-v1.** PE is 0 kJ/mol for separated atoms. A student records PE for two hypothetical diatomic molecules, P₂ and Q₂.

Distance (pm): 90, 105, 120, 135, 150
PE of P₂ (kJ/mol): +350, −40, −250, −390, −310
PE of Q₂ (kJ/mol): −300, −520, −420, −250, −140

Which statement correctly compares the two molecules?

- A. P₂ has the shorter bond, and P₂ has the greater bond energy.
- **B. Q₂ has the shorter bond, and Q₂ has the greater bond energy.**
- C. P₂ has the shorter bond, and Q₂ has the greater bond energy.
- D. Q₂ has the shorter bond, and P₂ has the greater bond energy.

Check: P₂ has its minimum of −390 kJ/mol at 135 pm and Q₂ has its minimum of −520 kJ/mol at 105 pm, so Q₂ has both the shorter bond and the larger bond energy.
Rationales: A: it assigns both properties to P₂, but P₂ has the minimum at the larger distance and the shallower well. C: it correctly sees that Q₂ is deeper but reads the distance of P₂ as the shorter one, when its minimum is at 135 pm and not 105 pm. D: it correctly places Q₂ at the shorter distance but then claims P₂ is stronger, when the well of P₂ is only 390 kJ/mol deep and the well of Q₂ is 520 kJ/mol deep.

**N-2.2-A-v2.** PE is 0 kJ/mol for separated atoms. A student records PE for two hypothetical diatomic molecules, U₂ and V₂.

Rationales: A: it reverses the lengths, but the minimum of U₂ is at 100 pm, which is shorter than the 140 pm of V₂. B: it assigns both properties to U₂, which has the shorter bond and the shallower well. D: it uses the rule that a longer bond is a weaker bond, but bond energy is the depth of the well, which is larger for V₂ at 460 kJ/mol than for U₂ at 210 kJ/mol.

## Seed N-2.2-B (new) — Topic 2.2 Intramolecular Force and Potential Energy
**Skill:** use a potential-energy curve to predict the direction of the net force and the motion of two atoms released from rest at a given distance.

For two hydrogen atoms, PE is 0 kJ/mol when they are far apart. The PE is −436 kJ/mol at the equilibrium distance of 74 pm, −300 kJ/mol at 60 pm, and −300 kJ/mol at 100 pm. Two hydrogen atoms are released from rest when their nuclei are 60 pm apart. Which description is correct?

- A. The atoms move closer together, because their PE is negative and negative PE means the net force is attractive.
- B. The atoms stay at 60 pm, because any distance with negative PE is stable.
- **C. The net force is repulsive, because electron and nucleus repulsions outweigh attractions inside 74 pm; the atoms move apart, and PE decreases as kinetic energy increases until 74 pm.**
- D. The atoms separate completely, because repulsion at short distances always exceeds the energy that holds the atoms together.

Check: at 60 pm the PE is higher than the minimum, and PE decreases as the distance increases toward 74 pm, so the net force pushes the atoms apart. The total energy of −300 kJ/mol is below zero, so the atoms remain bound.
Rationales: A: the sign of the PE does not give the direction of the force, which depends on whether PE falls or rises as the distance changes, and PE falls as the atoms move apart from 60 pm. B: only the minimum at 74 pm is an equilibrium distance, and at 60 pm the PE is above the minimum so a net force acts. D: the total energy is −300 kJ/mol, which is below zero, so the atoms cannot escape to infinite separation without gaining energy.

**N-2.2-B-v1.** For two nitrogen atoms in N₂, PE is 0 kJ/mol when the atoms are far apart, −945 kJ/mol at the equilibrium distance of 110 pm, and −500 kJ/mol at 150 pm. Two nitrogen atoms are released from rest when their nuclei are 150 pm apart. Which description is correct?

- A. The net force is repulsive, because PE is negative and the atoms are farther apart than at equilibrium.
- B. The atoms remain at 150 pm, because the PE there is negative.
- C. The atoms drift apart until they are free atoms, because the PE at 150 pm is higher than at 110 pm.
- **D. The net force is attractive; the atoms move closer together, PE decreases, and kinetic energy increases until the distance is 110 pm.**

Check: at 150 pm, PE (−500) is above the minimum (−945 at 110 pm), and PE falls as the distance decreases toward 110 pm, so the net force is attractive.
Rationales: A: at distances greater than the equilibrium distance, attractions between nuclei and electrons outweigh repulsions, so the net force is attractive and not repulsive. B: a negative PE alone does not make a distance an equilibrium, and the atoms feel a net force because PE changes with distance at 150 pm. C: the total energy of −500 kJ/mol is below zero, so the atoms are bound and cannot separate completely without added energy.

**N-2.2-B-v2.** For two chlorine atoms in Cl₂, PE is 0 kJ/mol when the atoms are far apart, −243 kJ/mol at the equilibrium distance of 199 pm, and −120 kJ/mol at 170 pm. Two chlorine atoms are released from rest when their nuclei are 170 pm apart. Which description is correct?

- A. The atoms move closer together, because PE is negative and the atoms were released from rest.
- **B. The net force is repulsive; the atoms move apart, passing through 199 pm with their maximum kinetic energy.**
- C. The atoms do not move, because the PE at 170 pm is negative and so the atoms are stable.
- D. The atoms fly apart and separate completely, because a distance shorter than 199 pm always means the atoms repel enough to break the bond.

Check: at 170 pm the PE (−120) is higher than the minimum (−243 at 199 pm), so PE falls as the atoms separate and the net force is repulsive; the kinetic energy is greatest where PE is lowest, at 199 pm.
Rationales: A: moving closer from 170 pm would raise PE and need energy input, so a released pair moves toward the lower PE at 199 pm, which is farther apart. C: only the equilibrium distance has zero net force, and at 170 pm the atoms feel a net repulsion even though PE is negative. D: the total energy of −120 kJ/mol is below zero, so the atoms oscillate about 199 pm without breaking apart.

**N-2.2-B-v3.** For the H–Br bond, PE is 0 kJ/mol when the atoms are far apart, −366 kJ/mol at the equilibrium distance of 141 pm, and −100 kJ/mol at 220 pm. A hydrogen atom and a bromine atom are released from rest when their nuclei are 220 pm apart. Which description is correct?

- A. The net force is repulsive, because the atoms are farther apart than their equilibrium distance.
- B. The atoms stay at 220 pm, because they are neutral atoms and exert no net force.
- **C. The net force is attractive; the atoms move closer together as PE decreases and kinetic energy increases toward 141 pm.**
- D. The atoms move apart to infinite separation, because the PE at 220 pm is higher than the PE at 141 pm.

Check: the PE at 220 pm (−100) is above the minimum, and PE falls as the atoms approach 141 pm, so the force is attractive and the atoms speed up as they approach.
Rationales: A: beyond the equilibrium distance, the attraction between nuclei and electrons outweighs the repulsions, so the force is attractive. B: neutral atoms still attract through electron and nucleus interactions at these distances, and the PE value of −100 kJ/mol shows the interaction is not zero. D: a system with negative total energy cannot separate to infinity, and the lower PE at 141 pm means the atoms move toward it and not away.

## Seed N-2.3-A (new) — Topic 2.3 Structure of Ionic Solids
**Skill:** apply Coulomb's law quantitatively, using ion charges and the sum of ionic radii, to compare the strength of ionic attractions in two crystals.

Approximate ionic radii (pm): Mg²⁺ 72, O²⁻ 140, Na⁺ 102, F⁻ 133. Treat the distance between neighboring ions as the sum of the ionic radii. The magnitude of the electrostatic potential energy of a cation-anion pair is proportional to (charge of cation × charge of anion) ÷ distance. About how many times larger is this magnitude for a neighboring Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ pair in MgO than for a neighboring Na⁺ and F⁻ pair in NaF?

- A. About 2.0 times, because the sum of the charges of the ions in MgO is twice the sum in NaF.
- B. About 4.9 times larger.
- C. About 4.0 times, because the charge product is 4 times larger and the distances are almost identical.
- **D. About 4.4 times larger.**

Check: MgO distance = 72 + 140 = 212 pm, so 4/212 = 0.01887. NaF distance = 102 + 133 = 235 pm, so 1/235 = 0.004255. Ratio = 0.01887 ÷ 0.004255 = 4.43.
Rationales: A: potential energy depends on the product of the charges and not on their sum, and the distance also differs. B: this value is the ratio of the forces, 4 × (235/212)², because force varies as 1/d², whereas the potential energy varies as 1/d. C: it ignores the smaller distance in MgO, 212 pm against 235 pm, which raises the ratio above the charge-product ratio of 4.

**N-2.3-A-v1.** Approximate ionic radii (pm): Ca²⁺ 100, O²⁻ 140, Rb⁺ 152, Br⁻ 196. Treat the distance between neighboring ions as the sum of the ionic radii. The magnitude of the electrostatic potential energy of a cation-anion pair is proportional to (charge of cation × charge of anion) ÷ distance. About how many times larger is this magnitude for a neighboring Ca²⁺ and O²⁻ pair in CaO than for a neighboring Rb⁺ and Br⁻ pair in RbBr?

- **A. About 5.8 times larger.**
- B. About 8.4 times larger.
- C. About 4.0 times, because only the charge product matters and the ions are about the same size.
- D. About 2.0 times, because the cation and anion charges in CaO add to 4 and those in RbBr add to 2.

Check: CaO distance = 100 + 140 = 240 pm, so 4/240 = 0.01667. RbBr distance = 152 + 196 = 348 pm, so 1/348 = 0.002874. Ratio = 5.80.
Rationales: B: this is the force ratio, 4 × (348/240)², which uses an inverse-square dependence, whereas potential energy depends on 1/d. C: it ignores that the RbBr ions are much larger, so the distance in RbBr is 348 pm against 240 pm in CaO. D: the energy depends on the product of the charges, 2 × 2 against 1 × 1, and not on their sum, and the distances differ too.

**N-2.3-A-v2.** Approximate ionic radii (pm): Al³⁺ 54, O²⁻ 140, K⁺ 138, Br⁻ 196. Treat the distance between neighboring ions as the sum of the ionic radii. The magnitude of the electrostatic potential energy of a cation-anion pair is proportional to (charge of cation × charge of anion) ÷ distance. About how many times larger is this magnitude for a neighboring Al³⁺ and O²⁻ pair in Al₂O₃ than for a neighboring K⁺ and Br⁻ pair in KBr?

- A. About 6.0 times, because the charge product is 6 times larger.
- B. About 17.8 times larger.
- **C. About 10.3 times larger.**
- D. About 2.5 times, because the charges in Al₂O₃ add to 5 and those in KBr add to 2.

Check: Al₂O₃ distance = 54 + 140 = 194 pm, so 6/194 = 0.03093. KBr distance = 138 + 196 = 334 pm, so 1/334 = 0.002994. Ratio = 10.33.
Rationales: A: it leaves out the distance, but the ions in Al₂O₃ are much closer, 194 pm against 334 pm, which raises the ratio well above 6. B: this is the force ratio, 6 × (334/194)², since force depends on 1/d² while potential energy depends on 1/d. D: energy depends on the product of the charges, 3 × 2 against 1 × 1, and not on their sum.

**N-2.3-A-v3.** Approximate ionic radii (pm): Ba²⁺ 135, O²⁻ 140, Cs⁺ 167, I⁻ 220. Treat the distance between neighboring ions as the sum of the ionic radii. The magnitude of the electrostatic potential energy of a cation-anion pair is proportional to (charge of cation × charge of anion) ÷ distance. About how many times larger is this magnitude for a neighboring Ba²⁺ and O²⁻ pair in BaO than for a neighboring Cs⁺ and I⁻ pair in CsI?

- A. About 7.9 times larger.

C: it ignores the distance, but the ions in CsI are far apart at 387 pm against 275 pm in BaO, so the ratio exceeds 4.

## Seed N-2.3-B (new) — Topic 2.3 Structure of Ionic Solids
**Skill:** explain electrical conductivity data for an ionic compound in terms of mobile charged particles in the lattice, liquid and solution.

A student tests the electrical conductivity of sodium chloride in three forms and records the results.

Solid NaCl crystals: no current
Molten NaCl at 801°C: large current
NaCl dissolved in water: large current

Which explanation is consistent with all three results?

- A. In the solid, the ions are present but cannot carry charge; when NaCl melts, its ions are converted to Na and Cl atoms that share delocalized electrons.
- B. Ionic bonds exist only in solution, so the solid contains neutral atoms and the ions form only when the compound melts or dissolves.
- **C. The ions are held in fixed positions by strong attractions in the solid and cannot move; melting or dissolving frees them, and the mobile ions carry the current.**
- D. The electrons in solid NaCl are held tightly by chloride ions, and heating or dissolving the solid lets those electrons move freely as the current.

- **C. Formal charge −1, oxidation number +3**
- D. Formal charge +3, oxidation number −1

Check: formal charge = 3 − 0 − 4 = −1. Oxidation number: 4(−1) + B = −1, so B = +3.
Rationales: A: it treats the two numbers as the same, but formal charge splits the shared pairs equally, so boron has −1. B: it takes the ion charge as both quantities, but fluorine is more electronegative and takes the shared electrons, giving boron +3. D: it reverses the two values, though boron has three valence electrons and four bonds, which gives −1 as formal charge.

**N-2.6-B-v3.** In one Lewis diagram of the sulfite ion, SO₃²⁻, sulfur forms three single bonds to oxygen and has one lone pair. Which choice gives the formal charge and the oxidation number of sulfur in this diagram?

- **D. Formal charge +1, oxidation number +4**

## Seed N-2.7-A (new) — Topic 2.7 VSEPR and Hybridization
**Skill:** count sigma and pi bonds and assign hybridization for several atoms in a molecule from its bonding pattern.

Acrylonitrile, H₂C=CH–C≡N, is used to make plastics. Its atoms are bonded as shown: a C=C double bond, a C–C single bond and a C≡N triple bond, with two hydrogen atoms on the first carbon and one on the second. Which statement about its bonding is correct?

- A. It has 5 sigma bonds and 4 pi bonds, and the carbon in the C≡N group is sp hybridized.
- **B. It has 6 sigma bonds and 3 pi bonds, and the carbon in the C≡N group is sp hybridized.**
- C. It has 6 sigma bonds and 3 pi bonds, and the carbon in the C≡N group is sp² hybridized.
- D. It has 6 sigma bonds and 3 pi bonds, and the CH₂ carbon is sp³ hybridized.

Check: sigma bonds are 3 C–H, 1 C=C, 1 C–C and 1 C≡N, a total of 6; pi bonds are 1 in C=C and 2 in C≡N, a total of 3. The nitrile carbon has 2 electron domains (one single bond and one triple bond), so it is sp; the CH₂ carbon has 3 domains and is sp².
Rationales: A: it counts the triple bond as three pi bonds, but every multiple bond contains exactly one sigma bond, so the count of sigma bonds is 6. C: the carbon in the nitrile group has two electron domains, not three, so it is sp hybridized and not sp². D: the CH₂ carbon has three electron domains, two C–H bonds and one double bond to carbon, so it is sp² and not sp³.

**N-2.7-A-v1.** Propyne, H₃C–C≡C–H, has a methyl carbon bonded to three hydrogen atoms and a C≡C triple bond to a carbon that is bonded to the last hydrogen. Which statement about its bonding is correct?

- A. It has 6 sigma bonds and 2 pi bonds, and the methyl carbon is sp² hybridized.
- B. It has 5 sigma bonds and 3 pi bonds, and the terminal carbon is sp hybridized.
- **C. It has 6 sigma bonds and 2 pi bonds, and the methyl carbon is sp³ hybridized while both other carbons are sp hybridized.**
- D. It has 6 sigma bonds and 2 pi bonds, and all three carbon atoms are sp hybridized.

Check: sigma bonds are 3 C–H (methyl), 1 C–C, 1 C≡C and 1 C–H (terminal), a total of 6; pi bonds are 2 in the triple bond. The methyl carbon has 4 domains (sp³), and the two triple-bonded carbons have 2 domains each (sp).
Rationales: A: the methyl carbon has four single bonds, so it has four electron domains and is sp³, not sp². B: it counts 5 sigma bonds, which treats part of the triple bond as pi bonds only, but a triple bond has 1 sigma bond and 2 pi bonds. D: the methyl carbon has four sigma bonds and no pi bonds, so it requires four hybrid orbitals and is sp³.

**N-2.7-A-v2.** Acetaldehyde, H₃C–CH=O, has a methyl carbon bonded to a second carbon, which is bonded to one hydrogen atom and double bonded to oxygen. Which statement about its bonding is correct?

- A. It has 5 sigma bonds and 2 pi bonds, and the carbonyl carbon is sp hybridized.
- B. It has 6 sigma bonds and 1 pi bond, and the carbonyl carbon is sp³ hybridized.
- C. It has 7 sigma bonds and 0 pi bonds, because a double bond counts as two sigma bonds.
- **D. It has 6 sigma bonds and 1 pi bond, and the carbonyl carbon is sp² hybridized.**

Check: sigma bonds are 3 C–H (methyl), 1 C–C, 1 C–H and 1 C=O, a total of 6; the C=O double bond has 1 pi bond. The carbonyl carbon has 3 electron domains (C, H, O), so it is sp².
Rationales: A: it counts 5 sigma bonds and 2 pi bonds, treating the C=O as two pi bonds, and it assigns sp to a carbon with 3 domains. B: the sigma and pi counts are correct, but the carbonyl carbon has three electron domains, and sp³ would need four. C: a double bond is one sigma bond plus one pi bond, and not two sigma bonds, so the molecule has 6 sigma bonds and 1 pi bond.

**N-2.7-A-v3.** Acetonitrile, H₃C–C≡N, has a methyl carbon bonded to a carbon that has a triple bond to nitrogen. Which statement about its bonding is correct?

- A. It has 5 sigma bonds and 2 pi bonds, and the methyl carbon is sp hybridized.
- B. It has 4 sigma bonds and 3 pi bonds, and the nitrogen atom is sp hybridized.
- C. It has 5 sigma bonds and 2 pi bonds, and the nitrogen atom is sp³ hybridized.
- **D. It has 5 sigma bonds and 2 pi bonds, and the nitrogen atom is sp hybridized.**

Check: sigma bonds are 3 C–H, 1 C–C and 1 C≡N, a total of 5; pi bonds are 2. Nitrogen has 2 electron domains (one triple bond and one lone pair), so it is sp hybridized.
Rationales: A: the methyl carbon has four sigma bonds and no pi bonds, so it is sp³ and not sp. B: it counts only 4 sigma bonds and 3 pi bonds, which wrongly treats the triple bond as three pi bonds with no sigma bond. C: nitrogen has two electron domains, a bond domain and a lone pair, which needs 2 hybrid orbitals and so is sp and not sp³.

## Seed N-2.7-B (new) — Topic 2.7 VSEPR and Hybridization
**Skill:** use electron-domain repulsion, including the greater repulsion of lone pairs, to explain observed deviations in bond angle.

The H–X–H bond angles are about 109.5° in CH₄, 107° in NH₃ and 104.5° in H₂O. In each molecule the central atom has four electron domains. Which explanation best accounts for the decrease?

- A. Nitrogen and oxygen are more electronegative than carbon, so they pull the hydrogen atoms closer together.
- B. Lone pairs are attracted to the hydrogen nuclei and pull the hydrogen atoms toward each other.
- **C. A lone pair repels neighboring electron domains more strongly than a bonding pair does, so as lone pairs replace bonding pairs the bonding pairs are pushed closer together.**
- D. The central atoms get smaller from carbon to oxygen, so the hydrogen atoms crowd together and the angle must narrow.

Check: all three have tetrahedral electron-domain geometry (ideal 109.5°); NH₃ has 1 lone pair and H₂O has 2, and the lone pair-bonding pair repulsion exceeds the bonding pair-bonding pair repulsion, compressing the angle.
Rationales: A: electronegativity changes bond polarity but does not account for the angle trend through the order of repulsions between domains. B: lone pairs do not attract the hydrogen nuclei, and the angle decreases because the lone pair repels the bonding pairs. D: the H–X–H angle in VSEPR is set by repulsions among electron domains and not by the size of the central atom.

**N-2.7-B-v1.** In SF₆ every F–S–F angle between adjacent fluorine atoms is 90°. In BrF₅, which also has six electron domains around the central atom, the angle between the axial fluorine and each basal fluorine is about 85°. Which explanation best accounts for the smaller angle in BrF₅?

- A. Bromine is larger than sulfur, so the fluorine atoms are forced closer together.
- **B. The lone pair on bromine repels the bonding pairs more strongly than they repel one another, pushing the fluorine atoms away from the lone pair and closer to the axial fluorine.**
- C. Fluorine is more electronegative in BrF₅ than in SF₆, so the F atoms pull toward each other.
- D. The lone pair on bromine attracts the fluorine atoms, which pulls them toward the lone pair and increases the angle.

Check: both have octahedral electron-domain geometry; BrF₅ has one lone pair, which repels bonding pairs more strongly than they repel each other and bends the F atoms away from it.
Rationales: A: the angle change is explained by repulsion of electron domains and not by the size of the central atom. C: the fluorine atoms are the same element in both species and the bond polarity does not set the angle in VSEPR. D: lone pairs repel, not attract, the bonding pairs, and the observed angle gets smaller, not larger.

**N-2.7-B-v2.** In the trigonal bipyramidal electron-domain geometry, the ideal angle between two equatorial positions is 120°. In SF₄, which has this electron-domain geometry, the equatorial F–S–F angle is about 102°. Which explanation best accounts for the smaller angle?

- **A. The lone pair sits in an equatorial position and repels the two equatorial bonding pairs more strongly than they repel each other, pushing them closer together.**
- B. Sulfur has 34 valence electrons, which compresses every bond angle by the same amount.
- C. The lone pair attracts the equatorial fluorine atoms and pulls them toward each other.
- D. Fluorine atoms are small, so they can approach each other more closely than the bonding pairs would allow.

Check: SF₄ has 5 electron domains (4 bonds, 1 lone pair); the lone pair repels bonding pairs more strongly than they repel one another, so the equatorial angle falls below 120°.
Rationales: B: sulfur has 6 valence electrons and the molecule has 34 in total, and the number of electrons does not equally compress the angles of the molecule. C: a lone pair repels bonding pairs, so it pushes the fluorine atoms away from it and does not pull them. D: bond angles in VSEPR come from repulsions between electron domains and not from the sizes of the outer atoms.

**N-2.7-B-v3.** The O–S–O bond angle is about 109.5° in the sulfate ion, SO₄²⁻, and about 106° in the sulfite ion, SO₃²⁻, in Lewis diagrams that give sulfur an octet in both ions. Which explanation best accounts for the smaller angle in SO₃²⁻?

- A. SO₃²⁻ has fewer oxygen atoms, so there is less space for them and they must be closer together.
- B. The extra negative charge of SO₃²⁻ attracts the oxygen atoms to each other.
- C. Sulfite sulfur is bonded to oxygen by double bonds, which are shorter and so pull the oxygen atoms together.
- **D. Sulfur in SO₃²⁻ has a lone pair that repels the three bonding pairs more strongly than they repel one another, compressing the O–S–O angle.**

Check: SO₄²⁻ has 4 bonding domains and no lone pair (tetrahedral, 109.5°); SO₃²⁻ has 3 bonding domains and 1 lone pair (trigonal pyramidal), and the lone pair compresses the angles below 109.5°.
Rationales: A: having fewer oxygen atoms would leave more room around sulfur and not less, and the angle changes because of the lone pair. B: negative charge does not attract oxygen atoms to each other, and they carry partial negative charges that repel each other. C: in the octet-rule diagram of SO₃²⁻ all S–O bonds are single, and bond length is not what sets the angle.

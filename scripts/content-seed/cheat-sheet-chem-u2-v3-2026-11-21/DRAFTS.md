## 2.1 Types of Chemical Bonds

**Know: core_idea**
```
- Bonding is one continuum: unequal sharing generally grows with the electronegativity difference, from nonpolar to ionic.
- No cutoff separates the types; every polar bond has some ionic character.
- C–H is treated as effectively nonpolar.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
What to lean on when classifying:

| Evidence | Points toward |
| Metal–nonmetal pair | Generally ionic (a shortcut only) |
| Nonmetal–nonmetal pair | Generally covalent |
| Given properties, e.g. molten conductivity | Decide the call over the shortcut |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Subtract the electronegativities; the more electronegative atom takes δ⁻.
2. Place the bond on the continuum (a larger difference is more polar, more ionic), letting any given properties outrank the metal/nonmetal shortcut.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “C–H and O–H are both covalent and NaCl is ionic, so C–H and O–H behave the same and NaCl is a different kind of bond.” It skips step 1 and breaks step 2: it treats bonding as separate boxes, not a continuum.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) C–H: 2.55 − 2.20 ≈ 0.35; O–H: 3.44 − 2.20 ≈ 1.24, δ⁻ on O; Na–Cl: 3.16 − 0.93 ≈ 2.23. (2) Ionic character rises C–H < O–H < Na–Cl; Na–Cl is classified ionic but still retains some covalent character.
```

## 2.2 Intramolecular Force and Potential Energy

**Know: core_idea**
```
- On a potential energy vs internuclear distance graph, the minimum's x-value is the bond length; the well depth below the separated-atom baseline (zero) is the bond energy.
- For the same pair of elements, higher bond order generally gives a shorter bond and a larger bond energy together.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
- Each added bond puts more shared electron density between the nuclei: they sit closer and the well is deeper.
- For ion pairs, strength rises with larger charges and with smaller ions (shorter distance); cite both, never one alone.
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Get each bond's length and energy, from given data or from a curve (minimum's x-value, well depth).
2. Rank length and energy together by bond order: shorter goes with larger energy.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “The triple bond is strongest because it has three bonds instead of one, so there's more holding the atoms together.” It skips steps 1 and 2: no length or energy is used, and length is never tied to energy.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) Given data: C–C 154 pm, 347 kJ/mol; C=C 134 pm, 614 kJ/mol; C≡C 120 pm, 839 kJ/mol. (2) Higher bond order moves the minimum to a shorter distance and makes the well deeper, so shorter length and larger energy rise together as one consequence of bond order.
```

## 2.3 Structure of Ionic Solids

**Know: core_idea**
```
- An ionic solid is one extended, repeating 3-D array of alternating cations and anions that maximizes attraction and minimizes repulsion, not discrete molecules or ion pairs.
- Not tested: knowledge of specific crystal structures.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Each property traces to the alternating array:

| Property | Cause |
| High melting point | Many strong attractions throughout take much energy to overcome |
| Brittle | A shifted layer lines up like charges; repulsion cracks the crystal |
| Conducts only when molten or dissolved | Ions are locked in place in the solid |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Say the solid is one extended array of alternating charges.
2. Pick the property asked and give its cause from the array.
3. Name the Coulombic force at work (attractions overcome, or like-charge repulsion); skip crystal-type names.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Student A names the actual crystal structure, so it earns more credit than Student B's layer-shifting answer.” It breaks steps 2 and 3: a crystal name is not assessed and gives no cause of brittleness.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) The solid is one extended lattice of alternating ions. (2) Shifting one layer lines like-charged ions up. (3) The Coulombic repulsion overcomes the attractions along that plane, so the crystal fractures; naming rock-salt or face-centered cubic is not required.
```

```
Metals in 2.4 deform rather than fracture because delocalized electrons hold shifted layers together; Unit 3 keeps building properties from structure.
```

## 2.4 Structure of Metals and Alloys

**Know: core_idea**
```
- A metal is an array of positive ions in a sea of delocalized valence electrons, mobile throughout the solid: that is why it conducts.
- Layers slide while the sea holds them together: malleable.
- Interstitial atoms block sliding (harder, less malleable), yet the alloy still conducts.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Radius comparison, not the metals' identity, classifies an alloy:

| | Interstitial | Substitutional |
| Radii | Significantly different | Comparable |
| Second atom | Fills gaps | Replaces at lattice sites |
| Example | Steel (C in Fe) | Brass (Zn for Cu) |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Compare the two atomic radii.
2. Very different: interstitial (the small atom fills gaps); comparable: substitutional (one atom replaces the other).
3. Cite the radius comparison as the reason.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Steel and brass are both alloys, so they're the same type.” It skips steps 1 and 3: with no radius comparison it cannot tell the types apart.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) Carbon is much smaller than iron; zinc's radius is comparable to copper's. (2) Steel is interstitial (C in gaps between Fe atoms); brass is substitutional (Zn replaces Cu at lattice positions). (3) The radius comparison decides, not both being alloys; steel still conducts because mobile delocalized electrons remain.
```

## 2.5 Lewis Diagrams

**Know: core_idea**
```
- A Lewis diagram is valence-electron bookkeeping by a fixed procedure: the electrons you draw must equal the total.
- Meet octets where the count allows (H has two); a central atom still short of an octet may take a lone pair as an extra bond.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
The charge sets the total and the brackets:

| Species | Valence-electron total | Diagram |
| Neutral | Sum over the atoms | No brackets |
| Anion | Sum + 1 per unit of negative charge | Brackets, charge outside |
| Cation | Sum − 1 per unit of positive charge | Brackets, charge outside |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Write the charge-adjusted valence-electron total as a number.
2. Draw bonds, then lone pairs, to meet octets.
3. Count the electrons drawn and confirm they equal the total; bracket an ion with its charge outside.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Carbon has 4 and each oxygen has 6, so 4 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 22 electrons total.” It breaks step 1: the 2− charge is ignored, so the total is 2 short and every later count inherits the error.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) CO₃²⁻: C 4 + 3 × O 6 + 2 for the 2− = 24. (2) With C central, one C=O and two C–O bonds use 8 electrons; the other 16 are lone pairs (2 on the double-bonded O, 3 on each single-bonded O). (3) 8 + 16 = 24, every atom has an octet; bracket the diagram with 2− outside.
```

## 2.6 Resonance and Formal Charge

**Know: core_idea**
```
- Formal charge = valence electrons − lone-pair electrons − half the bonding electrons.
- Equivalent structures: resonance, one averaged structure. Nonequivalent: the octet rule and formal charge choose.
- The Lewis model has limits, especially for odd-electron species.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Averaging is measurable. Nitrite, NO₂⁻, has N–O bond order (1 + 2)/2 = 1.5:

| N–O bond | Bond order | Length |
| Single | 1 | Longest |
| Nitrite, both bonds | 1.5 | In between |
| Double | 2 | Shortest |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Decide whether the candidate structures are equivalent.
2. Equivalent: draw every form and state one averaged structure, never switching. Nonequivalent: check octets, compute formal charge, and prefer formal charges nearest zero, with negative on the more electronegative atom.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “The ion rapidly flips between the two structures, so at any instant one N–O bond is double and the other single.” It breaks step 2: resonance forms are one averaged structure, not successive states.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) The two nitrite forms (N=O on the left or the right oxygen) are equivalent. (2) The real ion is one averaged structure, not a switching one: each N–O bond has order 1.5, so the two bonds are identical and equal in length.
```

## 2.7 VSEPR and Hybridization

**Know: core_idea**
```
- Count regions of electron density: each lone pair, or bond of any order, is one.
- Name geometry from atom positions only; actual angles can differ from ideal.
- Not assessed: deriving or depicting hybrid orbitals; d-orbital hybridization (over four pairs, give the shape only); molecular orbital diagrams.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Regions set the ideal angle and the label (labels stop at sp³):

| Regions | Ideal angle | Hybridization |
| 2 | 180° | sp |
| 3 | 120° | sp² |
| 4 | 109.5° | sp³ |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Count regions around the central atom; a double or triple bond is one.
2. Name the geometry from atom positions only, with its angle.
3. Give sp, sp² or sp³ from the same count only if asked.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Carbon is attached to two hydrogens plus two separate bonds to oxygen, so it has four domains and is sp³.” It breaks step 1: the C=O double bond is one region, so carbon has three.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) Formaldehyde's carbon has two C–H bonds and one C=O, which counts once: three regions. (2) With no lone pair, the geometry is trigonal planar, about 120°. (3) Three regions means sp², not sp³.
```

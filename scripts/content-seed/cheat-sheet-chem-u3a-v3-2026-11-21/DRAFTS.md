## 3.1 Intermolecular and Interparticle Forces

**Know: core_idea**
```
- Comparable size, roughly weakest to strongest: London dispersion (all particles), dipole-dipole (polar), hydrogen bonding (H on N, O or F, to a nearby N, O or F). Ion-dipole is typically stronger than dipole-dipole.
- London forces are often the strongest net force between very large molecules.
- "London dispersion" is not a synonym for "van der Waals".
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
For molecules of comparable size, structure points to the strongest force:

| Structure shows | Typically the strongest force |
| Nonpolar, no ions | London dispersion |
| Polar, no H on N, O or F | Dipole-dipole, plus London |
| H on N, O or F | Hydrogen bonding, plus the rest |
| Ion with polar molecule | Ion-dipole |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Read the structure: polarity, ions, H on N, O or F.
2. Name the strongest force present and cite that evidence.
3. Compare by force type, allowing for size, not by molar mass alone.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Chloromethane has more mass than methane so it boils higher, and methanol has an OH group so it boils higher too.” It skips steps 2 and 3: it reasons from mass and a group name, not from a named force and its structural evidence.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) CH₄ is symmetric and nonpolar; CH₃Cl has a polar C–Cl bond in an asymmetric shape; CH₃OH has an O–H bond. (2) Strongest force: CH₄ London dispersion only; CH₃Cl dipole-dipole; CH₃OH hydrogen bonding. (3) Boiling points (−161.5, −24.2, 64.7 °C) follow that order, not molar mass (16, 50, 32).
```

## 3.2 Properties of Solids

**Know: core_idea**
```
- Vaporization fully overcomes IMFs, so vapor pressure and boiling point track IMF strength (stronger IMFs generally mean lower vapor pressure and higher boiling point); melting only rearranges particles, so melting point tracks it more loosely.
- Covalent network solids: 3-D networks (diamond) are rigid and hard; layered ones (graphite) are soft.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
| Solid | Held by | Typical properties |
| Ionic | Ion attraction | Brittle, high melting point; conducts only if ions move |
| Covalent network | Covalent bonds | High melting point; rigid unless layered |
| Molecular | Weak IMFs | Low melting point; no conduction |
| Metallic | Free valence electrons | Conducts, malleable, ductile |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Classify the solid by its particles and what holds them.
2. Name what must break, move or flow for the property asked.
3. Explain from that structure, not from the class name alone.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Both are covalent network solids with covalent bonds throughout, so they should have the same hardness.” It skips steps 2 and 3: it stops at the class name and ignores that graphite's layers are held only by weak forces.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) Both are carbon network solids: diamond is a 3-D network (four covalent neighbors per carbon), graphite is flat layers (three neighbors). (2) Moving a layer in diamond breaks strong covalent bonds; graphite's layers are held only by weak London dispersion forces. (3) So diamond is extremely hard and graphite is soft: the property depends on which bonds must break.
```

## 3.3 Solids, Liquids, and Gases

**Know: core_idea**
```
- Crystalline solid: regular repeating 3-D arrangement; amorphous solid: irregular arrangement. Both are solids.
- Not assessed: understanding or interpreting phase diagrams.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
| Phase | Spacing | Motion | Order |
| Solid | Close contact | Limited, no overall translation | Regular if crystalline, irregular if amorphous |
| Liquid | Close contact, molar volume typically similar to solid | Continually moving and colliding | Depends on the forces between particles |
| Gas | Far apart (set by T, P, V) | Constant | No fixed order |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Name the phases being compared.
2. For each, state spacing, motion and order in particle terms.
3. Tie any volume difference to spacing and motion, not to phase-diagram features.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Water vapor takes up more room because gases are less dense than liquids or solids.” It skips steps 2 and 3: density restates the observation without saying anything about particle spacing or motion.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) Ice (about 19.6 mL/mol), liquid water (about 18) and vapor (22,400: the ideal-gas molar volume at STP, a hypothetical value for water). (2) Ice: ordered lattice, close contact, limited motion; liquid: disordered, still close, continually moving and colliding; vapor: far apart, independent, constantly moving, no fixed order. (3) Solid and liquid stay near 18 to 20 mL/mol because IMFs keep molecules in close contact; vapor is over a thousand times larger because of spacing and motion.

## 3.4 Ideal Gas Law

**Know: core_idea**
```
- PV = nRT, with R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K): P in atm, V in L, T in K.
- Partial pressure of A = total pressure × mole fraction of A; total pressure = sum of the partial pressures.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Choose the route from the data given:

| Given | Route |
| P, V, n, T of one gas | PV = nRT |
| Moles of each gas, plus V and T | Add moles, get total P from PV = nRT, then mole fraction × total P for each gas |
| Partial pressures | Add them for total P |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Choose the equation: PV = nRT, or mole fraction and Dalton's law for a mixture.
2. Convert every quantity to match R (kelvin, atm, L) before any arithmetic.
3. For each gas in a mixture, find its mole fraction, then multiply by total pressure.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Total moles are 0.70, so plug into PV = nRT with T = 27, the Celsius value.” It skips step 2: R needs kelvin, so the temperature must be 300 K.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) Mixture: PV = nRT gives total pressure, then mole fractions give partials. (2) T = 300 K (already kelvin), V = 10.0 L, n(total) = 0.50 + 0.20 = 0.70 mol: P(total) = (0.70)(0.08206)(300) ÷ 10.0 = 1.72, so 1.7 atm to two significant figures. (3) N₂: 0.50 ÷ 0.70 = 0.714, × 1.72 = 1.23, so 1.2 atm; O₂: 0.286 × 1.72 = 0.49 atm. The partials add to 1.7 atm.
```

## 3.5 Kinetic Molecular Theory

**Know: core_idea**
```
- KE = ½mv². Kelvin temperature is proportional to the average KE of the particles, not to their average speed.
- At the same temperature, different gases share the same average KE, so the lighter gas moves faster on average.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
| Comparison | Average KE | Average speed | Maxwell-Boltzmann curve |
| Same gas, higher T | Greater | Greater | Shifts to higher speeds, broadens |
| Lighter gas, same T | Same | Greater | Shifts to higher speeds, broadens |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. State first: same temperature, same average KE.
2. Use KE = ½mv² to turn the mass difference into a speed difference.
3. Describe how the two speed distributions differ.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Xenon atoms are much heavier, so they have greater average kinetic energy than helium atoms at the same temperature.” It breaks step 1: average KE depends on temperature, not on mass.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) At 300 K, He and Xe have the same average KE. (2) KE = ½mv², so He (4.00 g/mol) must move much faster than Xe (131.3 g/mol) to have that same KE. (3) He's Maxwell-Boltzmann curve is shifted to higher speeds and is broader than Xe's.
```

## 3.6 Deviation from Ideal Gas Law

**Know: core_idea**
```
- The ideal gas law assumes no attractions between particles and negligible particle volume.
- Deviation is generally largest, not smallest, at low temperature (near condensation) and at very high pressure.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
| Condition | Mechanism | Effect |
| Low T, near condensation | Attractions between slow particles | Pressure lower than PV = nRT predicts |
| Very high P | Particle volume is a real fraction of the container | Less free space than the ideal model assumes |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Read the condition: low temperature near condensation, or very high pressure.
2. Name its mechanism: attractions, or particle volume.
3. State the effect, and that deviation is generally largest here.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Gases behave most ideally at high pressure and low temperature since those are extreme, controlled conditions.” It breaks step 3: it inverts the result and names no mechanism.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) One gas is cooled to near condensation; the other is compressed to very high pressure. (2) Cooled gas: attractions between slow particles reduce the force on the walls, so measured pressure is below the ideal prediction. Compressed gas: the particles' own volume leaves less free space than the ideal model assumes. (3) Both are conditions where deviation is generally largest, not smallest.
```

```
The attractions are the forces ranked in 3.1, and the low-temperature effect rests on the slower particles of 3.5.

## 3.7 Solutions and Mixtures

**Know: core_idea**
```
- Solution (homogeneous): properties are uniform throughout the sample. Heterogeneous mixture: properties vary by location.
- Solutions can be solid (some alloys), liquid or gas (air).
- Molarity = moles of solute ÷ liters of solution.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Which number goes under the division:

| The problem gives | Do |
| Final solution volume | Use it (in liters) |
| Solvent volume added | Do not substitute it for the final volume; they can differ |
| Mass of solute | Convert to moles first |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Classify: uniform throughout is a solution; varying by location is heterogeneous.
2. Find moles of solute and the final solution volume in liters.
3. Divide; never use the solvent volume measured beforehand.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “M = 0.250 mol ÷ 0.500 L = 0.500 M either way, since 500 mL of water plus the NaCl is basically 500 mL of solution.” It breaks step 3: it treats solvent volume as solution volume, so the second case need not be 0.500 M.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) NaCl dissolved in water is uniform throughout, so it is a solution. (2) n = 0.250 mol; final volume 500. mL = 0.500 L. (3) M = 0.250 mol ÷ 0.500 L = 0.500 M. Starting from 500. mL of water gives a final volume that is not exactly 500. mL (the solute adds volume), so that molarity differs slightly from 0.500 M.
```

## 5.1 Reaction Rates

**Know: core_idea**
```
- Reaction rate = a species' rate of disappearance or appearance (a positive magnitude) ÷ its coefficient.
- Concentration, temperature, surface area, catalyst and other environment can change the rate.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Species rates differ only by their coefficients:

| Species in the balanced equation | Rate of change of concentration |
| Reactant, coefficient a | disappears at a × reaction rate |
| Product, coefficient b | appears at b × reaction rate |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Identify the species whose rate is given and the species asked about.
2. Divide the given species' rate by its coefficient to get the reaction rate.
3. Multiply by the asked species' coefficient.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “B increases at 0.20 M/s because the reaction happens at one rate.” It skips step 2: it never divides by the coefficient, so it treats the two species' rates as equal.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) For 2A → B, A falls at 0.20 M/s (coefficient 2); B is asked (coefficient 1). (2) Reaction rate = 0.20 M/s ÷ 2 = 0.10 M/s. (3) B rises at 1 × 0.10 M/s = 0.10 M/s.
```

## 5.2 Introduction to Rate Law

**Know: core_idea**
```
- rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ; the orders m and n come from experimental data; overall-equation coefficients do not generally give them.
- Overall order = m + n.
- Units of k follow the overall order: M s⁻¹ (0), s⁻¹ (1), M⁻¹ s⁻¹ (2).
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Double one concentration, others constant:

| Rate changes by | Order in that reactant |
| ×1 (no change) | 0 |
| ×2 | 1 |
| ×4 | 2 |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Pick two trials where only one concentration changes.
2. Solve rate factor = (concentration factor)^order for that reactant; repeat for each reactant, then write the rate law.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “First order, because A doubled and the rate changed.” It skips step 2: a change in rate alone does not give the order; the rate factor must be compared with the concentration factor.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) Only [A] changes: it doubles (×2) and the rate quadruples (×4). (2) 4 = 2ᵐ, so m = 2: second order in A (rate ∝ [A]²).
```

## 5.3 Concentration Changes over Time

**Know: core_idea**
```
- The order is the one whose plot ([A], ln[A] or 1/[A] against t) is linear.
- First order only: constant half-life, t½ = 0.693/k; radioactive decay is first order.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
| Linear plot | Order | Slope |
| [A] vs t | zero | −k |
| ln[A] vs t | first | −k |
| 1/[A] vs t | second | +k |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Choose the plot that is linear to get the order.
2. Take k from that plot's slope, using the matching integrated rate law.
3. Use t½ = 0.693/k only if the order is first.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Second order because concentration is changing with time.” It skips step 1: concentration changes in every order; only which plot is linear identifies it.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (Illustrative numbers, chosen for this model answer.) (1) ln[A] vs t is linear with negative slope: first order. (2) ln[A]ₜ − ln[A]₀ = −kt, so slope = −k; for a slope of −0.0231 s⁻¹, k = 0.0231 s⁻¹. (3) t½ = 0.693 ÷ 0.0231 s⁻¹ = 30.0 s.
```

```

```

## 5.4 Elementary Reactions

**Know: core_idea**
```
- An elementary reaction is a single molecular event.
- Collisions of three or more particles at once are rare.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Does the coefficient shortcut apply?

| Equation | Powers in the rate law |
| One elementary step, A + B → products | Reactant coefficients: rate = k[A][B] |
| Overall equation of a multistep mechanism | Cannot be read from the overall coefficients (use data, or the mechanism: 5.8 gives the slow-step shortcut where it applies) |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Confirm the equation is a single elementary step.
2. Write rate = k × each reactant concentration raised to its coefficient; products never appear.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Only CO₂ appears because it is a product of the step.” It breaks step 2: the step rate law follows the colliding reactants, never the products.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) NO₂ + CO → NO + CO₂ is given as one elementary step. (2) Reactants NO₂ and CO each have coefficient 1, so rate = k[NO₂][CO].
```

## 5.5 Collision Model

**Know: core_idea**
```
- A successful collision needs (a) energy at or above the activation energy and (b) a suitable orientation.
- In most reactions only a small fraction of collisions react.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Other things equal:

| Change | Effect on successful collisions |
| Higher temperature | larger fraction at or above activation energy |
| Higher concentration or more exposed surface area | can increase collisions per second |
| Catalyst | lower barrier or more effective collisions |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Name the factor that changed.
2. Say which requirement it affects: collision frequency, fraction at or above activation energy, or orientation.
3. Conclude that successful collisions per second change, so the rate changes.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Because particles collide more often, and that is the only effect of temperature.” It applies step 2 incompletely: temperature also enlarges the fraction of particles at or above the activation energy.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) Temperature rises. (2) The energy distribution shifts so a larger fraction of particles has energy at or above activation energy (collision frequency may also rise). (3) More collisions succeed per second, so the rate increases.
```

## 5.6 Reaction Energy Profile

**Know: core_idea**
```
- Profile: reactants → transition state (peak) → products.
- Forward Eₐ = peak − reactants; reverse Eₐ = peak − products; ΔE = products − reactants.
- Not tested: Arrhenius calculations (qualitative reasoning is).
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
| Feature on the diagram | What it tells you |
| Peak height above reactants | the barrier (Eₐ) that affects rate |
| Products below reactants | ΔE negative: exothermic |
| Products above reactants | ΔE positive: endothermic |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Label reactants, transition state (peak) and products.
2. Measure Eₐ up from the starting level to the peak.
3. Measure the overall change as products − reactants; its sign gives exothermic or endothermic.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “The activation energy is negative because products are lower.” It mixes up steps 2 and 3: the products-minus-reactants sign belongs to ΔE; Eₐ is measured up to the peak.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) The peak is the transition state; products sit below reactants. (2) Eₐ = peak − reactants, a positive barrier. (3) ΔE = products − reactants < 0, so the reaction is exothermic.
```

## 5.7 Introduction to Reaction Mechanisms

**Know: core_idea**
```
- A mechanism is a set of elementary steps; added together they must match the overall equation.
- A species on both sides of the sum in equal amounts cancels and is absent from the overall equation.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Classify a canceled species by the order of the steps:

| Species | Pattern across steps |
| Intermediate | produced first, consumed later |
| Catalyst | consumed first, regenerated later |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Add the steps, cancel species on both sides, and check the sum matches the overall equation.
2. For each canceled species, read the steps in order to decide intermediate or catalyst.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “It is a catalyst because it cancels out of the overall equation.” It skips step 2: intermediates and catalysts both cancel; the order of production and consumption decides.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) The species is absent from the overall equation, so it cancels. (2) It is produced in step 1 and consumed in step 2: an intermediate (a catalyst would be consumed first and regenerated later).
```


## 5.8 Reaction Mechanism and Rate Law

**Know: core_idea**
```
- If every step is irreversible or the first step is slow, the slow (rate-determining) step sets the rate law.
- That step is elementary, so its reactant coefficients give its rate law; the overall equation's coefficients do not.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Check what the slow step contains:

| Slow-step reactants | Rate law |
| Only species from the overall reaction | write it directly from the step |
| Includes an intermediate | rewrite the intermediate first (5.9) |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Identify the slow step.
2. Write rate = k × that step's reactant concentrations, each to its coefficient.
3. Check that no intermediate remains in the law.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Use the overall reaction coefficients instead of the slow step.” It breaks step 2: the powers come from the slow elementary step's reactants, not the overall equation.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) Step 1 is slow: A + B → C. (2) Its reactants set the law: rate = k[A][B]. (3) The law contains only A and B, the step's reactants; if either were an intermediate, 5.9 would apply.
```

**Link (practice_bridge)**
```
5.10 reads the slow step off an energy profile as generally the largest barrier.
```

## 5.9 Pre-Equilibrium Approximation

**Know: core_idea**
```
- Intermediates should not usually appear in the observed rate law.
- A fast reversible step before the slow step gives an equilibrium expression that rewrites the intermediate in terms of earlier reactants.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Illustrative mechanism (overall A + B → P), in mechanism order:

| Step | Result |
| 1 fast: A ⇌ I | K = [I]/[A], so [I] = K[A] |
| 2 slow: I + B → P | rate = k[I][B] |
| Substitute | rate = kK[A][B] |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Write the slow-step rate law.
2. Write the pre-equilibrium expression and solve for the intermediate.
3. Substitute it so the law contains only reactants.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “No relationship is needed because intermediates can always appear in final rate laws.” It skips step 2: without the equilibrium expression the intermediate stays in the law.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) Slow step I + B → P: rate = k[I][B], which contains the intermediate I. (2) Fast step A ⇌ I: K = [I]/[A], so [I] = K[A]. (3) Substituting: rate = kK[A][B], in terms of reactants that can be controlled or measured.
```

## 5.10 Multistep Reaction Energy Profile

**Know: core_idea**
```
- A multistep profile has one peak (transition state) per elementary step.
- Each well between two peaks is an intermediate.
- Each step's Eₐ runs from that step's starting level up to its peak.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
| Feature | Reading |
| n peaks | n elementary steps, n − 1 intermediates |
| Largest barrier | usually the slowest step |
| Final valley | the products, not an intermediate |
| Reactants to products | overall ΔE, not a step barrier |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Count peaks (steps) and the wells between them (intermediates).
2. Measure each step's barrier from its own starting level to its peak.
3. Link the largest barrier (each measured from its own starting level) to the usually slowest step, not the overall ΔE.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “It is the final product because it is lower than the first peak.” It breaks step 1: the products are the final level; a well between two peaks is an intermediate.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) Two peaks mean two elementary steps; the valley between them is an intermediate, formed in step 1 and consumed in step 2. (2) Compare the two barriers, each from its own starting level. (3) The larger barrier usually marks the slow step.
```

## 5.11 Catalysis

**Know: core_idea**
```
- A catalyst provides a different pathway and is regenerated, so it is not consumed overall.
- It must increase effective collisions and/or lower the activation energy.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
| Claim | Evidence to cite |
| Catalyst | consumed in one step, regenerated in a later step |
| Intermediate | produced in one step, consumed in a later step |
| "Lowers Eₐ" only | not enough to identify a catalyst |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Cite the step where the species is consumed and the later step where it is regenerated.
2. Distinguish it from an intermediate (produced first, consumed later).
3. Connect the alternate pathway to the increased rate.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “X is a catalyst because catalysts lower activation energy and X is not in the overall reaction.” It skips step 1: no step shows X consumed and then regenerated.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) X is consumed in step 1 and regenerated in step 2. (2) So it is not consumed overall; an intermediate is instead produced first and consumed later. (3) The alternate pathway lowers the barrier or raises effective collisions, so the rate rises.
```

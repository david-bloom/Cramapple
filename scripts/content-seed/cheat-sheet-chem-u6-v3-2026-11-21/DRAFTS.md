## 6.1 Endothermic and Exothermic Processes

**Know: core_idea**
```
- Name the system first; the surroundings are everything else.
- Exothermic: system energy decreases, energy goes to the surroundings; ΔH < 0 at constant pressure.
- Endothermic: system energy increases, energy comes from the surroundings; ΔH > 0.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Dissolving can go either way, depending on the attractions before and after mixing (the energy balance, not one attraction alone):

| Energy released by new attractions vs energy needed to break old ones | Dissolving is | Surroundings |
| Released more | Exothermic | May warm |
| Released less | Endothermic | May cool |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Name the system and the surroundings.
2. Track which way the energy moves.
3. Assign endothermic or exothermic to the process, not to a vague temperature remark.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Exothermic, because the beaker loses warmth and cold is released.” It skips steps 1 and 2: no system is named and the energy direction is wrong.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) The dissolving process is the system; the beaker and water are the surroundings. (2) The beaker feels cold, so energy flows from the surroundings into the system. (3) The process is endothermic.
```

## 6.2 Energy Diagrams

**Know: core_idea**
```
- The vertical axis is the system's energy; compare the final level with the initial level.
- Products below reactants: exothermic, ΔH < 0. Products above reactants: endothermic, ΔH > 0.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Two different heights on one diagram:

| Distance | Tells you |
| Reactant level to product level | Overall energy change, ΔH, and its sign |
| Reactant level to the peak | Activation energy, not ΔH |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Label the reactant (initial) and product (final) levels.
2. Compare product level with reactant level to find the sign of ΔH.
3. Name the process; keep any barrier height separate from ΔH.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Negative, because the reaction went forward on the diagram.” It skips step 2: arrow direction is not the comparison of product and reactant levels.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) Reactants sit low; products sit higher. (2) Products are above reactants, so the system absorbed energy overall and ΔH is positive. (3) The process is endothermic; any barrier shown (reactant level to the peak) is activation energy, a separate quantity.
```

## 6.3 Heat Transfer and Thermal Equilibrium

**Know: core_idea**
```
- Heat is energy transferred by particle collisions, from the warmer sample to the cooler one.
- Thermal equilibrium: equal temperature, which means equal average particle kinetic energy.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Temperature and total thermal energy are different quantities:

| Quantity | Depends on |
| Temperature | Average kinetic energy of the particles |
| Total thermal energy | Average kinetic energy, amount of matter and its composition |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Say which sample is warmer; energy moves from it to the cooler sample.
2. Explain by collisions: the warmer sample's average kinetic energy falls, the cooler one's rises.
3. State equilibrium as equal temperature, not equal total thermal energy.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Yes, because equal temperature means equal energy.” It breaks step 3: equal temperature means equal average kinetic energy, not equal total thermal energy.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) Energy moves from the small warm sample to the large cool one. (2) Collisions lower the warm sample's average kinetic energy and raise the cool sample's. (3) At the same final temperature the average kinetic energy is equal, but total thermal energy also depends on amount and heat capacity, so the samples need not contain equal thermal energy.
```

## 6.4 Heat Capacity and Calorimetry

**Know: core_idea**
```
- q = mcΔT with ΔT = T(final) − T(initial); q > 0 means heat absorbed, q < 0 released.
- Energy is conserved: q(system) = −q(surroundings).
- Equal heat given to equal masses with different specific heat capacities gives different ΔT.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
In solution calorimetry the measured object is the solution:

| Item | Use |
| m in q = mcΔT | Mass of the solution warmed (usually total solution mass), not just the solute |
| q = mcΔT | Heat gained or lost by the solution (surroundings) |
| Reacting system | Opposite sign, taking the solution as the only absorber of heat |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Choose m (the solution's mass, not just the solute), c and ΔT = final − initial.
2. Calculate q for the solution; its sign follows ΔT.
3. Flip the sign for the reacting system; round to the data's significant figures.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Use 0.10 g because that is the reacting solid.” It breaks step 1: q = mcΔT describes the warmed solution, so m is the solution's mass.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) m = 100.0 g + 0.10 g = 100.1 g; with c = 4.18 J/(g·°C) and ΔT = +2.0 °C assumed for this example. (2) q(solution) = 100.1 × 4.18 × 2.0 ≈ +8.4×10² J. (3) q(system) = −q(solution) ≈ −8.4×10² J: the reaction released heat.
```

## 6.5 Energy of Phase Changes

**Know: core_idea**
```
- Sloped heating-curve region: temperature changes; use q = mcΔT.
- Plateau: temperature is constant during a pure substance's phase change; use q = nΔH(molar).
- A complementary pair has ΔH equal in size and opposite in sign: ΔH(condensation) = −ΔH(vaporization).
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Direction fixes the sign:

| Phase change | Energy | ΔH |
| Melting, vaporization | Absorbed | Positive |
| Freezing, condensation | Released | Negative |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Decide: sloped region or plateau?
2. On a plateau use the phase-change enthalpy, not q = mcΔT, and set the sign from the direction.
3. Explain constant temperature through particle separation.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Because no energy is being added during boiling.” It breaks step 3: energy is being added; the reason temperature stays constant is where it goes.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) Constant temperature during boiling marks a plateau, so q = mcΔT does not apply. (2) Boiling absorbs energy, so ΔH(vaporization) is positive. (3) The added energy separates particles into the gas phase instead of raising their average kinetic energy, so temperature stays constant.
```

## 6.6 Introduction to Enthalpy of Reaction

**Know: core_idea**
```
- At constant pressure, ΔH(reaction) is the heat the reacting system absorbs (+) or releases (−).
- Per mole: ΔH = q(system) ÷ n (moles of reaction).
- Not tested: technical distinctions between enthalpy and internal energy.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Read the solution's temperature, then flip to the reaction:

| Solution temperature | Solution (surroundings) | Reaction (system) | ΔH |
| Rises | Gained heat | Released heat | Negative |
| Falls | Lost heat | Absorbed heat | Positive |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Link the observation to heat flow into or out of the solution.
2. Flip the sign for the reaction: q(reaction) = −q(solution).
3. If asked per mole, divide q(reaction) by moles.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Positive, because the temperature increased.” It skips step 2: the warming belongs to the solution; the reacting system's sign is opposite.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) The solution's temperature rose, so the solution gained heat. (2) The reacting system lost that heat, so q(reaction) is negative: exothermic, ΔH negative. (3) Per mole: if q(solution) = +8.4×10² J came from 0.0100 mol of reaction, ΔH = −8.4×10² J ÷ 0.0100 mol = −84 kJ/mol.
```

## 6.7 Bond Enthalpies

**Know: core_idea**
```
- Breaking a bond requires energy; forming a bond releases it.
- Average bond energies of all reactant bonds broken and all product bonds formed give an estimate of ΔH, in kJ per mole of reaction as written.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Compare what the reaction pays with what it recovers:

| Energy released forming bonds vs energy required breaking bonds | Reaction |
| Released > required | Exothermic, ΔH negative |
| Released < required | Endothermic, ΔH positive |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. List bonds broken (reactants) and bonds formed (products), counting with coefficients.
2. Breaking costs energy, forming releases it: sum each list.
3. ΔH ≈ Σ(broken) − Σ(formed).
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Because breaking strong bonds releases extra energy.” It breaks step 2: breaking bonds costs energy; forming strong bonds is what releases it.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: (1) H₂ + Cl₂ → 2 HCl: broken H–H and Cl–Cl; formed 2 H–Cl. (2) Broken: 436 + 243 = 679 kJ required; formed: 2 × 431 = 862 kJ released (energies given). (3) ΔH ≈ 679 − 862 = −183 kJ per mole of reaction as written: released exceeds required, so exothermic.
```

## 6.8 Enthalpy of Formation

**Know: core_idea**
```
- ΔH°(reaction) = Σ n·ΔH°f(products) − Σ n·ΔH°f(reactants), with n the balanced coefficients.
- ΔH°f values are per mole of substance formed (kJ/mol).
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Each species enters the sum once per coefficient:

| Species | Contribution to ΔH° |
| Product, coefficient n | + n × ΔH°f |
| Reactant, coefficient n | − n × ΔH°f |
| Element in its standard state | 0 |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Set up Σ(products) − Σ(reactants).
2. Multiply each ΔH°f by its balanced coefficient.
3. Subtract the reactant sum from the product sum; check sign and kJ.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “They should not be multiplied because the table value already belongs to the reaction.” It breaks step 2: the table value is per mole, and the equation uses n moles.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: N₂(g) + 3 H₂(g) → 2 NH₃(g), with ΔH°f(NH₃) = −45.9 kJ/mol given. (1) ΔH° = [2·ΔH°f(NH₃)] − [ΔH°f(N₂) + 3·ΔH°f(H₂)]. (2) 2 × (−45.9) = −91.8 kJ; both elements contribute 0. (3) ΔH° = −91.8 − 0 = −91.8 kJ per mole of reaction as written.
```

## 6.9 Hess's Law

**Know: core_idea**
```
- The overall ΔH equals the sum of the steps' ΔH (energy is conserved).
- Reverse an equation: ΔH changes sign. Multiply it by c: ΔH is multiplied by c.
- Not tested: the concept of state functions.
```

**Know: mechanism (what_students_need_to_understand)**
```
Decide what to do to each given equation by comparing it with the target:

| Compared with the target | Usually |
| Species on the opposite side | Reverse (ΔH sign flips) |
| Coefficient differs | Multiply (ΔH × c) |
| Species not in the target | Make it cancel |
```

**Score: steps (how_this_becomes_points)**
```
1. Reverse or scale the given equations until they add to the target, with other species cancelling.
2. Change each ΔH exactly as its equation changed.
3. Add the adjusted ΔH values only after the equations sum to the target.
```

**Score: Weak (common_point_loss)**
```
Weak: “Because the calculator order might change the answer.” It skips step 1: the ΔH values add only after the equations sum to the target, and order of arithmetic is not the issue.
```

**Score: Full credit (answer_move)**
```
Full credit: target N₂(g) + 2 O₂(g) → 2 NO₂(g); given ½N₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → NO(g), ΔH = +90.3 kJ, and NO₂(g) → NO(g) + ½O₂(g), ΔH = +57.1 kJ. (1) Multiply the first by 2: N₂ + O₂ → 2 NO; reverse the second and multiply by 2: 2 NO + O₂ → 2 NO₂; they sum to the target (2 NO cancels). (2) ΔH = 2(+90.3) and 2(−57.1) = +180.6 kJ and −114.2 kJ. (3) Sum: +180.6 − 114.2 = +66.4 kJ.
```

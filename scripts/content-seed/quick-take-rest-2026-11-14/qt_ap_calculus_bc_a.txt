1.4|Read the table entries approaching from both sides of the target input, state the trend, then give the value the trend supports.|Choosing a single table entry instead of describing the trend across nearby entries on both sides.
1.5|Split the expression into components and confirm each component's limit exists (for a quotient, the denominator's limit must be nonzero). Then apply the matching property.|Applying a limit property, especially the quotient property, when its required condition, such as a nonzero denominator limit, is not met.

1.7|Read the expression's form first (continuous function, 0/0, piecewise, absolute value, one-sided marker) and name the matching strategy before computing.|Defaulting to a familiar technique without checking that the problem's form actually calls for it.
1.8|Write the bounding inequality, compute both outside limits, confirm they match, then conclude the middle function's limit equals that shared value.|Naming the theorem without showing the bounding relationship and the matching outside limits explicitly.
1.9|Convert the given representation (notation, graph, table, or words) into the statement 'as x approaches c, f(x) approaches L' before comparing it to anything.|Matching a table or graph to a function value instead of to approach behavior.
1.10|Check the left-hand limit, the right-hand limit, and f(c), in that order. Classify the discontinuity by which piece fails to match.|Calling every gap removable, or every discontinuity an asymptote, without checking behavior explicitly.
1.11|Check that f(c) exists, then that the limit exists, then that the limit equals f(c). Conclude continuity only after all three.|Calling a function continuous because the graph looks connected, without verifying all three conditions: f(c) exists, the limit exists, and they are equal.

1.14|Check each side of the input. If f(x) grows without bound, write the infinite limit (positive or negative) and name x = c as the vertical asymptote.|Writing only 'does not exist' when the problem asks for behavior or an asymptote connection.

2.1|Decide whether the question gives two specific inputs (average rate) or one exact moment or point (instantaneous rate), then choose the matching formula.|Using the average rate formula when the question asks for the rate at one instant.
2.2|Check the notation before using a value: f(a) is a value of the function itself, f'(a) is its rate of change at that point. They answer different questions.|Treating f'(a) as a y-value of the original function instead of as a rate or slope.
2.3|Find the two x-values closest to the target input, on either side if available. Compute the secant slope between exactly those two points.|Using distant data values, or failing to distinguish an estimate from an exact derivative.
2.4|Confirm continuity, then separately check for corners, cusps, or vertical tangents. A derivative exists at the point only if none of those are present.|Claiming a derivative exists at a corner or discontinuity because the graph has points on both sides.
2.5|Rewrite any radical or reciprocal expression as x to a power, then apply the power rule: multiply by the exponent, subtract one from the exponent.|Dropping coefficients or mishandling negative and fractional exponents.
2.6|Differentiate term by term, keeping every coefficient and sign visible. 2.8|Before simplifying, write the full structure as its own line. First factor times derivative of second, plus second factor times derivative of first.|Showing or computing only one of the two required product-rule terms, even when the omitted term would have been easy.
2.9|Write 'low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared' explicitly, keeping that exact subtraction order, before simplifying to a final value.|Reversing the numerator terms or forgetting to square the denominator.

3.1|Write down which piece is inner and which is outer first. For sin^2(x), inner = sin x, outer = squaring.

@@ 1.1 c
- You cannot count atoms directly, so n = m/M bridges a measurable mass to moles.
- Avogadro's number (6.022x10^23/mol) bridges moles to particle count.
- A substance's molar mass in grams numerically equals one particle's average mass in amu.
@@ 1.1 w
- Stoichiometry, solution concentration and gas laws all start by converting between mass and moles.
- A wrong molar mass or a mole/particle mix-up propagates into every later calculation, not just this topic's.
@@ 1.1 h
1. Apply n = m/M (or a rearrangement) with the molar mass of the substance actually asked about.
2. Decide whether Avogadro's number is needed to reach a particle count.
3. Do not stop at moles when particles were requested.
@@ 1.1 a
n = m/M = 3.42 g / 180.16 g/mol ~ 0.01898 mol.

Multiplying by Avogadro's number gives the particle count: 0.01898 mol x 6.022x10^23/mol ~ 1.14x10^22 glucose molecules.

Molar mass, not Avogadro's number, converts mass to moles; Avogadro's number only converts moles to particle count.
@@ 1.2 c
- A single-element mass spectrum has one peak per isotope: position is the isotope's mass, size is its natural relative abundance.
- Average atomic mass is the isotope-weighted average of those peaks.
- Excluded from the AP Exam: spectra of multiple elements, or peaks from anything other than singly charged monatomic ions.
@@ 1.2 w
- This is how average atomic mass is actually measured, not memorized.
- Isotopes of one element share a proton count and differ only in neutron count.
- The 2025 FRQ isotope-mass-difference point scored a mean of just 0.55/1.0, mostly from attributing the difference to electrons or protons instead of neutrons.
@@ 1.2 h
1. Read each peak's mass and relative abundance.
2. Multiply mass by fractional abundance and sum across every peak.
3. To explain a mass difference, name neutron count: proton count (and electron count in a neutral atom) is fixed.
@@ 1.2 a
| Isotope | Mass (amu) | Abundance | Product |
| Mg-24 | 23.985 | 0.7899 | 18.945 |
| Mg-25 | 24.986 | 0.1000 | 2.499 |
| Mg-26 | 25.983 | 0.1101 | 2.861 |

Sum ~ 24.31 amu, matching magnesium's periodic-table mass of 24.305.

Mg-24 and Mg-25 both have 12 protons (defining the element) and 12 electrons (neutral atom); the one-amu difference comes from Mg-25's one additional neutron.
@@ 1.3 c
- Law of definite proportions: the mass ratio of elements in any pure sample of a compound is fixed.
- The empirical formula is the lowest whole-number atom ratio matching that mass ratio.
- A molecular formula is an integer multiple of it: actual molar mass divided by the empirical formula's mass.
@@ 1.3 w
- Experimental mass data, like combustion analysis (masses of CO2 and H2O from burning a sample), leads to a compound's formula.
- It works only because every sample of a pure compound gives the same element ratio, however much you burn.
@@ 1.3 h
1. Convert given masses or mass percentages to moles of each element.
2. Divide every mole value by the smallest to get a whole-number ratio (the empirical formula).
3. Divide the molecular molar mass by the empirical formula's mass to find the scaling factor.
@@ 1.3 a
The mole ratio C:H:O = 3.33:6.66:3.33 reduces to 1:2:1, giving empirical formula CH2O with empirical mass 12.01+2(1.008)+16.00 ~ 30.03 g/mol.

The actual molar mass (180 g/mol) is about 180.16/30.03 ~ 6 times the empirical mass, so the molecular formula is (CH2O)x6 = C6H12O6, not CH2O.

The empirical formula alone under-answers a molecular-formula question.
@@ 1.4 c
- A pure substance has one particle type in a fixed proportion; a mixture has two or more whose proportions can vary sample to sample.
- A homogeneous mixture can look as uniform as a compound, so appearance never substitutes for checking whether the composition ratio stays constant across independent samples.
@@ 1.4 w
- Fixed versus variable ratio is what makes empirical-formula reasoning (Topic 1.3) valid at all.
- The same evidence, a constant or inconstant elemental composition ratio, is what Unit 3's mixture-separation topics build on.
@@ 1.4 h
1. Use elemental analysis data from two or more samples to test whether a component's mass ratio holds constant.
2. State a purity conclusion that follows from that comparison.
3. Do not rely on appearance, texture, or one sample's data.
@@ 1.4 a
| Sample | % H | % O |
| Water (H2O), fixed ratio | 11.19 | 88.81 |
| Sample A | 11.2 | 88.8 |
| Sample B | 9.5 | 90.5 |

Sample A matches that ratio, but Sample B does not: a pure substance's composition ratio cannot vary between samples.

Because the ratio changed between bottles, at least one sample is not the pure substance claimed; appearance (both being clear liquids) provides no evidence either way.
@@ 1.6 c
- Each PES peak's position is the energy needed to remove an electron from a specific subshell; its height is roughly proportional to that subshell's electron count.
- Photon energy follows E = hv and equals the electron's binding energy plus its ejected kinetic energy.
@@ 1.6 w
- PES reads the Topic 1.5 configuration rules directly off real data.
- Core electrons, closer to the nucleus and less shielded, always need more energy to remove than valence electrons, whichever subshell Aufbau filled last.
@@ 1.6 h
1. Match peak positions and relative heights to an element's electron configuration.
2. Explain binding-energy differences by distance from the nucleus and shielding.
3. Do not explain them by the order subshells were filled.
@@ 1.6 a
| Peak height | Subshell |
| 2 | 1s |
| 2 | 2s |
| 6 | 2p |
| 2 | 3s |

Heights 2:2:6:2 are electron counts: 2+2+6+2 = 12 total electrons, 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2, so magnesium (Z=12).

The highest-binding-energy peak (height 2) is 1s because 1s electrons sit closest to the nucleus and are least shielded, not because 1s was filled first or last. Binding-energy order and Aufbau filling order are different orderings.
@@ 1.7 c
- Periodic position reflects recurring electron-configuration patterns; ionization energy, radius, electron affinity and electronegativity trends follow from Coulomb's law, shielding and effective nuclear charge.
- Excluded from the AP Exam: aufbau-exception configurations (chromium, copper); the standard filling order applies wherever trends are tested.
@@ 1.7 w
- Trends let you predict or estimate a property for an unfamiliar element from its position alone.
- This reasoning is tested throughout the exam: the same Coulomb's-law logic reappears whenever a later unit compares or estimates a property.
@@ 1.7 h
1. Explain the trend with effective nuclear charge and distance from the nucleus or shielding.
2. Never just name the trend's direction.
3. Use that reasoning to make a specific comparison or estimate when exact data isn't given.
@@ 1.7 a
Cl and S are in the same period, so their valence electrons sit at a similar distance from the nucleus with similar shielding from the same core configuration.

Chlorine has one more proton (Z=17 vs. 16), so greater effective nuclear charge. By Coulomb's law, a larger nuclear charge over a similar radius gives a stronger attraction on the valence electrons, so removing one from chlorine requires more energy.

The direction of the trend follows from this charge difference, not from the trend's name.
@@ 1.8 c
- Bonding depends on how valence electrons interact with the nucleus; same-column elements form analogous compounds.
- Typical ionic charge follows from valence electron count and position, and total charge must balance to zero.
- NaCl predicts KCl (same column as Na), but not CaCl: Ca loses two electrons for 2+, so CaCl2 balances and CaCl does not.
@@ 1.8 w
- This sets up bonding and compound formation for Unit 2.
- From periodic position and the zero-total-charge requirement alone, you can predict a formula or analogous compound for an element you haven't seen.
@@ 1.8 h
1. Derive an ion's typical charge from its group number or valence-electron count.
2. Build a formula whose total positive and negative charge sums to zero.
3. Use same-column analogies, but never assume elements from different groups share a charge.
@@ 1.8 a
| Element | Group | Reaches noble-gas configuration by | Typical charge |
| Ca | 2 | losing 2 valence electrons | 2+ |
| Cl | 17 | gaining 1 electron | 1- |

Balancing total charge to zero requires two Cl- ions for every Ca2+ ion, giving CaCl2.

The same-column analogy to NaCl breaks down because calcium's group, and therefore its charge, is different from sodium's.

@@ 2.1 c
- Electrons are shared unequally, with greater inequality generally accompanying a larger electronegativity difference: bonding is one continuous scale from nonpolar to ionic.
- No cutoff separates the types; every polar bond has some ionic character.
- C-H, with a small real difference, is treated as effectively nonpolar.
@@ 2.1 w
- Metal/nonmetal is only a shortcut: use observed properties when they are given.
- Electronegativity predicts bond polarity, but molecular polarity (2.7) also needs geometry.
- For ionic attractions (2.2, 2.3) use the ions' actual charges and separation; metals differ because valence electrons are delocalized (2.4).
@@ 2.1 h
1. Name the bond type.
2. Support it with an explicit electronegativity comparison: which atom pulls harder, which carries delta-negative.
3. Confirm with a property: conductivity when molten or dissolved, melting point, or brittleness versus malleability.
@@ 2.1 a
| Bond | Electronegativities | Difference | Type |
| C-H | 2.55, 2.20 | about 0.35 | effectively nonpolar covalent |
| O-H | 3.44, 2.20 | about 1.24 | polar covalent, delta-negative on O |
| Na-Cl | 0.93, 3.16 | about 2.23 | ionic |

Na-Cl has the largest gap of the three, so it is classified ionic. Because ionic and covalent form a continuum rather than two boxes, even this bond retains some covalent character.
@@ 2.2 c
- On a potential energy vs internuclear distance graph, the minimum marks the equilibrium bond length; the well depth below the separated-atom baseline (zero) is the bond energy.
- Higher bond order gives a shorter bond and a larger bond energy together.
- For ion pairs, Coulomb's law ties strength to charge size and distance.
@@ 2.2 w
- This turns a bonding label into numbers you can rank and defend.
- It returns in lattice-energy comparisons, bond-length and bond-energy rankings in 2.7, and later bond-energy arguments in thermodynamics.
- It is one of the few Unit 2 ideas tested as graph reading.
@@ 2.2 h
1. Read bond length from the minimum's x-value and bond energy from the well depth.
2. Rank length and energy together by bond order (shorter goes with larger energy).
3. For ions, cite both charge magnitude and internuclear distance, never one alone.
@@ 2.2 a
| Bond | Length (pm) | Energy (kJ/mol) |
| C-C single | 154 | 347 |
| C=C double | 134 | 614 |
| C-C triple | 120 | 839 |

Each additional bond adds more shared electron density directly between the two nuclei, pulling them closer and deepening the well.

On the PE curve, higher bond order shifts the minimum to a shorter distance and makes it lower (more negative relative to the separated-atom baseline). Shorter length and larger energy rise together as one consequence of bond order, not two independent facts.
@@ 2.3 c
- Cations and anions form one extended, repeating three-dimensional array that maximizes attraction and minimizes repulsion across the whole lattice.
- Properties follow from that alternating array, not from named crystal types, which the exam does not assess.
@@ 2.3 w
- Hardness, brittleness, high melting point, and conduction only when molten or dissolved all trace back to this one picture.
- It links the Coulomb's-law reasoning of 2.2 to bulk properties in Unit 3, though its own exam weight is narrower than Lewis diagrams and VSEPR.
@@ 2.3 h
1. State that the solid is one extended, repeating array of alternating charges.
2. Link a named property to it. High melting point: many strong attractions throughout need a lot of energy to overcome. Brittleness: a shifted layer lines up like charges and repulsion cracks the crystal.
@@ 2.3 a
Student B's explanation earns the point.

The AP Exam explicitly excludes knowledge of specific crystal structures: naming 'rock-salt' or 'face-centered cubic' is neither required nor sufficient.

Student B's reasoning is what the exam rewards: displacing one layer brings like-charged ions into alignment, the resulting Coulombic repulsion overcomes the lattice's attractive forces along that plane, and the crystal fractures rather than deforming the way a metal would.
@@ 2.4 c
- A metal is a lattice of positive ions in a delocalized sea of valence electrons.
- Interstitial alloys (steel, small C atoms in iron's gaps) block sliding: harder and less malleable, yet still conducting.
- Substitutional alloys swap in atoms of similar size.
@@ 2.4 w
- Delocalization from 2.1 now explains observable behavior: conductivity, malleability, luster.
- Atomic radius from Unit 1 tells the two alloy types apart; the size argument is a clean, frequently testable call.
@@ 2.4 h
1. Cite mobile delocalized electrons for conductivity.
2. Explain malleability: layers slide while the electron sea holds them together.
3. Classify an alloy by comparing the two atoms' radii: very different is interstitial, comparable is substitutional.
@@ 2.4 a
| Alloy | Radius comparison | Type | Second atom |
| Steel | carbon much smaller than iron | interstitial | in gaps between iron atoms |
| Brass | zinc comparable to copper | substitutional | replaces copper at lattice positions |

The types differ because classification rests on the radius comparison, not on both being metal mixtures.

Steel still conducts, since interstitial atoms block layer sliding but mobile delocalized electrons remain, although alloying can change its electrical resistance.
@@ 2.5 c
- A Lewis diagram is valence-electron bookkeeping done by a fixed procedure.
- Total the valence electrons: add one per unit of negative charge, subtract one per unit of positive.
- Place bonds and lone pairs to satisfy octets, converting a lone pair into an extra bond when a central atom is short.
@@ 2.5 w
- The usual failure is the count, not the drawing: a missed ion charge or an undrawn lone pair changes the total.
- Every answer built on the diagram (resonance, formal charge, shape) inherits the error, so check the count against your picture first.
@@ 2.5 h
1. The electron total matches the formula (charge-adjusted for ions).
2. Every bonding pair and lone pair is drawn, and octets are met where required.
3. An ion is bracketed with its charge outside.
@@ 2.5 a
| Source | Electrons |
| Carbon | 4 |
| Three oxygens, 6 each | 18 |
| 2- charge | 2 |
| Total | 24 |

With carbon central, one C=O and two C-O bonds use 8 electrons, leaving 16 as lone pairs: the double-bonded oxygen has 2 pairs and each single-bonded oxygen has 3. Every atom has an octet.

Enclose the diagram in brackets with 2- outside.
@@ 2.6 c
- Formal charge = valence electrons minus lone-pair electrons minus half the bonding electrons.
- With octet requirements, it selects among nonequivalent Lewis structures; equivalent structures are averaged as resonance.
- The Lewis model cannot cleanly represent odd-electron species.
@@ 2.6 w
- Resonance forms are alternative drawings, not successive states: never say a double bond moves back and forth.
- That is why both N-O bonds in nitrite are identical.
- Formal charge picks which of several legal diagrams to reason from.
@@ 2.6 h
1. Equivalent: draw all forms, reject switching, and state the real structure is one average (nitrite's N-O bond order is (1 + 2)/2 = 1.5, so both bonds are equal).
2. Nonequivalent: check octets, then compute formal charge.
3. Minimize formal charge, with negative on the more electronegative atom.
@@ 2.6 a
The two resonance structures are equivalent, so the real ion is a single averaged structure, not one that switches between two forms. Each N-O bond has an intermediate bond order of 1.5 (halfway between a single and a double bond).

| N-O bond | Length (pm) |
| Typical N=O double | ~115 |
| Nitrite, both bonds | about 124 |
| Typical N-O single | ~140 |

Both nitrite bonds measure the same length, confirming the averaged picture rather than two different bond lengths.
@@ 2.7 c
- VSEPR counts regions of electron density (each lone pair or bond, however many lines, is one) to predict geometry, ideal angles and hybridization.
- Ideal angles: sp 180, sp2 120, sp3 109.5 degrees; actual bond angles can differ.
- Off the exam: deriving hybrid orbitals, d-orbital hybridization (over four regions: shape only), molecular orbital theory.
@@ 2.7 w
- Geometry is the payoff of the unit and the gateway to Unit 3: polarity, solubility and boiling point depend on shape plus bond dipoles.
- Hybridization identification scored a mean of only 0.56 of 1.0 on the 2025 free-response item, mainly from counting C=O as two regions.
@@ 2.7 h
1. Count regions (a lone pair is one, a double or triple bond is one), name the geometry from atom positions only, and give the matching angle.
2. Assign sp, sp2 or sp3 from the same count only if asked.
3. For polarity, combine bond dipoles with the geometry to test whether they cancel.
@@ 2.7 a
Carbon has three regions: two C-H bonds and one C=O double bond, which counts as one.

Three regions means trigonal planar geometry, about 120 degrees, and sp2 hybridization, not sp3.

The costly error on the 2025 free-response item (mean 0.56 of 1.0) was counting C=O as two domains, giving four domains and sp3.

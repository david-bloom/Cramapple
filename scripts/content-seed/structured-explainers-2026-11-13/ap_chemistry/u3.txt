@@ 3.2 c

- Covalent network solids are usually rigid and high-melting; graphite is the exception: covalent within layers, but soft because weak London dispersion forces between layers let them slide.

@@ 3.2 a

@@ 3.3 c

- Not assessed: reading or interpreting phase diagrams.
@@ 3.3 w

@@ 3.3 a

@@ 3.4 c

- R's units (0.08206 L*atm/(mol*K)) fix P in atm, V in L, T in Kelvin; Celsius or torr silently break the calculation.
@@ 3.4 w
- This is the algebra and unit-consistency workhorse of Unit 3.
- It connects to 3.7's molarity (same solution-total-volume discipline) and 3.5's KMT (which explains why T must be absolute).
@@ 3.4 h

@@ 3.4 a

@@ 3.5 c
- KE = 1/2 m v^2; Kelvin temperature is directly proportional to average kinetic energy of all particles, not average speed.
- At the same temperature, gases of different molar mass share average KE, so the lighter gas moves faster.
- The Maxwell-Boltzmann speed-vs-fraction-of-particles curve broadens and shifts to higher speeds as temperature rises.
@@ 3.5 w
- Same average KE with different average speed is the single most commonly missed idea in this topic.
- It directly explains effusion/diffusion rate differences tested elsewhere.
@@ 3.5 h
1. Connect Kelvin temperature specifically to average kinetic energy, never average speed.
2. Use the mass-speed tradeoff to compare distributions of different gases at the same temperature.
@@ 3.5 a
| | Helium | Xenon |
| Molar mass (g/mol) | 4.00 | 131.3 |
| Average KE at 300 K | same | same |
| Average speed | much faster | much slower |

Average KE depends only on temperature, not on identity or mass. Because KE = 1/2 m v^2, helium's much smaller mass means its particles must move much faster on average to reach that same KE.

On a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution, helium's curve is shifted to substantially higher speeds and is broader than xenon's, though both are at 300 K.
@@ 3.6 c
- Real gases deviate from ideal for two distinct reasons under different conditions.
- Low temperature (near condensation): attractions pull slow particles together, so pressure measures lower than the ideal law predicts.
- Very high pressure: particle volume becomes a significant fraction of the container, leaving less free space than the ideal law assumes.
@@ 3.6 w
- This follows from 3.1's IMF strength and 3.5's particle motion/KMT.
- Name which mechanism (attraction or particle volume) applies under which condition, not one vague "high pressure, low temperature" trigger.
- Both effects mean deviation is largest, not smallest, at low temperature and high pressure.
@@ 3.6 h
1. Identify which mechanism (attraction versus particle volume) applies to the given condition.
2. State that these conditions cause the largest deviation from ideal, not the smallest.
@@ 3.6 a
These are the conditions where real gases deviate most from ideal, not least.

| Condition | Mechanism | Effect |
| Low temperature, near condensation | attractions between slow particles reduce wall-collision force | measured pressure lower than PV=nRT predicts |
| Very high pressure | particles' own finite volume (assumed negligible) is a real fraction of the total | less free space than the ideal model assumes |

One mechanism comes from attraction, one from particle volume; both make a gas behave less ideally.
@@ 3.7 c
- A solution is homogeneous: concentration, color and density are uniform wherever you sample; a heterogeneous mixture varies by location.
- Solutions need not be liquids: solid solutions (some alloys) and gas solutions (air) qualify if homogeneous.
- Molarity is moles of solute per liter of final solution (M = n_solute / L_solution), not of solvent added.
@@ 3.7 w
- Molarity's solution-volume (not solvent-volume) discipline parallels the unit-consistency care of 3.4's gas law.
- Homogeneous/heterogeneous classification underlies 3.9's separation-method reasoning.
@@ 3.7 h
1. Classify a mixture as homogeneous or heterogeneous, with justification.
2. Compute molarity using the total solution volume.
3. Do not use the volume of solvent added.
@@ 3.7 a
Correctly made solution: M = n_solute / L_solution = 0.250 mol / 0.500 L = 0.500 M.

If the colleague measured 500. mL of water and then dissolved NaCl in it, the final volume would be slightly more than 500 mL (the dissolving solid adds volume), so the true molarity is slightly lower than 0.500 M.

Always use the volume of the final solution after mixing, not the solvent volume measured beforehand; treating them as interchangeable introduces a real, if small, error.

@@ 3.9 c

@@ 3.10 c

- Similar IMFs let solvent particles surround and stabilize solute particles as effectively as the solute's own particles did; mismatched IMFs do not.
- This is 3.1's IMF vocabulary applied to miscibility instead of boiling point.
@@ 3.10 w
- This topic is graded on naming the actual force match or mismatch.
- Restating the mnemonic without the underlying forces earns nothing.
@@ 3.10 h

2. State whether they match, and predict solubility/miscibility from that.
3. Do not cite "like dissolves like" alone.
@@ 3.10 a

| Solvent | Dominant IMF | Match with I2 | Result |

@@ 3.11 c

@@ 3.11 w

- It connects to 3.13's Beer-Lambert law, typically applied in the UV/visible region for concentration measurements.
@@ 3.11 h

2. Do not over-interpret a non-UV/visible spectrum as electronic-structure evidence.
@@ 3.11 a
The classmate's claim is incorrect.

Infrared radiation carries enough energy to excite molecular vibrational transitions, here the stretching of the C=O bond, but not enough to promote an electron between electronic energy levels; that requires the higher-energy UV/visible region.

The IR band is evidence of bond vibration (a carbonyl group), not of the compound's electronic structure. An electronic-transition claim would need a UV/visible absorption spectrum.
@@ 3.12 c
- A photon is a packet of electromagnetic energy; its wavelength and frequency determine the exact energy transferred when light is absorbed or emitted.
- c = lambda nu: shorter wavelength means higher frequency.
- E = h nu: higher frequency means greater photon energy.
@@ 3.12 w
- Keep the chain straight: wavelength and frequency are inversely related, but energy and frequency are directly related.
- Absorption raises the species to a higher-energy state by the photon energy; emission lowers it by the emitted photon energy.
@@ 3.12 h
1. Convert wavelength to meters if needed.
2. Solve for frequency with c = lambda nu, then use E = h nu.
3. Connect the number to an energy change in the species, not just a calculator result.
@@ 3.12 a

| Photon | Wavelength vs. 500 nm | Frequency | Energy |
| 400 nm | shorter | higher | more |
| 700 nm | longer | lower | less |

Frequency is c divided by wavelength, and photon energy is E = h nu. The answer should compare frequency and energy, not just the size of the wavelength number.
@@ 3.13 c
- Beer-Lambert turns spectrophotometry into a concentration measurement.
- Absorbance equals molar absorptivity times path length times concentration: A = epsilon b c.
- With wavelength and path length fixed, epsilon and b are fixed, so absorbance changes linearly with concentration.
@@ 3.13 w
- Wavelength is normally chosen near maximum absorbance, so small concentration differences give clearer absorbance differences.
- Cuvette path length must stay the same if absorbances are compared by proportional reasoning.
@@ 3.13 h
1. Name the variables in A = epsilon b c.
2. Use direct proportionality only when epsilon and b are constant.
3. Interpret larger absorbance as more absorbing species along the light path, not a different photon energy claim.
@@ 3.13 a
Same compound, same wavelength, and same 1.00 cm path length, so epsilon and b are constant. Beer-Lambert reduces to A proportional to concentration.

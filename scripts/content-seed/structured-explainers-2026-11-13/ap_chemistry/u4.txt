@@ 4.1 c
- A reaction claim is really a composition claim.
- Chemical changes form new substances.
- Physical changes alter state, form, or mixture without changing identity.
@@ 4.1 w
- Gas, precipitate, heat, light, or color change can support a reaction claim.
- The strongest answer connects the observation to particles being rearranged into different substances.
@@ 4.1 h
1. Identify whether composition changes.
2. Name evidence for a reaction.
3. Describe what happened to the substances, not just the visible cue.
@@ 4.1 a
The formation of a solid precipitate suggests that ions in solution rearranged to form a new insoluble substance.

The observation matters because it points to a composition change, not merely because the mixture looks different.
@@ 4.2 c
- Net ionic equations show only the species that actually change during an aqueous process.
- They still conserve atoms and total charge.
@@ 4.2 w
- Move through the equation forms deliberately.
- The molecular equation shows formulas as written; the complete ionic equation splits strong aqueous electrolytes into ions.
- The net ionic equation removes spectator ions that are identical on both sides.
@@ 4.2 h
1. Dissociate correctly and give correct phases.
2. Cancel true spectators only.
3. Check both atom balance and charge balance in the final net ionic equation.
@@ 4.2 a
Cancel Na+ if it has the same charge and aqueous phase on both sides.

It is a spectator ion, so the net ionic equation should focus on the species that react or change phase.
@@ 4.3 c
- A balanced equation and a particulate diagram must tell the same conservation story.
- The same kinds and numbers of atoms are rearranged into products.
@@ 4.3 w
- Translate coefficients into counts or ratios of particles.
- Spectator ions may be shown in a complete particulate representation but omitted from a net ionic one.
- The products drawn must match formulas and charges from the balanced equation.
@@ 4.3 h
1. Match coefficients and conserve atoms and charge.
2. Choose the correct representation type.
3. Show particles in the proper combined or separated form.
@@ 4.3 a
The diagram must preserve the particle ratio represented by the coefficients.

If two formula units or sets of ions are needed for every one product unit, the particulate model should show that ratio or a consistent multiple of it.
@@ 4.4 c
- Physical versus chemical change depends on what changes at the particle level.
- Intermolecular attractions usually shift in physical changes; chemical bonds usually change in chemical changes.
@@ 4.4 w
- Avoid one-word rules.
- Melting separates particles by weakening intermolecular attractions, so composition stays the same; a reaction that forms new covalent or ionic substances changes composition.
- Dissolving an ionic solid can need a more careful argument because ionic attractions and ion-dipole interactions are involved.
@@ 4.4 h
1. Name the interaction being broken or formed.
2. Tie that interaction to composition change or no composition change.
@@ 4.4 a
It is a physical change.

The water molecules remain H2O; the main change is in intermolecular attractions and particle arrangement, not in the composition of the molecules.
@@ 4.5 c
- Stoichiometry is conservation of atoms turned into calculation.
- Balanced coefficients are mole ratios that connect measured amounts of reactants and products.
@@ 4.5 w
- Mass, volume of gas, or solution concentration data must first become moles.
- The balanced equation changes one substance amount into another.
- The final answer must return to the requested unit, often mass or concentration.
@@ 4.5 h
1. Convert to moles.
2. Use the correct coefficient ratio, identifying limiting information when needed.
3. Convert from moles to the requested unit instead of stopping early.
@@ 4.5 a
No. A mass comparison must include molar mass.

The mole ratio says 3 mol Zn per 2 mol Al, but the masses require multiplying each mole amount by its molar mass before comparing.
@@ 4.6 c
- A titration uses a measured volume of known titrant.
- It finds analyte amount through a specific balanced reaction.
@@ 4.6 w
- Distinguish chemical completion from the observed signal.
- At the equivalence point, the analyte has reacted according to the stoichiometric ratio.
- The endpoint is the indicator or instrument change used to detect that point, and it may only approximate equivalence.
@@ 4.6 h
1. Calculate titrant moles.
2. Apply the balanced mole ratio to get analyte moles, then divide by analyte volume for concentration.
3. Use endpoint/equivalence language precisely.
@@ 4.6 a
| Term | Meaning |
| Equivalence point | the stoichiometric point where the analyte has been consumed by the titrant |
| Endpoint | the observed color change used to signal that point, chosen to be close to equivalence |
@@ 4.7 c
- Reaction types are recognized by particle-level changes.
- Acid-base: proton transfer. Redox: electron transfer.
- Precipitation: insoluble product formation. Complete hydrocarbon combustion: CO2/H2O formation.
@@ 4.7 w
- Support the classification with evidence.
- Redox requires oxidation-number change; acid-base requires proton transfer.
- Precipitation requires an insoluble or sparingly soluble ionic product, with AP Chemistry limiting solubility-rule memorization to the stated soluble ions.
@@ 4.7 h
1. Identify the reaction signature.
2. Use allowed solubility information.
3. Do not rely on labels without evidence from formulas or oxidation numbers.
@@ 4.7 a
A solid product from mixing aqueous ions supports a precipitation reaction.

To call it redox, you would need evidence of oxidation-number changes; solid formation alone is not electron transfer.
@@ 4.8 c
- Bronsted-Lowry acid-base reactions are proton-transfer reactions.
- Acids donate H+ and bases accept H+.
@@ 4.8 w
- Track one proton through the equation.
- After an acid donates H+, it becomes its conjugate base; after a base accepts H+, it becomes its conjugate acid.
- Water can act as either donor or acceptor in aqueous reactions; Lewis acid-base ideas are outside this AP topic.
@@ 4.8 h
1. Identify donor and acceptor.
2. Pair conjugates correctly.
3. Explain relative strength from ionization behavior when asked.
@@ 4.8 a
NH3 is the base because it accepts H+ from water to become NH4+.

Water donates H+ and therefore acts as the acid in this direction.
@@ 4.9 c
- Redox reactions are electron-transfer reactions.
- Half-reactions make the electron accounting explicit.
@@ 4.9 w
- Connect oxidation numbers to half-reaction direction.
- Oxidation is loss of electrons, so electrons appear as products in the oxidation half-reaction; reduction is gain, so electrons appear as reactants in the reduction half-reaction.
- Balanced redox equations conserve atoms and charge.
@@ 4.9 h
1. Assign oxidation states and identify what is oxidized or reduced.
2. Place electrons on the correct side.
3. Scale half-reactions so electrons cancel.
@@ 4.9 a
Electrons are products: Al(s) -> Al3+(aq) + 3e-.

Aluminum loses electrons, so this is oxidation and electron loss must appear on the product side.

@@ 5.1 c
- Reaction rate is how quickly concentrations change as reactants become products.
- The balanced equation controls how species rates compare.
@@ 5.1 w
- Connect measured slopes to stoichiometry: if coefficients differ, one species may disappear twice as fast as another appears.
- Rate can also change when concentration, temperature, surface area, catalyst, or environment changes the number of successful collisions.
@@ 5.1 h
1. Read concentration-time data.
2. Use coefficient ratios to compare species rates.
3. Explain factor changes through particle behavior.
@@ 5.1 a
B increases at 0.10 M/s.

The balanced equation says 2 mol A are consumed for every 1 mol B formed, so the disappearance rate of A is twice the appearance rate of B.
@@ 5.2 c
- A rate law is an experimental relationship between rate and reactant concentrations.
- Orders are found from data.
@@ 5.2 w
- Use initial-rate comparisons: with one concentration doubled and others constant, a doubled rate means order one and a quadrupled rate means order two.
- Overall order is the sum of individual orders, and rate constant units depend on that overall order.
@@ 5.2 h
1. Isolate one variable between trials.
2. Solve for reactant orders and write the rate law.
3. Determine k with correct units when asked.
@@ 5.2 a
Second order.

Doubling [A] causes the rate to increase by a factor of four, so rate is proportional to [A]^2 for that reactant.
@@ 5.3 c
- Integrated rate laws identify reaction order.
- The order is the one whose concentration transformation produces a straight line over time.
@@ 5.3 w
- Match graph, equation, and slope. Zero order: linear [A] vs t, slope -k.
- First order: linear ln[A] vs t, slope -k, constant half-life.
- Second order: linear 1/[A] vs t, slope +k.
@@ 5.3 h
1. Choose the linear plot.
2. Extract k from the correct slope and use the matching integrated rate law.
3. Apply first-order half-life only when appropriate.
@@ 5.3 a
It is first order.

A linear ln[A] versus time plot matches ln[A]_t - ln[A]_0 = -kt, and the slope is -k.
@@ 5.4 c
- An elementary reaction is one molecular event.
- Its reactants directly determine the rate law for that step.
@@ 5.4 w
- Recognize the boundary of the shortcut.
- For an elementary step A + B -> products, rate = k[A][B]; for an overall reaction made of multiple steps, the overall coefficients do not automatically give orders.
- Collisions involving three or more particles at once are rare.
@@ 5.4 h
1. Identify molecularity.
2. Write the rate law for a named elementary step.
3. Avoid the elementary shortcut for overall equations.
@@ 5.4 a
The reactants NO2 and CO appear: rate = k[NO2][CO].

For an elementary step, the rate law follows the colliding reactant particles in that single step.
@@ 5.5 c
- A successful collision requires enough energy to overcome activation energy.
- It also requires a productive particle orientation.
@@ 5.5 w
- Explain rate changes through successful collisions.
- Raising temperature increases particle energy and the fraction above activation energy; increasing concentration or surface area can increase collision frequency.
- Orientation matters because not every collision forms products.
@@ 5.5 h
1. Name the factor changed.
2. Connect it to collision frequency, energy distribution, or orientation.
3. Explain why successful collision count changes.
@@ 5.5 a
Higher temperature shifts the energy distribution so a larger fraction of particles have energy at or above activation energy.

Collision frequency may also rise, but the key AP explanation is more energetically successful collisions.
@@ 5.6 c
- A reaction energy profile shows the energy barrier between reactants and the transition state.
- It also shows the energy difference between reactants and products.
@@ 5.6 w
- Separate kinetics from thermodynamics: activation energy affects rate.
- Overall energy change indicates endothermic or exothermic character.
- The Arrhenius equation supports qualitative temperature and activation-energy reasoning, but AP Chemistry does not assess Arrhenius calculations.
@@ 5.6 h
1. Label reactants, products, and transition state.
2. Label forward and reverse activation energy.
3. Label overall energy change correctly.
@@ 5.6 a
The overall energy change is negative, so the reaction is exothermic.

Activation energy is measured from reactants up to the transition-state peak and remains a barrier.
@@ 5.7 c
- A mechanism is a set of elementary steps that add to the overall reaction.
- Intermediates and catalysts cancel from the net equation for different reasons.
@@ 5.7 w
- Inspect individual steps.
- An intermediate is produced in one step and consumed in a later step; a catalyst is consumed first and regenerated later.
- The summed mechanism must match the overall balanced equation.
@@ 5.7 h
1. Add the steps and cancel correctly.
2. Classify species from the order in which they appear.
3. Check the overall reaction.
@@ 5.7 a
It is an intermediate.

| Species type | Pattern across steps |
| Intermediate | made during the mechanism, used up later |
| Catalyst | consumed in an earlier step, regenerated later |
@@ 5.8 c
- The rate-determining elementary step controls the rate law.
- This holds when it is the slow step that limits the mechanism.
@@ 5.8 w
- Write the slow-step rate law from the reactants in that elementary step.
- If the slow step contains only species from the overall reaction, the rate law is direct.
- If it contains an intermediate, another mechanism relationship is needed before the final rate law is acceptable.
@@ 5.8 h
1. Identify the slow step.
2. Use molecularity for that step.
3. Avoid overall-equation coefficients as rate-law powers.
@@ 5.8 a
Because the slow step is elementary, its reactants determine the rate law: rate = k[A][B].

The overall equation is not the source of the powers.
@@ 5.9 c
- Pre-equilibrium lets a fast reversible step relate an intermediate concentration to reactant concentrations.
- This happens before the slow step occurs.
@@ 5.9 w
- See why substitution is needed: the slow-step rate law may contain an intermediate.
- Since intermediates should not usually appear in the experimentally observed rate law, the earlier fast equilibrium expression is used to rewrite that intermediate.
@@ 5.9 h
1. Write the slow-step rate law.
2. Write the pre-equilibrium expression and solve for the intermediate.
3. Substitute without changing the mechanism logic.
@@ 5.9 a
A final rate law should be expressed in terms of reactants that can be controlled or measured.

The fast pre-equilibrium step can express [I] in terms of earlier reactants, allowing substitution into the slow-step rate law.
@@ 5.10 c
- A multistep energy profile represents a mechanism as multiple barriers and wells.
- Peaks are transition states; wells between peaks are intermediates.
@@ 5.10 w
- Connect graph features to mechanism steps.
- Each peak corresponds to an elementary-step transition state; each valley between reactants and products corresponds to an intermediate.
- The largest activation barrier usually marks the slowest step.
@@ 5.10 h
1. Count steps.
2. Identify intermediates and compare activation energies.
3. Connect the rate-determining step to the highest barrier, not total energy change.
@@ 5.10 a
The middle valley represents an intermediate.

It is a species formed after the first elementary step and consumed before the final products are reached.
@@ 5.11 c
- A catalyst changes the reaction pathway and is regenerated overall.
- So mechanism-step evidence is required to identify it.
@@ 5.11 w
- Prove catalyst status from the mechanism, not just from a definition.
- A catalyst is consumed in one step and regenerated in a later step, with no net consumption in the overall equation; an intermediate is produced first and consumed later.
- Catalysts can lower activation energy or improve effective collision pathways.
@@ 5.11 h
1. Cite the exact steps where the catalyst is consumed and regenerated.
2. Distinguish it from intermediates.
3. Connect the alternate pathway to increased rate.
@@ 5.11 a
X is a catalyst because the mechanism shows it being consumed in step 1 and regenerated in step 2, so it is not consumed overall.

The step evidence is the required justification.

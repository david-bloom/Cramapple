### Unit 5 — Kinetics (7-9%)

- **5.1 Reaction Rates.** EK: reaction rate = rate of reactant-to-product
  conversion per unit time; reactant/product concentration-change rates are
  fixed by balanced-equation stoichiometry; rate is influenced by
  concentration, temperature, surface area, catalysts, and other
  environmental factors. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **5.2 Introduction to Rate Law.** EK: rate is proportional to each
  reactant's concentration raised to a power (its order); the sum of
  reactant orders is the overall reaction order; the rate constant's units
  reflect the overall order and the constant is temperature-dependent;
  comparing initial rates determines individual reactant orders. Zero
  explicit exclusion statements.
- **5.3 Concentration Changes over Time.** EK: reaction order can be
  inferred from a concentration-vs-time graph shape. Integrated rate laws:
  **EQN (zeroth order):** $[A]_t-[A]_0=-kt$; **EQN (first order):**
  $\ln[A]_t-\ln[A]_0=-kt$ (linear ln[A] vs. t); **EQN (second order):**
  $1/[A]_t-1/[A]_0=kt$ (linear 1/[A] vs. t). Half-life (first order only,
  constant): **EQN:** $t_{1/2}=0.693/k$. Radioactive decay is an important
  first-order-kinetics illustration. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **5.4 Elementary Reactions.** EK: an elementary reaction's rate law can be
  inferred directly from collision stoichiometry; simultaneous three(+)-
  particle collisions are rare. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **5.5 Collision Model.** EK: successful elementary-reaction collisions
  need both sufficient energy (overcoming activation energy) and correct
  orientation; the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution gives a qualitative
  estimate of the energetically-sufficient collision fraction and its
  temperature dependence. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **5.6 Reaction Energy Profile.** EK: the reaction coordinate plots energy
  from reactants through a transition state to products; the
  reactant/transition-state energy difference is the forward activation
  energy; the Arrhenius equation relates rate-temperature dependence to
  activation energy. **Exclusion Statement (verbatim):** "Calculations
  involving the Arrhenius equation will not be assessed on the AP Exam."
  (Source of the first clause of existing flat-list item 8.)
- **5.7 Introduction to Reaction Mechanisms.** EK: a mechanism = sequence of
  elementary steps (reactants, intermediates, products, catalysts) that sum
  to the overall balanced equation; an intermediate is produced by some
  steps and consumed by others, present only transiently. **Exclusion
  Statement (verbatim):** "Collection of data pertaining to detection of a
  reaction intermediate will not be assessed on the AP Exam." (Source of the
  second clause of existing flat-list item 8; this identical exclusion
  sentence is restated verbatim again under 5.8 in the CED.)
- **5.8 Reaction Mechanism and Rate Law.** EK: for mechanisms where every
  step is irreversible, or where the first step is rate-limiting, the
  overall rate law is set by the slowest (rate-limiting) elementary step's
  molecularity. **Exclusion Statement:** identical restatement of the 5.7
  reaction-intermediate-detection exclusion (see above).
- **5.9 Pre-Equilibrium Approximation.** EK: when the first elementary step
  is *not* rate-limiting, approximations such as pre-equilibrium are needed
  to derive the rate law. Zero explicit exclusion statements. (Confirms the
  existing fact pack's correction: this is "Pre-Equilibrium Approximation,"
  not an older "Steady-State Approximation" label — verified directly
  against the CED page.)
- **5.10 Multistep Reaction Energy Profile.** EK: elementary-step energetics
  combine into a full multistep-reaction energy profile. Zero explicit
  exclusion statements.
- **5.11 Catalysis.** EK: a catalyst increases effective-collision frequency
  and/or lowers activation energy via an alternate pathway; catalyst net
  concentration is constant overall, though it may be consumed in the
  rate-determining step and regenerated later. Enzyme catalysis
  (reactant-binding), acid-base catalysis (covalent, proton gain/loss), and
  surface catalysis (covalent/binding to a surface) are named catalytic
  mechanisms, each introducing new intermediates/elementary reactions. Zero
  explicit exclusion statements.

**Documented misconception (2025 FRQ Q7 Part C, Topic 5.11, catalyst
identification in a mechanism):** mean score 0.51/1.0. The dominant error
was definitional rather than mechanistic — stating a generic catalyst
property ("increases rate," "lowers activation energy") without citing the
specific mechanistic evidence required (consumed as a reactant in step 1,
regenerated as a product in step 2, absent from the overall equation); a
secondary error confused the catalyst with a reaction intermediate. Chief
Reader note: catalyst/intermediate/rate-determining-step questions require
evaluating the *individual mechanism steps*, not the overall summed
equation — the overall equation is a validity check only, and its
absence-of-species is a common distractor students misread as "spectator
ion" reasoning.

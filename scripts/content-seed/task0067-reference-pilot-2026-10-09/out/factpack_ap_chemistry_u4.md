### Unit 4 — Chemical Reactions (7-9%)

- **4.1 Introduction for Reactions.** EK: physical change = property change
  without composition change (phase changes, mixture formation/separation);
  chemical change = transformation into new substance(s), typically with
  different composition — evidenced by heat/light, gas formation,
  precipitate formation, and/or color change. Zero explicit exclusion
  statements.
- **4.2 Net Ionic Equations.** EK: all physical/chemical processes can be
  represented symbolically by balanced equations; balancing enforces
  conservation of both atoms and charge. Molecular, complete ionic, and net
  ionic equations are differing symbolic forms chosen by context. Zero
  explicit exclusion statements.
- **4.3 Representations of Reactions.** EK: balanced equations in any form
  translate into symbolic particulate representations. Zero explicit
  exclusion statements.
- **4.4 Physical and Chemical Changes.** EK: bond-breaking/forming
  processes are typically chemical; IMF-only changes (e.g. phase changes)
  are typically physical — but some processes (e.g. salt dissolution) admit
  plausible arguments either way, since ionic-bond breaking and ion-dipole
  formation both occur. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **4.5 Stoichiometry.** EK: because atoms are conserved, product amounts
  can be calculated from known reactant amounts and vice versa, using
  balanced-equation coefficients as mole-ratio information; stoichiometry
  combines with the ideal gas law and molarity for gas/solution
  calculations. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **4.6 Introduction to Titration.** EK: the titrant (known concentration)
  reacts specifically/quantitatively with the analyte; the equivalence point
  is total analyte consumption; the endpoint is the observable property
  change (e.g. color) signaling it. Zero explicit exclusion statements.
- **4.7 Types of Chemical Reactions.** EK: acid-base reactions transfer one
  or more protons; redox reactions transfer one or more electrons (tracked
  via oxidation-number changes) — combustion is an important redox
  subclass, producing CO₂ + H₂O from complete hydrocarbon combustion;
  precipitation reactions mix aqueous ions to form an insoluble/sparingly
  soluble ionic compound (all Na⁺, K⁺, NH₄⁺, and NO₃⁻ salts are soluble).
  **Exclusion Statements (verbatim, two — the prior flat list merged both
  into one sentence with item 8.6's Lewis exclusion):** (1) "The meaning of
  the terms 'reducing agent' and 'oxidizing agent' will not be assessed on
  the AP Exam." (2) "Rote memorization of 'solubility rules' other than
  those implied in 4.7.A.5 will not be assessed on the AP Exam." (Source of
  the first two clauses of existing flat-list item 7.)
- **4.8 Introduction to Acid-Base Reactions.** EK: Brønsted-Lowry acid =
  proton donor, base = proton acceptor; water plays a dual donor/acceptor
  role in aqueous acid-base chemistry; conjugate acid-base pairs and
  relative strength can be identified from ionization. **Exclusion
  Statement (verbatim):** "Lewis acid-base concepts will not be assessed on
  the AP Exam. The emphasis in AP Chemistry is on reactions in aqueous
  solution." (Source of the third clause of existing flat-list item 7.)
- **4.9 Oxidation-Reduction (Redox) Reactions.** EK: balanced redox
  equations can be constructed from half-reactions. Zero explicit exclusion
  statements. (Note: CED's full topic title is "Oxidation-Reduction (Redox)
  Reactions" — the existing topic map's shorter "Oxidation-Reduction
  Reactions" is an acceptable alias, not an error.)

**Documented misconception (2025 FRQ Q6, Topics 4.5/4.9 — the single
lowest-scoring FRQ on the 2025 exam, mean 1.34/4):** writing a correctly-
balanced net ionic redox equation (Part B) was the most accessible point
(0.52/1.0); the two most challenging points on this question were
stoichiometric mass comparison across a redox reaction (Part C, 0.10/1.0 —
students routinely stopped at a mole-ratio comparison without converting to
mass via molar mass, e.g. concluding Zn's mass change is greater "because 3
mol Zn form per 2 mol Al" without multiplying through by each element's
molar mass) and constructing an oxidation half-reaction with electrons
correctly placed as a product (Part A errors included writing the
*reduction* direction for a species already identified as the anode, and
omitting electrons from the half-reaction entirely).
